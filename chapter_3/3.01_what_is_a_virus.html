3.1 What is a virus: a genome in a protein coat

In one sentence: a virus is not a cell but a tiny particle made of a piece of DNA or RNA inside a protein shell (sometimes with a membrane around it), and it can do nothing on its own; it only "comes alive" inside a host cell.

Chapter 3 is about human pathogens, the microbes that cause disease, but the syllabus says it will address only viruses: their structure (this page) and their replication in host cells (pages 3.2 and 3.3). Two of the fifteen example exam questions are about viruses, both about retroviruses and reverse transcriptase (page 3.3). Those questions make no sense without the vocabulary of this page: genome, capsid, envelope, DNA virus, RNA virus.

1. Why pathogens matter (3.1)

A pathogen (ziekteverwekker) is a microbe capable of causing an infectious disease (a disease you can catch from a pathogen, besmettelijke ziekte). The syllabus gives some numbers and examples:

  • Infectious diseases cause about one quarter of all human deaths worldwide: more than all forms of cancer combined, second only to cardiovascular (heart and blood vessel) diseases.
  • Old diseases such as tuberculosis and malaria stay a heavy burden and are getting harder to treat because of rising drug resistance (the microbes no longer respond to the medicines).
  • New diseases keep emerging: COVID-19 (coronavirus disease 2019), first seen in 2019, infected hundreds of millions and killed millions in a pandemic (a worldwide epidemic).
  • Some diseases long blamed on other causes turn out to be infections: most stomach ulcers are caused not by stress or spicy food but by the bacterium Helicobacter pylori infecting the stomach lining.
  • The burden is unequal: poorer countries suffer more (cholera in Haiti after the 2010 earthquake, through poor sanitation), but some diseases are typical of industrialised countries (Legionnaires' disease, a bacterial lung infection spread by air-conditioning systems).

Two more points. First, pathogens exploit the normal workings of their host's cells, so studying them teaches us normal cell biology and gives us ways to treat and prevent infections. Second, only a small fraction of microbes are pathogens. Microbes make up much of the biomass of Earth, produce the oxygen we breathe and the soil nutrients for our food, and most microbes living on and in our body are harmless or even helpful.

2. How a virus differs from a cell (3.2)

The syllabus opens with SARS-CoV-2, the virus that causes COVID-19: it infects the cells of the airways, multiplies there and spreads to other people in exhaled breath. How does such a virus differ from a cell?

  • Viruses are much smaller and simpler than both prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.
  • They lack the structures and the metabolic machinery of cells: no ribosomes, no enzymes for making energy, no membrane of their own (an envelope, when present, is borrowed from the host).
  • They exist as infectious particles made of genes encased in a protein coat.

Are viruses alive?

Historically viruses were seen as biological chemicals; the Latin word virus means poison (gif). Because they cause so many diseases, researchers in the late 1800s compared them with bacteria and called them the simplest living forms. But a virus cannot reproduce or perform any metabolic activity without a host cell. Most biologists today agree that viruses are not truly alive: they occupy a grey area between living things and chemicals. Outside a cell a virus is "a packaged set of genes in transit from one host cell to another".

Size

The smallest viruses are about 20 nm across, smaller than a ribosome; millions would fit on the head of a pin. Even the largest known virus, 1500 nm, is barely visible in a light microscope. Compare: a bacterium is about 1000 nm, a human cell about 10 000 to 20 000 nm.

Samenvatting

Het kan zich niet vermenigvuldigen zonder gastheercel, dus de meeste biologen vinden virussen niet echt levend: een grijze zone tussen leven en chemie. Grootte: 20 nm (kleiner dan een ribosoom) tot 1500 nm.

3. The parts of a virus (3.2.1, Figure 3-1)

Begin state: nothing but a particle. A virus is an infectious particle composed of one or more molecules of nucleic acid, encased in a protein coat, and sometimes enveloped by a membrane. Three parts, from the inside out.

3a. The viral genome

We usually think of genes as made of double-stranded DNA, but viruses break that rule. A viral genome may be double-stranded DNA, single-stranded DNA, double-stranded RNA or single-stranded RNA, depending on the type of virus. A virus is called a DNA virus or an RNA virus according to the kind of nucleic acid in its genome. The genome is usually a single linear or circular molecule, although some viruses have several molecules. Size: the smallest viruses have only three genes, the largest several hundred to 2000; for comparison, a bacterial genome has about 200 to a few thousand genes.

3b. The capsid: the protein coat

The protein shell that encloses the genome is the capsid (capside). It is built from many protein subunits, called capsomeres, but usually of only a few different kinds of protein. The shape depends on the virus:

  • Rod-shaped = helical viruses: the subunits are arranged in a spiral (helix) around the genome. Example: tobacco mosaic virus (TMV, a plant virus), a rigid rod made of more than 1000 molecules of a single protein.
  • Polyhedral = icosahedral viruses: a closed shell with flat faces. An icosahedron is a solid with 20 triangular faces (ikosaëder; eikosi = twenty). Example: adenoviruses (viruses of the respiratory tract of animals), with 252 identical protein molecules arranged in such a 20-sided shell.
  • More complex: the bacteriophages, or phages (viruses that infect bacteria; phagein = to eat) have the most complex capsids: an icosahedral head plus a protein tail with fibres, which they use to inject their DNA into a bacterium.

Some viruses carry a few viral enzymes inside the capsid, such as a viral polymerase (an enzyme that copies nucleic acid). Page 3.3 shows why: a retrovirus brings its own reverse transcriptase.

3c. The envelope: a borrowed membrane

Some viruses have an extra layer outside the capsid: a membranous envelope (envelop = omhulsel). Influenza viruses, coronaviruses and many other animal viruses have one. The envelope is derived from the host cell's membranes: it contains host phospholipids and host membrane proteins, together with viral proteins and glycoproteins that stick out as spikes. Those spikes are the "keys" with which the virus recognises a host cell (page 3.2).

Must remember

Every virus = genome + capsid. Some viruses = genome + capsid + envelope. The envelope is not made by the virus from scratch: it is a piece of host membrane with viral glycoproteins planted in it. The capsid and the envelope are what the virus uses to get into a cell; the genome is what it brings in.

Why viruses were so useful to science

The simplicity of viruses made them valuable tools. Studying the viruses that infect bacteria (phages) gave crucial evidence that genes are made of nucleic acids, and helped work out the molecular mechanisms of DNA replication, transcription and translation (the subjects of BIO01 chapters 4 and 5).

(2) De capside: eiwitjas van vele subeenheden (capsomeren) van weinig soorten eiwit; helicaal (staaf, TMV) of icosaëdrisch (20 driehoekige vlakken, adenovirus met 252 eiwitten); bacteriofagen hebben de ingewikkeldste capside (kop + staart). 6. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

What is a virus made of?
One or more molecules of nucleic acid (DNA or RNA), encased in a protein coat (the capsid), and in some viruses also wrapped in a membranous envelope.
Why are viruses said not to be truly alive?
They have no metabolism and no ribosomes, and they cannot reproduce or do anything without a host cell. Outside a cell they are packaged genes in transit; most biologists put them in a grey area between life and chemicals.
How big are viruses?
From about 20 nm (smaller than a ribosome; millions fit on a pinhead) to 1500 nm for the largest known virus, which is barely visible in a light microscope.
What kinds of nucleic acid can a viral genome be?
Double-stranded DNA, single-stranded DNA, double-stranded RNA or single-stranded RNA. The virus is called a DNA virus or an RNA virus accordingly. Usually one linear or circular molecule, sometimes several.
How many genes does a virus have?
From only three in the smallest viruses to several hundred or 2000 in the largest. A bacterium has about 200 to a few thousand.
What is a capsid, and what are the two common shapes?
The protein shell around the genome, built from many subunits (capsomeres) of few protein types. Rod-shaped helical capsids (TMV: over 1000 copies of one protein in a spiral) and icosahedral capsids with 20 triangular faces (adenovirus: 252 identical proteins).
Which viruses have the most complex capsids?
Bacteriophages (phages), the viruses that infect bacteria: a head, a tail and fibres used to inject DNA into the bacterium.
Where does a viral envelope come from, and what does it contain?
From the host cell's membranes. It contains host phospholipids and host membrane proteins plus viral proteins and glycoproteins. Influenza viruses and coronaviruses are enveloped.
Can a virus carry enzymes?
Yes, some carry a few viral enzymes inside the capsid, for example a viral polymerase.
What share of human deaths is caused by infectious diseases, and which bacterium causes most stomach ulcers?
About one quarter of all deaths worldwide. Helicobacter pylori, infecting the stomach lining, causes most stomach ulcers.
Why were viruses so useful for molecular biology?
Their simplicity. Phage experiments gave crucial evidence that genes are made of nucleic acids and helped work out DNA replication, transcription and translation.

Learn these by heart

  • Not truly alive: cannot reproduce without a host cell; grey area between life and chemicals.
  • Size 20 nm (smaller than a ribosome) to 1500 nm.
  • Genome: dsDNA, ssDNA, dsRNA or ssRNA; linear or circular; 3 to 2000 genes.
  • Helical = rod, spiral of subunits (tobacco mosaic virus, 1000+ copies of one protein).
  • Icosahedral = 20 triangular faces (adenovirus, 252 identical proteins).
  • Phages (viruses of bacteria) = the most complex capsids, with a tail.
  • Envelope = host membrane (host lipids and proteins) + viral glycoproteins; influenza, coronavirus.

8. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. Which of these does every virus have?
    • a membrane envelope
    • ribosomes
    • a nucleic acid genome inside a protein capsid
    • a tail to inject DNA
    Answer

    c. Genome plus capsid is the definition. An envelope is present only in some viruses; a tail only in phages; ribosomes never (the virus uses the host's).

  2. True or false: "A virus has its own metabolism, but it is very slow."
    Answer

    False. A virus has no metabolism at all and no enzymes for making energy; it is completely dependent on the host cell. That is why it is not considered truly alive.

  3. A particle is 20 nm across and consists of RNA in a protein shell. Is it bigger or smaller than a ribosome?
    Answer

    Smaller. The smallest viruses (about 20 nm) are smaller than a ribosome (about 25 nm). The largest viruses reach 1500 nm.

  4. Name the four possible kinds of viral genome.
    Answer

    Double-stranded DNA, single-stranded DNA, double-stranded RNA, single-stranded RNA. Our own cells only use dsDNA as genome; viruses use all four.

  5. What decides whether a virus is called a "DNA virus" or an "RNA virus"?
    Answer

    The kind of nucleic acid that makes up its genome. (Not what it uses inside the cell: an RNA virus still needs the host's DNA-based machinery, and a retrovirus even makes a DNA copy, page 3.3.)

  6. Tobacco mosaic virus is a rigid rod of more than 1000 copies of one protein. What capsid type is this?
    Answer

    Helical. The subunits wind in a spiral (helix) around the genome; rod-shaped viruses are called helical viruses.

  7. Adenovirus has 252 identical protein molecules arranged in 20 triangular faces. What is this shape called?
    Answer

    An icosahedron; such viruses are icosahedral viruses. Many protein copies, but only one kind of protein: capsids are built from few protein types.

  8. Where does the envelope of an influenza virus come from?
    • it is made by viral enzymes from scratch
    • it is a piece of host cell membrane with viral glycoproteins in it
    • it is part of the capsid
    • it comes from another virus
    Answer

    b. The envelope is derived from the host's membranes: host phospholipids and proteins, plus viral proteins and glycoproteins that the virus planted there (page 3.2 shows how).

  9. Which viruses have the most complex capsids, and what is the extra part for?
    Answer

    Bacteriophages, the viruses of bacteria. Their tail apparatus injects the viral DNA into the bacterium.

  10. How many genes can a viral genome contain?
    Answer

    From 3 (the smallest viruses) to several hundred or 2000 (the largest). A bacterium has about 200 to a few thousand, so the biggest viruses overlap with the smallest bacteria.

  11. Infectious diseases cause what fraction of human deaths worldwide, and how does that compare with cancer?
    Answer

    About one quarter; that is more than all forms of cancer combined, second only to cardiovascular diseases.

  12. What causes most stomach ulcers?
    Answer

    Infection of the stomach lining by the bacterium Helicobacter pylori, not stress or spicy food. (An example of a disease now known to be an infection.)

  13. Why does the syllabus say viruses "occupy a grey area"?
    Answer

    They have genes and can evolve and cause disease like living things, but they cannot reproduce or metabolise on their own; they need a host cell for everything. So they sit between living organisms and chemicals.
