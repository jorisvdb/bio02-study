3.3 RNA viruses, retroviruses and the HIV cycle

In one sentence: animal viruses are sorted into six classes by their genome, RNA viruses need enzymes that cells do not have, and the retroviruses (HIV) go one step further: they copy their RNA back into DNA with reverse transcriptase and build that DNA into the host's chromosome for good.

This is the page the example exam is about: "What is the function of reverse transcriptase in retroviruses?" and "How do retroviruses, such as HIV, differ from other viruses?". Both questions test one idea: the normal direction of information is DNA → RNA (BIO01 chapter 5, the central dogma); a retrovirus runs it backwards, RNA → DNA. The syllabus also refers to the recording "HIV reproductive cycle". Everything here builds on the general cycle of page 3.2.

1. The six classes of animal viruses (3.2.2.2.2, Table 1)

Animal viruses are commonly classified by their genetic material: double- or single-stranded DNA, or double- or single-stranded RNA, with the RNA viruses split further. Although some phages and most plant viruses are RNA viruses, the broadest variety of RNA genomes is found among animal viruses. Here is the syllabus's Table 1, with the question each class has to solve: "how do I get mRNA, so that the host's ribosomes will make my proteins?"

Three kinds of single-stranded RNA genome (classes IV, V, VI)

  • Class IV: the genome can directly serve as mRNA. The host's ribosomes translate it into viral protein immediately after infection. (Think of it as the virus delivering a ready-to-read message.)
  • Class V: the genome is the opposite strand: it serves as a template for mRNA synthesis. It is transcribed into complementary RNA strands, which work both as mRNA and as templates for making more genomic RNA. Figure 3-3 of the syllabus (page 3.2) shows an enveloped class V virus.
  • Class VI: the retroviruses, section 2 below.
Must remember: the enzyme cells do not have

All viruses that use an RNA genome as a template for mRNA (classes III and V, and class IV when it copies its genome) need RNA → RNA synthesis. There are no such enzymes in most cells. So these viruses use a viral enzyme, an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, encoded by the viral genome. After the viral proteins are made, the enzyme is packaged together with the genome inside the capsid during self-assembly, so the next infected cell gets it straight away.

2. Retroviruses: running the central dogma backwards

The RNA animal viruses with the most complicated replicative cycles are the retroviruses (class VI). They carry an enzyme called reverse transcriptase (RT) that transcribes an RNA template into a DNA copy: an RNA → DNA flow of information, the opposite of the usual direction (DNA → RNA). This backwards step is the source of the name: retro means backward (achteruit).

The medically most important retrovirus is HIV (human immunodeficiency virus), which causes AIDS (acquired immunodeficiency syndrome: the immune system fails, so other infections take over). An HIV particle is an enveloped virus containing two identical molecules of single-stranded RNA and two molecules of reverse transcriptase. Why bring the enzyme? Because, again, the host cell has no enzyme that makes DNA from an RNA template.

Exam link: the two example-exam questions

Q8. "What is the function of reverse transcriptase in retroviruses?" → Reverse transcriptase uses viral RNA as a template for DNA synthesis. Wrong options: it does not convert host RNA into viral DNA (the template is the viral RNA); it does not cut (hydrolyse) host DNA; it does not make RNA from RNA (that is the class V enzyme); it does not translate RNA into protein (that is the ribosome).
Q9. "How do retroviruses, such as HIV, differ from other viruses?" → They can transcribe a DNA copy from an RNA template. The other options are true of all viruses (nucleic acids that code for proteins; reproduce only inside living cells) or simply wrong for HIV (its genome is RNA, not DNA).

3. The HIV replicative cycle (Figure 3-4)

Begin state: an HIV particle (envelope with glycoprotein spikes, capsid, 2 RNA genomes, 2 reverse transcriptase molecules) meets a human white blood cell carrying the right receptor. Goal: new HIV particles, and a permanent base inside the cell.

  1. Entry. The envelope glycoproteins bind the receptor; the envelope fuses with the cell membrane; the capsid enters and releases its contents into the cytoplasm.
  2. Reverse transcription. In the cytoplasm the reverse transcriptase molecules catalyse the synthesis of viral DNA, using the viral RNA as template. (The enzyme also makes the second DNA strand, so the result is double-stranded viral DNA.)
  3. Into the nucleus. The newly made viral DNA enters the cell's nucleus.
  4. Integration. The viral DNA is integrated into the DNA of a chromosome. The integrated viral DNA is called a provirus. It never leaves the host's genome: it remains a permanent resident of the cell, copied along with the chromosome every time the cell divides. This step is unique to retroviruses.
  5. Transcription by the host. The host's RNA polymerase transcribes the proviral DNA into RNA molecules, exactly as it transcribes the cell's own genes.
  6. Two uses for that RNA. The RNA molecules function both as mRNA for the synthesis of viral proteins (by host ribosomes) and as genomes for the new viruses.
  7. Assembly and release. New capsids with two RNA genomes and reverse transcriptase assemble and leave the cell by budding, wrapped in host membrane with viral glycoproteins (page 3.2).
Why HIV cannot be removed from the body

The provirus is part of a human chromosome and is copied with it; it is invisible to drugs that attack free virus. That is why HIV infection is treated but not cured: the drugs stop new rounds of the cycle (for instance by blocking reverse transcriptase), but the integrated copies stay.

Story from Alberts (background, not exam material)

Retroviruses taught us about cancer. The first tumour virus, the Rous sarcoma virus of chickens (1911), is a retrovirus that had accidentally picked up a chicken gene, Src, on its travels and carried a mutated, overactive version of it. That was the first oncogene (chapter 2). Reverse transcriptase itself became one of the most used tools in the laboratory: it is how scientists make a DNA copy (cDNA) of any mRNA (chapter 4).

4. Chapter summary (3.2.3)

The syllabus closes with four sentences; they are the minimum to take into the exam.

  1. A virus consists of a nucleic acid surrounded by a protein coat. A virus is a small nucleic acid genome enclosed in a protein capsid and sometimes a membranous envelope. The genome may be single- or double-stranded DNA or RNA.
  2. Viruses replicate only in host cells. They use the enzymes, ribosomes and small molecules of host cells to synthesise progeny (offspring) viruses.
  3. Each type of virus has a characteristic host range, decided by whether the cell has surface proteins that the viral surface proteins can bind to.
  4. Many animal viruses have an envelope. Retroviruses (such as HIV) use reverse transcriptase to copy their RNA genome into DNA, which can be integrated into the host genome as a provirus.

  2. Explain why RNA viruses need a virally encoded RNA polymerase (RNA → RNA; no such enzyme in most cells) and that it is packaged into the capsid.
  3. Define reverse transcriptase: it uses viral RNA as a template for DNA synthesis (exam Q8) and makes retroviruses different from all other viruses (exam Q9).

7. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

On what basis are animal viruses classified, and what are the six classes?
On their genetic material: I dsDNA, II ssDNA, III dsRNA, IV ssRNA that serves directly as mRNA, V ssRNA that is a template for mRNA, VI ssRNA retroviruses.
What is the difference between a class IV and a class V virus?
A class IV genome is itself mRNA and is translated immediately after infection. A class V genome is the complementary strand: it must first be transcribed into RNA that works as mRNA and as template for new genomes.
Why must RNA viruses encode their own polymerase?
Copying an RNA genome, or making mRNA from an RNA template, needs RNA → RNA synthesis, and most cells have no enzyme that can do that. The viral genome encodes the enzyme, which is then packaged with the genome in the capsid.
What is a retrovirus, and where does the name come from?
An RNA virus (class VI) that uses reverse transcriptase to transcribe its RNA into a DNA copy: RNA → DNA, the reverse of the normal direction. Retro means backward.
What is the function of reverse transcriptase?
It uses viral RNA as a template to synthesise DNA (reverse transcription). It does not copy host RNA, does not cut DNA, does not make RNA from RNA and does not make protein.
What is inside an HIV particle?
An envelope with glycoproteins, a capsid, two identical molecules of single-stranded RNA and two molecules of reverse transcriptase.
Where does reverse transcription take place in the HIV cycle?
In the cytoplasm, right after entry: the reverse transcriptase molecules are released there and make viral DNA, which then enters the nucleus.
What is a provirus?
The viral DNA after it has been integrated into the DNA of a host chromosome. It never leaves the host genome and is copied with the chromosome; a permanent resident of the cell.
Which enzyme transcribes the provirus, and what is the RNA used for?
The host's own RNA polymerase. The RNA serves both as mRNA for viral proteins and as the genome packed into new viruses.
How do retroviruses differ from other viruses?
They can transcribe a DNA copy from an RNA template and integrate it into the host genome as a provirus. All viruses have nucleic acids that code for proteins and reproduce only in living cells, so those are not differences.
How does HIV leave the cell?
By budding through the plasma membrane, taking a piece of host membrane with viral glycoproteins as its envelope (page 3.2).
What disease does HIV cause, and what does the name mean?
AIDS, acquired immunodeficiency syndrome: the immune system fails because HIV infects and damages certain immune cells. HIV = human immunodeficiency virus.

8. To memorize

Learn these by heart
  • Six classes by genome: I dsDNA · II ssDNA · III dsRNA · IV ssRNA = mRNA · V ssRNA = template for mRNA · VI retrovirus.
  • Class IV: genome translated immediately. Class V: first transcribed into complementary RNA (= mRNA and template for new genomes).
  • RNA → RNA needs a viral enzyme: no such enzyme in most cells; it is encoded by the viral genome and packed into the capsid.
  • Retrovirus = reverse transcriptase: RNA → DNA, the reverse of the central dogma; retro = backward.
  • HIV causes AIDS; enveloped; 2 identical ssRNA + 2 RT per particle; infects certain immune cells.
  • Cycle: entry → RT makes DNA in the cytoplasm → DNA into the nucleus → integration = provirus → host RNA polymerase transcribes → RNA = mRNA and genome → assembly, budding.
  • The provirus never leaves the host genome; integration is unique to retroviruses.
  • Exam Q8: RT uses viral RNA as template for DNA synthesis.
  • Exam Q9: retroviruses differ because they transcribe a DNA copy from an RNA template.
  • Summary: virus = genome + capsid (± envelope); replicates only in host cells; host range by surface proteins; retroviruses RNA → DNA → provirus.

9. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. What is the function of reverse transcriptase in retroviruses?
    • it converts host cell RNA into viral DNA
    • it hydrolyses the host cell's DNA
    • it uses viral RNA as a template for making complementary RNA strands
    • it translates viral RNA into viral proteins
    • it uses viral RNA as a template for DNA synthesis
    Answer

    e. RT makes DNA on the viral RNA template. (a) wrong template; (b) RT cuts nothing; (c) RNA → RNA is the class V enzyme; (d) translation is done by ribosomes.

  2. How do retroviruses such as HIV differ from other viruses?
    • they contain nucleic acids that code for proteins
    • they can reproduce only inside living cells
    • they contain DNA that is used as a template to make RNA
    • they can transcribe a DNA copy from an RNA template
    Answer

    d. (a) and (b) are true of every virus, so they are not differences. (c) is false: HIV's genome is RNA. Only retroviruses make DNA from RNA.

  3. A virus has a single-stranded RNA genome that ribosomes can translate directly. Which class is it?
    Answer

    Class IV. Its genome is itself mRNA, so viral proteins are made immediately after infection. (Class V genomes are the complementary strand and must be transcribed first.)

  4. Why does a class V virus (for example influenza) have to carry a polymerase inside its capsid?
    Answer

    Its genome is a template for mRNA, so the first thing it needs in the cell is RNA → RNA synthesis, and the cell has no enzyme for that. If the enzyme were not packaged with the genome, nothing could happen before any viral protein is made.

  5. True or false: "The host cell's RNA polymerase can copy the HIV RNA genome directly into mRNA."
    Answer

    False. Host RNA polymerase only reads DNA. HIV must first make a DNA copy with reverse transcriptase and integrate it; only the proviral DNA is transcribed by the host enzyme.

  6. Put the HIV cycle in order: integration as provirus, budding, reverse transcription, transcription by host RNA polymerase, entry, viral DNA enters the nucleus, RNA used as mRNA and genome.
    Answer

    Entry → reverse transcription (cytoplasm) → viral DNA enters the nucleus → integration as provirus → transcription by host RNA polymerase → RNA used as mRNA and as genome → assembly and budding.

  7. What does an HIV particle carry besides its envelope and capsid?
    Answer

    Two identical single-stranded RNA molecules and two molecules of reverse transcriptase. The enzyme travels with the genome because the host cannot provide it.

  8. What is a provirus, and why does it matter for treatment?
    Answer

    Viral DNA integrated into a host chromosome; it never leaves and is copied with the cell's DNA. Drugs can block new cycles, but the integrated copies stay, so the infection is controlled rather than cured.

  9. Which step of the HIV cycle happens in the cytoplasm, and which in the nucleus?
    Answer

    Cytoplasm: reverse transcription (RNA → DNA) and later translation of viral mRNA. Nucleus: integration of the viral DNA as a provirus and its transcription by host RNA polymerase.

  10. In which direction does information flow in reverse transcription, and what is the normal direction?
    Answer

    Reverse transcription: RNA → DNA. Normal (central dogma): DNA → RNA → protein. "Retro" = backward refers to this reversed step.

  11. The RNA transcribed from the provirus has two jobs. Which?
    Answer

    It is the mRNA from which host ribosomes make viral proteins, and it is the genome packed into new virus particles.

  12. Why is it wrong to say "HIV contains DNA that is used as a template to make RNA"?
    Answer

    The HIV particle contains RNA, not DNA. DNA appears only inside the infected cell, after reverse transcription. (The provirus is then indeed transcribed, but that DNA is not part of the virus particle.)

  13. Which classes of animal virus have a DNA genome, and which have RNA?
    Answer

    DNA: I (dsDNA) and II (ssDNA). RNA: III (dsRNA), IV, V and VI (ssRNA); IV, V and VI differ in what the single strand is (mRNA, template for mRNA, or retrovirus).

  14. Name the step of the HIV cycle that no other kind of virus performs.
    Answer

    Integration of a DNA copy of its RNA genome into a host chromosome as a provirus (after reverse transcription). The syllabus calls this "a characteristic unique to retroviruses".
