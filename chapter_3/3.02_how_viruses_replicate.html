3.2 How viruses replicate: the general cycle and enveloped viruses

In one sentence: a virus gets its genome into a cell it can recognise, forces the cell to copy that genome and build viral proteins from the cell's own materials and machines, lets the parts assemble themselves, and sends hundreds of new viruses out, by bursting the cell or by budding off wrapped in its membrane.

Page 3.1 described the particle. This page is about what the particle does, which is the second learning objective of the chapter: "the student explains how viruses replicate". It covers the rules that hold for all viruses (host range, the general cycle) and then the first variation: viruses with an envelope, including the herpesviruses that hide in nerve cells. Page 3.3 adds the RNA viruses and HIV. Every step here is a "begin state → steps" story, so follow the numbers in the figures.

1. Obligate intracellular parasites and the host range (3.2.2)

Viruses lack metabolic enzymes and the equipment for making proteins, such as ribosomes. So they are obligate intracellular parasites: obligate = they have no choice (verplicht), intracellular = inside a cell. They can replicate only within a host cell. The syllabus's phrase: viruses in isolation are "merely packaged sets of genes in transit from one host cell to another".

Which cells can a virus infect? The host range

Each virus can infect the cells of only a limited number of host species; this is its host range (gastheerbereik). The reason is a recognition system: a virus identifies a host cell by a "handshake" fit between viral surface proteins and specific receptor molecules on the outside of the cell. Those receptors are normally proteins that do a useful job for the cell; the virus has simply adopted them as doors (portals of entry). No fitting receptor, no infection.

  • Broad host range: West Nile virus and equine encephalitis virus (two different viruses) can each infect mosquitoes, birds, horses and humans.
  • Narrow host range: measles virus (mazelen) infects only humans.
  • Limited to a tissue: even inside one host, a virus usually infects only particular cell types. Human cold viruses infect only the cells lining the upper respiratory tract; HIV binds to receptors present only on certain immune cells.
Must remember

Host range = which species and which cells a virus can enter, decided by the fit between viral surface proteins and host receptors. In an enveloped virus the viral surface proteins are the glycoprotein spikes of the envelope (page 3.1).

2. The general replicative cycle (3.2.2.1, Figure 3-2)

Begin state: a virus particle (genome in a capsid) bumps into a host cell that has the right receptor. Goal of the virus: hundreds or thousands of new particles. The syllabus describes the simplest case: a DNA virus with a capsid of a single kind of protein.

  1. Binding and entry. The virus binds to the cell and the viral genome makes its way inside. How depends on the virus and the cell: some phages use their tail to inject DNA into a bacterium; other viruses are taken up by endocytosis; enveloped viruses enter by fusion of the envelope with the host's plasma membrane.
  2. Taking over. Once inside, the proteins the viral genome encodes commandeer the host (take command): the cell is reprogrammed to copy the viral genome and to make viral proteins.
  3. Copying the genome. The host provides the nucleotides. Many DNA viruses use the host's DNA polymerases to make new genomes along the viral DNA as template. RNA viruses cannot: a cell has no enzyme that copies RNA into RNA, so they use a virally encoded RNA polymerase that uses RNA as a template (page 3.3).
  4. Making viral proteins. The viral genes are transcribed and translated with the host's enzymes, ribosomes, tRNAs, amino acids and ATP. The capsid proteins (capsomeres) pile up.
  5. Self-assembly. New genomes and capsomeres spontaneously assemble into new virus particles; no extra machinery is needed.
  6. Exit. Hundreds or thousands of viruses leave the cell, which often damages or destroys it. This damage, and the body's reaction to it, causes many of the symptoms of a viral infection. The new viruses can infect further cells.
Who provides what

The host provides everything except the plan: nucleotides, enzymes (for DNA viruses even the DNA polymerase), ribosomes, tRNAs, amino acids and ATP. The virus provides the genome and, in some cases, one enzyme the host does not have (an RNA-copying polymerase, or reverse transcriptase; page 3.3).

(4) Virale eiwitten worden gemaakt met ribosomen, tRNA's, aminozuren en ATP van de gastheer. (5) Genomen en capsomeren assembleren vanzelf. (6) Honderden virussen verlaten de cel, die vaak beschadigd of gedood wordt: dat geeft de ziektesymptomen.

3. Animal viruses: two things to look at (3.2.2.2)

Everyone has had viral infections: cold sores, influenza, the common cold. All these viruses replicate only inside host cells, but animal viruses differ in how. The syllabus sorts the variations by two features:

  1. the type of genome: double- or single-stranded DNA or RNA (page 3.3), and
  2. the presence or absence of an envelope (this page, section 4).

Bacterial and plant viruses are not discussed in this chapter. The syllabus also points to a recording, "HIV reproductive cycle", which belongs with page 3.3.

4. Enveloped viruses: entering and leaving through the membrane (3.2.2.2.1, Figure 3-3)

An animal virus with an envelope (a membranous outer layer, page 3.1) uses that envelope to enter the host cell. The viral glycoproteins sticking out of the envelope bind to specific receptors on the cell surface; then the envelope fuses with the cell membrane and the capsid is inside. The interesting part is how the virus gets a new envelope on the way out. Begin state: the viral genome is inside the cell and is being expressed. Goal: new enveloped particles outside the cell.

  1. Making the spikes. Ribosomes bound to the ER of the host cell make the protein parts of the viral envelope glycoproteins (the same route a cell uses for its own membrane proteins, BIO01 chapter 5).
  2. Adding the sugars. Cellular enzymes in the ER and the Golgi apparatus add the sugar chains, turning them into glycoproteins.
  3. To the surface. The viral glycoproteins, embedded in membrane derived from the host cell, are transported to the cell surface and sit in the plasma membrane.
  4. Budding. In a process much like exocytosis, new viral capsids are wrapped in membrane as they bud from the cell: the capsid pushes out through a patch of membrane that carries the viral glycoproteins, and pinches off.

So the viral envelope is usually derived from the host's plasma membrane, although all or most of the molecules in it are specified by viral genes. Important difference with the general cycle above: this replicative cycle does not necessarily kill the host cell, in contrast to the lytic cycles of phages (lytic = the cell bursts, lysis = splitting open). The enveloped viruses are now free to infect other cells.

A variation: herpesviruses

Some viruses get their envelope not from the plasma membrane. Herpesviruses (the viruses of cold sores, koortsblaasjes, and genital sores) are temporarily cloaked in membrane from the nuclear envelope of the host; they shed that membrane in the cytoplasm and acquire a new envelope from the membrane of the Golgi apparatus. Herpesviruses have a double-stranded DNA genome and replicate within the host cell nucleus, using a mix of viral and cellular enzymes to copy and transcribe their DNA.

Their special trick is latency (latent = present but inactive, sluimerend). Copies of the viral DNA can remain behind as mini-chromosomes in the nuclei of certain nerve cells. There they stay silent until some physical or emotional stress triggers a new round of active virus production. The new viruses infect other cells and cause the typical blisters. Once someone has a herpesvirus infection, flare-ups can recur throughout life: the virus never leaves.

Learning objective: "The student explains how viruses replicate."

  1. Explain "obligate intracellular parasite" and why viruses are one (no metabolism, no ribosomes).
  2. Explain host range through the receptor "handshake", with broad (West Nile) and narrow (measles) examples and tissue specificity (cold viruses, HIV).
  3. Tell the general replicative cycle in order: entry (injection, endocytosis or fusion), takeover, genome copying, protein synthesis, self-assembly, exit with damage.
  4. List what the host provides (nucleotides, enzymes, ribosomes, tRNAs, amino acids, ATP) and say that DNA viruses use host DNA polymerase while RNA viruses bring their own RNA polymerase.
  5. Describe how an enveloped virus enters (glycoprotein–receptor binding, fusion) and leaves (glycoproteins made via ER and Golgi, budding like exocytosis), and that this need not kill the cell.

7. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

What does "obligate intracellular parasite" mean, and why does it apply to viruses?
An organism that can only multiply inside a host cell. Viruses have no metabolic enzymes and no ribosomes, so they can replicate only within a host cell; outside they are packaged genes in transit.
What decides which cells a virus can infect?
The "handshake" fit between viral surface proteins and specific receptor molecules on the outside of the cell. The receptors are host proteins with their own jobs that the virus uses as doors. This gives each virus its host range.
Give an example of a virus with a broad host range and one with a narrow host range.
Broad: West Nile virus (and equine encephalitis virus) infects mosquitoes, birds, horses and humans. Narrow: measles virus infects only humans.
Why does a cold virus not infect your liver?
Viral infection is usually limited to particular tissues: the cold virus binds receptors found only on the cells lining the upper respiratory tract. Likewise HIV binds receptors present only on certain immune cells.
Name three ways a viral genome can get into a cell.
Injection through a phage's tail into a bacterium; uptake by endocytosis; fusion of a viral envelope with the host's plasma membrane.
What does the host cell provide for virus replication?
Nucleotides for new genomes, and enzymes, ribosomes, tRNAs, amino acids, ATP and other components for making viral proteins. Many DNA viruses even use the host's DNA polymerase.
Why do RNA viruses need their own polymerase?
To copy their genome they need an enzyme that uses RNA as a template to make RNA; host cells have no such enzyme, so the virus encodes it itself.
How are new virus particles put together?
By self-assembly: once genomes and capsomeres are made, they spontaneously assemble into new viruses.
Why does a viral infection make you ill?
The exit of hundreds or thousands of viruses often damages or destroys the cell; that cellular damage and death, plus the body's responses to it, cause many of the symptoms.
Describe how an enveloped virus gets its envelope.
ER-bound host ribosomes make the protein parts of the viral glycoproteins; ER and Golgi enzymes add sugars; the glycoproteins travel in host membrane to the cell surface; new capsids bud through that membrane, wrapped in it, like exocytosis. The envelope is host plasma membrane with viral proteins.
Does an enveloped virus kill its host cell?
Not necessarily. Budding lets viruses leave without bursting the cell, unlike the lytic cycle of phages.
How is a herpesvirus different, and why do cold sores come back?
Its envelope comes first from the nuclear envelope and then from the Golgi; it is a dsDNA virus that replicates in the nucleus. Copies of its DNA stay latent as mini-chromosomes in nerve cells; physical or emotional stress reactivates them, and the new viruses cause blisters again, throughout life.

8. To memorize

Learn these by heart
  • Viruses = obligate intracellular parasites: no metabolism, no ribosomes → replicate only in a host cell.
  • Host range = "handshake" fit of viral surface proteins with host receptors; broad (West Nile) or narrow (measles); often one tissue (cold virus: upper airways; HIV: certain immune cells).
  • Entry: injection (phage tail), endocytosis, or fusion (envelope with plasma membrane).
  • Inside: viral proteins commandeer the cell → copy genome + make viral proteins.
  • Host gives nucleotides, enzymes, ribosomes, tRNAs, amino acids, ATP; DNA viruses use host DNA polymerase; RNA viruses use their own RNA polymerase.
  • Self-assembly → hundreds of viruses exit → cell damaged → symptoms.
  • Enveloped virus: spikes made on ER ribosomes, sugars in ER + Golgi, to the surface, then budding (like exocytosis).
  • Envelope = host plasma membrane with viral glycoproteins; budding need not kill the cell (unlike the lytic phage cycle).
  • Herpesvirus: envelope from nuclear envelope, then Golgi; dsDNA, replicates in the nucleus.
  • Herpes latency: mini-chromosomes in nerve cells; stress → flare-up (cold sores), lifelong.

9. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. Why can a virus not multiply in a test tube full of nucleotides and amino acids?
    Answer

    Because it has no enzymes, no ribosomes and no energy system of its own. It needs a living host cell to supply those: it is an obligate intracellular parasite.

  2. Measles virus infects only humans; West Nile virus infects mosquitoes, birds, horses and humans. What is this property called and what causes the difference?
    Answer

    Host range. It depends on which cells carry a receptor that fits the virus's surface proteins (the "handshake"). Measles proteins fit a receptor found only on human cells.

  3. Which of these is NOT a way a viral genome enters a cell?
    • injection by a phage tail
    • endocytosis
    • fusion of the envelope with the plasma membrane
    • the virus drills a hole with its own ribosomes
    Answer

    d. Viruses have no ribosomes. The three real routes are injection, endocytosis and fusion.

  4. Put the steps of the general replicative cycle in order: self-assembly, genome entry, exit, genome copying and protein synthesis, binding to a receptor.
    Answer

    Binding to a receptor → genome entry → (takeover) genome copying and protein synthesis → self-assembly → exit. Exit often destroys the cell.

  5. True or false: "A DNA virus always brings its own DNA polymerase."
    Answer

    False. Many DNA viruses use the host's DNA polymerases to copy their genome. It is the RNA viruses that must bring (encode) their own RNA polymerase, because cells have no enzyme that copies RNA from an RNA template.

  6. List five things the host cell supplies to a replicating virus.
    Answer

    Nucleotides, enzymes, ribosomes, tRNAs, amino acids, ATP (any five). The virus supplies only its genes, and sometimes one special enzyme.

  7. Who assembles the new virus particles?
    Answer

    Nobody: the viral nucleic acids and capsomeres self-assemble spontaneously once they have been made.

  8. Where are the glycoprotein spikes of an enveloped virus made, and where do they get their sugars?
    Answer

    The protein parts are made by host ribosomes bound to the ER; cellular enzymes in the ER and the Golgi apparatus add the sugars. Then they travel in host membrane to the cell surface.

  9. The envelope of an influenza virus is "host membrane". Then which part of it is viral?
    Answer

    The phospholipids come from the host, but the glycoproteins (the spikes) and most other proteins in it are specified by viral genes. The membrane is borrowed; the proteins are the virus's own.

  10. How does budding differ from the lytic cycle of a phage for the host cell?
    • budding always kills the cell, lysis never does
    • budding does not necessarily kill the cell; lysis bursts it
    • they are the same thing
    • budding happens only in bacteria
    Answer

    b. In budding the capsid leaves wrapped in a piece of membrane, like exocytosis, so the cell can survive. Lysis means the cell bursts open.

  11. Which process in the cell's own life does budding resemble?
    Answer

    Exocytosis: a vesicle fusing with the plasma membrane to push something out. Here the capsid is wrapped in membrane as it leaves.

  12. A herpesvirus is enveloped, but its first envelope is not from the plasma membrane. Where does it come from, and what replaces it?
    Answer

    From the nuclear envelope; the virus sheds it in the cytoplasm and gets a new envelope from the Golgi apparatus membrane. Herpesviruses are dsDNA viruses that replicate in the nucleus.

  13. Why can cold sores come back years after the first infection?
    Answer

    Herpesvirus DNA stays behind as latent mini-chromosomes in the nuclei of nerve cells. Physical or emotional stress triggers a new round of virus production, the viruses infect other cells, and blisters appear again. Flare-ups can recur for life.

  14. HIV infects only certain immune cells. Which idea from this page explains that?
    Answer

    Tissue specificity of the host range: HIV's surface proteins bind receptors that are present only on those immune cells. No receptor, no entry.
