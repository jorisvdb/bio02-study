4.11 What you can sequence: whole genomes, exomes, targets, microbial communities, epigenomes and transcriptomes

In one sentence: the same sequencing machines can read everything (a whole genome), only the protein-coding parts (the exome), a few chosen regions, all the microbes in a sample, the chemical labels on the DNA, or all the RNA of a cell; the choice is a trade-off between completeness, depth and cost.

Page 4.10 explained how a sequencer reads DNA. This page is about what you feed it and why. The exam objectives name metagenomics and transcriptomics explicitly, and the chapter-2 pages on cancer genomes (exome sequencing, methylation, RNA levels) use the methods described here. The syllabus figures for this page are 4-39 (overview), 4-40 (exome capture), 4-41 (WGS versus WES versus targeted) and 4-42 (RNA-seq).

1. Whole genome sequencing (WGS): everything (4.9.5)

WGS reads the complete DNA of an organism. With second- and third-generation methods it is now cheap enough to use routinely.

UsesDrawbacks
  • find genomic sequence variations linked to human disease
  • re-sequence a genome quickly: align the new short reads to a reference genome instead of assembling from scratch, which is far simpler
  • catalogue naturally occurring variation between individuals
  • as costs drop: find disease-associated mutations in individual patients
  • the volume of data needs large computers and skilled bioinformatics, especially for large or complex genomes
  • variable coverage: regions with low depth can miss rare mutations or structural variants
  • an incomplete or biased reference leads to assembly and alignment errors
  • ethics: privacy of genetic data; and the clinical meaning of many variants is still uncertain
Samenvatting

WGS leest het hele genoom. Gebruik: ziektevarianten vinden, snel hersequencen tegen een referentie, variatie catalogiseren. Nadelen: enorme datahoeveelheid en bio-informatica, wisselende dekking mist zeldzame varianten, een gebrekkige referentie geeft fouten, en ethiek (privacy, onzekere betekenis van varianten).

2. Exome sequencing (WES) and targeted sequencing: read less, read deeper (4.9.5, 4.9.6)

The reasoning: protein-coding genes are only 1.5 % of the human genome, yet many diseases are caused by changes in exactly that part. If you sequence only the exome, you have to produce and sift through far less data (trillions of bases become a manageable set), so you can afford more depth and more patients. Whole exome sequencing (WES) reads all the exons of all known protein-coding genes.

How the exons are fished out (Figure 4-40)

Begin state: fragmented genomic DNA with linkers, amplified by PCR (as in page 4.10). Goal: keep only the fragments that contain an exon.

  1. Denature the double-stranded fragments to single strands.
  2. Add RNA probes: synthetic RNA molecules whose sequences match all known exons. Each probe carries a biotin. Fragments that contain an exon hybridise to a probe (base-pair with it); fragments from the other 98.5 % of the genome find no partner.
  3. Add magnetic beads coated with streptavidin. Streptavidin grips the biotin, so every probe, with its captured DNA fragment, is now stuck to a bead.
  4. Hold the beads with a magnet and wash away the unbound DNA (the 98.5 %).
  5. Release (elute) the DNA from the probes and sequence it with Illumina or another deep-sequencing method.

The price of reading less: WES misses mutations in promoters and splice sites and all other non-coding regions, which can also disturb a gene. For many questions the gain (1.5 % of the data to interpret) outweighs that loss, so WES has become common. As WGS keeps getting cheaper and analysis tools better, the syllabus expects WES to decline in favour of WGS.

Targeted region sequencing and the depth trade-off (Figure 4-41)

Targeted sequencing goes one step further: only a few chosen regions (for example a panel of known cancer genes) are captured and read. The smaller the region, the more depth you can afford for the same cost:

ApproachRegionTypical depthFindsCost
WGSthe whole genome> 30Xall variant types: SNPs, small insertions/deletions (indels), structural variantshighest
WESexons, 1.5 %> 50X to ~100Xall variant types, but only in coding regionslower
targetedchosen regions> 500Xvariants in the selected regions only; rare variants reliablylowest

Metagenome sequencing: a whole community at once

Page 4.2 noted that most microbes cannot be grown in the lab. Metagenome sequencing skips the growing: DNA is extracted directly from a sample (sea water, soil, the human gut) and all of it is sequenced together. The reads reveal which species are present (the diversity of the community) and which genes they carry (the functional potential, for example genes for breaking down a pollutant). Applications: environmental samples and host-associated microbiomes (the community of microbes living on or in a host).

Exam link

The chapter-2 cancer pages use these methods: a tumour's "complete genome sequence" is WGS, "the 21 000 genes that code for protein (the exome)" is WES, and The Cancer Genome Atlas combined WGS/WES, methylation and RNA sequencing on more than 10 000 tumours. The example exam asks which technique is "most useful for increasing the amount of DNA" (that is PCR, page 4.3) and which identifies the genes two cell types express (a DNA microarray or RNA-seq, page 4.13).

3. Epigenome sequencing: reading the labels, not the letters (Figure 4-39b)

The epigenome decides which genes are open for use. Four methods, all ending in "-seq" (= followed by deep sequencing), map different labels genome-wide:

MethodWhat it mapsHow, in one line
bisulfite sequencingDNA methylation: which C's carry a methyl groupthe chemical bisulfite changes unmethylated C into U (read as T) but leaves methylated C untouched; comparing the sequence before and after shows every methylated C
ChIP-seqhistone modifications (chemical marks on histone proteins that control chromatin accessibility)an antibody that recognises one histone mark pulls out the DNA wrapped around those histones; that DNA is sequenced ("ChIP" = chromatin immunoprecipitation: fishing chromatin out with an antibody)
ATAC-seqopen chromatin: regions where regulatory elements are activean enzyme (a transposase) can only cut and tag DNA that is not packed away; the tagged, accessible pieces are sequenced ("ATAC" = Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin)
DRIP-seqR-loops: places where RNA stays bound to the DNAan antibody against DNA–RNA hybrids pulls those regions out, then sequencing ("DRIP" = DNA-RNA immunoprecipitation); R-loops influence gene regulation and genome stability

Together these methods support everything from broad genetic surveys to targeted questions about regulation. (Remember from page 4.10 that Nanopore can read methylation directly, without bisulfite.)

Samenvatting

4. Transcriptome sequencing: RNA-seq (4.9.7, Figure 4-42)

Begin state: cells of a tissue, each containing thousands of different RNAs in different amounts. Goal: know which RNAs are there and how much of each: the transcriptome. Because sequencers read DNA, the RNA is first converted to cDNA (page 4.3).

  1. Isolate total RNA from the sample.
  2. Fragment the RNA into pieces of sequencing size.
  3. Convert to cDNA with reverse transcriptase; cDNA is more stable and is what the machine can read.
  4. Ligate adapters and amplify by PCR: a sequencing library.
  5. Sequence by NGS (deep sequencing: many millions of reads).
  6. Map the reads to the reference genome or transcriptome and count them per gene.

What RNA-seq tells you: how strongly each gene is expressed in those cells (the prevalence of each transcript), which splice variants are made, which non-coding RNAs are present, and RNA variants. Page 4.13 compares it with the older microarray method. RNA can also be treated with enzymes or chemicals that react with modified ribonucleotides in a way that shows up in the sequence, which maps RNA modifications across the whole transcriptome.

Samenvatting

RNA-seq: totaal RNA isoleren → fragmenteren → cDNA maken met reverse transcriptase → adapters + PCR → diep sequencen → reads mappen op het genoom en tellen per gen. Resultaat: hoeveel elk gen tot expressie komt, welke splice-varianten en niet-coderende RNA's er zijn; met speciale behandelingen ook RNA-modificaties.

8. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

Why is re-sequencing a genome much easier than sequencing it the first time?
Because the reads can be aligned to an existing reference genome instead of being assembled from scratch; the computer only needs to place each read and note the differences.
Name three drawbacks of whole genome sequencing.
Huge data volume needing computing power and bioinformatics; variable coverage that can miss rare mutations or structural variants; errors from an incomplete or biased reference. Plus ethical issues: privacy and variants of uncertain meaning.
What is the exome, and what fraction of the genome is it?
All the exons of all protein-coding genes, about 21 000 genes: about 1.5 % of the human genome.
What is the assumption behind exome sequencing?
That many of the changes underlying human disease lie in protein-coding genes, so reading only the exons finds most disease mutations while cutting the data to be analysed enormously.
How are exon-containing fragments separated from the rest?
The fragments are denatured and hybridised with biotinylated RNA probes for all known exons. Streptavidin-coated magnetic beads catch the biotin; a magnet holds the beads while the unbound 98.5 % of the DNA is washed away; the exon DNA is then released and sequenced.
What does exome sequencing miss?
Mutations outside exons: in promoters, splice sites and other non-coding regions that can also change how a gene works.
Compare the typical depth of WGS, WES and targeted sequencing.
WGS more than 30X; WES more than 50X up to about 100X; targeted sequencing more than 500X. The smaller the region, the deeper and cheaper.
What is metagenome sequencing and when is it useful?
Sequencing all the DNA in a sample (soil, water, gut) without growing the organisms. It shows which species are present and which genes they carry. Useful because most microbes cannot be cultured.
How does bisulfite sequencing reveal methylation?
Bisulfite converts unmethylated cytosine to uracil (read as T) but does not change methylated cytosine. Any C that is still read as C after treatment was methylated.
What do ChIP-seq, ATAC-seq and DRIP-seq map?
ChIP-seq: histone modifications (an antibody pulls out chromatin with that mark). ATAC-seq: open, accessible chromatin (a transposase tags only accessible DNA). DRIP-seq: R-loops, where RNA is bound to DNA (antibody against DNA–RNA hybrids).
Why does RNA-seq start by making cDNA?
Sequencers read DNA, and cDNA is more stable. Reverse transcriptase copies the RNA fragments into cDNA, which then gets adapters and is sequenced.
How does RNA-seq measure gene expression?
Reads are mapped to the genome or transcriptome and counted per gene; a gene with many reads produced much RNA. The reads also show which exons were joined (splice variants) and which non-coding RNAs exist.

9. To memorize

Learn these by heart
  • WGS = everything, > 30X; uses: disease variants, re-sequencing against a reference, variation catalogue.
  • WGS drawbacks: data volume, variable coverage, biased reference, ethics.
  • Exome = all exons = 1.5 %, ~21 000 genes; WES > 50–100X; misses promoters and splice sites.
  • Exome capture: denature → biotin RNA probes → streptavidin magnetic beads → magnet + wash (98.5 % gone) → elute → sequence.
  • Targeted sequencing: a few regions, > 500X, cheapest.
  • Metagenome: all DNA of a sample together, no culturing: species + genes.
  • Bisulfite-seq = methylation (unmethylated C → U/T, methylated C stays).
  • ChIP-seq = histone marks; ATAC-seq = open chromatin; DRIP-seq = R-loops (RNA on DNA).
  • RNA-seq: isolate RNA → fragment → cDNA → adapters + PCR → NGS → map and count.
  • RNA-seq shows expression level, splice variants, non-coding RNAs (and modifications with special treatments).
  • Trade-off: wide and shallow (WGS) versus narrow and deep (targeted).

10. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. What percentage of the human genome does the exome represent?
    • 15 %
    • 1.5 %
    • 50 %
    • 98.5 %
    Answer

    b. Protein-coding exons are about 1.5 % of the genome (about 21 000 genes). The other 98.5 % is washed away in exome capture.

  2. What is the job of biotin and streptavidin in exome capture?
    Answer

    Biotin is the handle on each RNA probe; streptavidin, on the magnetic beads, grips biotin extremely tightly. So every probe with its captured exon fragment ends up on a bead, which a magnet can hold while the rest is washed away.

  3. Why are the probes made for exons and not for introns?
    Answer

    Because the aim is to read only the protein-coding part. Probes matching exons pair with (hybridise to) fragments that contain an exon; intron-only fragments find no probe and are discarded.

  4. A patient's disease is caused by a mutation in a promoter. Would exome sequencing find it?
    Answer

    No. A promoter lies outside the exons, so it is not captured. That is the known weakness of WES; WGS would find it.

  5. Which approach gives the deepest coverage for the least money, and what is its limitation?
    Answer

    Targeted sequencing (> 500X): only chosen regions are read, so each gets many reads. Limitation: it finds variants only in those regions; anything elsewhere is invisible.

  6. True or false: "Metagenome sequencing first requires growing each microbe in culture."
    Answer

    False. The point of metagenome sequencing is to sequence the DNA of the whole community directly from the sample, which is why it reveals organisms that cannot be cultured.

  7. After bisulfite treatment and sequencing, a position that was C in the original reads as T. Was that C methylated?
    Answer

    No. Bisulfite converts unmethylated C to U, which is read as T. A methylated C is protected and still reads as C.

  8. Which method would you use to find all regions of open chromatin in a cell type?
    • bisulfite sequencing
    • ChIP-seq
    • ATAC-seq
    • RNA-seq
    Answer

    c. ATAC-seq uses a transposase that can only tag accessible DNA. ChIP-seq maps a specific histone mark; bisulfite maps methylation; RNA-seq measures RNA.

  9. What is a DNA–RNA hybrid region called, and which method maps it?
    Answer

    An R-loop, mapped by DRIP-seq (an antibody against DNA–RNA hybrids pulls those regions out, then sequencing). R-loops affect gene regulation and genome stability.

  10. Put the RNA-seq steps in order: map reads, isolate RNA, make cDNA, fragment RNA, sequence, add adapters and amplify.
    Answer

    Isolate RNA → fragment → cDNA → adapters + amplify → sequence → map and count.

  11. Gene A gets 10 000 reads and gene B 50 reads in an RNA-seq experiment. What do you conclude?
    Answer

    Gene A is much more strongly expressed than gene B in those cells: far more of its RNA was present, so far more cDNA reads map to it.

  12. A cancer study reports "copy number variations, aneuploidy and methylation changes" in tumours. Which methods from this page gave each?
    Answer

    Copy number and chromosome gains or losses come from WGS (or WES) read depth across regions; methylation from bisulfite sequencing (or direct Nanopore reading). Expression changes would come from RNA-seq.

  13. Why does the syllabus expect exome sequencing to decline?
    Answer

    Because whole genome sequencing keeps getting cheaper and faster and analysis tools improve. Once WGS costs little more than WES, its completeness (promoters, splice sites, structural variants) wins.
