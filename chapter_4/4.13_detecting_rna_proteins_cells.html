4.13 Detecting RNA, proteins and cells: qRT-PCR, reporter genes, microarrays, RNA-seq, Western blot and flow cytometry

In one sentence: to find out whether a gene is used, you measure its mRNA (one gene with qRT-PCR, all genes with a microarray or RNA-seq) or its protein (with antibodies on a Western blot), or you let the gene light up a reporter; to sort and count the cells themselves, you send them one by one past a laser (flow cytometry).

The pages before this one asked "which DNA is there?". The last page of the course asks "which genes are switched on?" and "is this protein present?". Different cells of one body have the same DNA but use different genes (BIO01 chapter 6), so these are the methods that tell a nerve cell from a muscle cell at the molecular level. Example exam question 15 asks exactly this: which technique finds the genes that two cell types express? The answer is the DNA microarray. This page also closes the chapter with a "which technique for which question" table for the whole chapter.

1. The question: how strongly is a gene used? (4.11)

Many genes are regulated at the level of transcription: the cell decides how many mRNA copies to make. So the usual way to study the expression of a gene, or of a set of genes, is to measure how much of its RNA is present. The syllabus gives four ways to do that, from one gene to all genes at once:

  1. qRT-PCR: one gene (or a few), very sensitive, uses PCR on a cDNA copy.
  2. Reporter genes: you do not measure the RNA itself; you couple the gene's control region to an easily seen "reporter" protein and look where and when it appears.
  3. DNA microarray: thousands of genes at once, by hybridization of labelled cDNA to a chip of DNA spots.
  4. RNA-seq (deep sequencing): all RNAs at once, by sequencing the cDNA copies (page 4.11).

Most of these rely on hybridization to probes: a labelled DNA pairs with the RNA (or its cDNA copy) you are looking for. After the RNA methods, two more sections cover proteins (Western blot) and whole cells (flow cytometry).

Samenvatting

2. qRT-PCR: measuring the RNA of one gene (4.11.1, Figure 4-46)

The full name is quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR, also called quantitative PCR or qPCR). "Quantitative" (kwantitatief) means it gives a number: how much RNA there was. It is PCR (page 4.3) applied to RNA, in two stages:

  1. Reverse transcription: reverse transcriptase makes a cDNA copy of the RNA of interest (Figure 4-12).
  2. PCR with a second primer inside the transcript: the cDNA is amplified so that a conveniently sized product appears. The product is measured on an agarose gel, or, much better, by a machine that watches a dye binding to the DNA after every cycle, "in real time".

The trick of reading the amount: in the exponential phase of PCR the amount of product doubles every cycle, so the amount of product after a given number of cycles is proportional to the amount of RNA you started with. The machine draws a horizontal threshold line and notes the cycle at which each sample crosses it. More RNA at the start = fewer cycles needed to reach the threshold. The syllabus example:

RNA in the sampleCycles to reach the threshold
100 ng (nanogram, 10⁻⁹ gram)about 18
1 ngabout 25
1 pg (picogram, 10⁻¹² gram, a thousand times less than 1 ng)about 35

3. Reporter genes: let the gene show itself (4.11.2, Figure 4-47)

A reporter gene is a gene whose product is easy to see. You fuse the regulatory region of the gene you study (its promoter) to the reporter, put the construct into cells or an organism, and wherever and whenever the promoter is active, the reporter appears. The common reporters:

ReporterWhat it isWhat you see
β-galactosidase (lacZ)the enzyme of the E. coli lac operon (BIO01 chapter 6) that splits galactose sugarsa coloured product (blue) when a special galactose derivative is added
luciferasethe firefly enzyme (vuurvliegje)light from a chemical reaction
Green Fluorescent Protein (GFP)a protein from a jellyfish (kwal) that fluoresces by itselfgreen fluorescence, no added substance needed
Spinachan RNA (not a protein) selected to fluoresce when it binds a specific compoundfluorescence from the RNA itself

Two ways of coupling, which report different things:

  • Promoter → reporter: the reporter is made whenever the promoter is switched on. It reports transcriptional regulation only.
  • Reporter fused to the ORF: the reporter is attached to the gene's own protein-coding part. It is only made if the gene is transcribed from its own promoter and translated with its own sequences, so it reports transcription and translation together.

Why reporters are useful: they can be read quickly and repeatedly, so you can follow expression over time; they show expression in particular tissues of a whole organism (Figure 4-47: GFP fused to the promoter of the cyclin E gene in the worm C. elegans lights up a row of dividing cells in the ventral nerve cord); and they allow you to screen thousands of bacterial colonies for the ones that contain the gene or regulatory region you want (a colony that turns blue has lacZ switched on). As an alternative, specific transcripts can also be detected inside cells with the FISH approach of page 4.12.

4. DNA microarray: thousands of genes on one chip (4.11.3, Figure 4-48)

A DNA microarray (chip) is a glass slide with thousands of tiny spots in rows and columns; every spot contains many copies of one DNA fragment, for example a piece of every known ORF of the genome. You met the same kind of chip in aCGH (page 4.12), where genomic DNA was hybridized to it. Here the chip is used to measure transcription genome-wide:

  1. Isolate the pool of RNA from the cells.
  2. Make fluorescently labelled cDNA copies of it (reverse transcriptase + labelled nucleotides).
  3. Hybridize the cDNA mixture to the chip. A cDNA pairs with the spot that holds the complementary DNA fragment, that is, the spot of its own gene.
  4. Scan the chip: the brightness of each spot reflects how much mRNA of that gene the cells contained.

Two samples at once: to compare two conditions (wild type versus mutant, with and without heat shock, nerve cell versus muscle cell), you label the cDNA of sample 1 with a green dye (Cy3) and that of sample 2 with a red dye (Cy5), mix them and hybridize both to the same chip. Each spot is scanned at both colours:

Figure 4-48b of the syllabus shows a famous use: yeast cells were made to go through the cell cycle in step, RNA was taken at fixed time points, and every gene's expression was plotted as one row (red = more than the control, green = less). Genes with a similar pattern were placed next to each other (clustering), which revealed groups of genes that are switched on together at particular moments of the cycle.

Exam link

Example exam question 15: nerve cells and muscle cells express different genes; which technique is most efficient to identify the genes each cell type expresses? Answer: a DNA microarray. Why not the others? PCR amplifies DNA, and qRT-PCR would need a separate reaction per gene. Gel electrophoresis of DNA fragments separates DNA by size; it says nothing about which genes are used. Isolating all proteins of each tissue is possible (mass spectrometry, page 4.8) but far less efficient than one chip that reads thousands of genes in one hybridization. (Today RNA-seq would also do the job; the exam expects the microarray.)

5. RNA-seq: just sequence all the cDNAs (4.11.4)

The newest and most important approach is simply to sequence the cDNAs made from the RNA population, a method called RNA-seq (page 4.11, Figure 4-42). With deep sequencing it is easy to read many millions of cDNAs; counting how often each gene's sequence appears tells its expression level, especially when two populations are compared directly. RNA-seq can also be aimed at sub-populations of RNA: RNAs below a certain size, or RNAs processed in a specific way. One trick from the syllabus: to find unprocessed RNAs, which still carry a 5′ triphosphate (three phosphates at their 5′ end, the way RNA polymerase made them), treat the total RNA with a terminator exonuclease, an enzyme that eats RNAs with a 5′ cap or a single phosphate; only the unprocessed ones survive and are sequenced.

One caveat for all cDNA methods (microarray and RNA-seq)

Both methods need a cDNA copy, and reverse transcriptase does not copy every RNA equally well. An RNA with a strong secondary structure (a region folded back on itself) or with a modified nucleotide can make the enzyme fall off, so that RNA is under-counted or missed. Researchers turn this weakness into a tool: where reverse transcriptase stops tells where the structure or modification sits, and some modifications make the enzyme insert the wrong nucleotide, so a "mutation" in the sequencing data marks the modified position.

Samenvatting

6. Western blot: finding one protein with antibodies (4.12, Figure 4-49)

To follow one specific protein, in a cell extract or in its place in the cell, there are two approaches: antibodies against the protein itself, or antibodies against a tag added to the protein by cloning (page 4.4). This section is about antibodies against the protein.

What an antibody is. The immune system of an animal makes antibodies: Y-shaped proteins that bind one target with very high affinity (strength of binding) and specificity (they bind that target and almost nothing else). To obtain antibodies against your protein, you inject a mouse, rabbit, goat or chicken with the purified protein, or with a short peptide (a piece of its amino acid chain), and collect the antibodies from its blood.

The Western blot (also called immunoblot) detects a specific protein that has been fixed on a membrane:

  1. SDS-PAGE: the protein mixture is separated by size on a polyacrylamide gel (page 4.8). Proteins of known size (markers) run alongside.
  2. Transfer: an electric field moves the proteins out of the gel onto a membrane (a thin sheet that proteins stick to, hydrophobic or positively charged). A dye such as Ponceau S red can show all the proteins and the markers on the membrane.
  3. Blocking: the membrane is soaked in a protein-rich solution (for example BSA, bovine serum albumin, a cheap cow protein) so that antibodies cannot stick to empty spots: this reduces non-specific binding.
  4. Primary antibody: the antibody against your protein is added; it binds its band. Unbound antibody is washed away.
  5. Secondary antibody: an antibody that binds the primary antibody is added. It carries the label: radioactivity, a fluorophore, or an enzyme such as horseradish peroxidase (mierikswortelperoxidase) that makes a coloured or light-emitting product. Wash again.
  6. Detection: autoradiography (for radioactivity) or a phosphorimager / camera (for fluorescence or light) shows one band at the size of your protein.
Controls

The result is only as good as the specificity of the primary antibody. Some antibodies, especially polyclonal ones (a mixture of antibodies from the animal, recognising several spots, or epitopes, on the protein), also react with other proteins. So always include controls, for example an extract from cells that lack the protein of interest: if a band still appears there, the antibody is not specific.

7. Flow cytometry: cells one by one past a laser (4.13)

Flow cytometry (flowcytometrie; "cyto" = cell, "metry" = measuring) analyses the properties of cells or particles in a fluid. The cells are lined up in a single-file stream and pass one at a time through a laser beam, like cars driving one by one through a toll booth (tolhuisje). As each cell passes, detectors record how it scatters the light (its size and shape) and whether it fluoresces. Thousands of cells are examined in a few minutes.

Markers: the power of the method is that you can tag features of interest with fluorescent dyes or with fluorescently labelled antibodies: a protein on the cell surface, DNA content, or another molecule. When a tagged cell passes the laser, the dye lights up and the instrument records the signal. This identifies different cell types (for example kinds of immune cells) and reveals changes in behaviour: are the cells growing, dividing or dying?

Uses: in medicine, flow cytometry diagnoses diseases such as leukemia by finding abnormal cells in the blood, and monitors the immune system in people with HIV (chapter 3: the virus kills particular immune cells, so counting them tracks the disease) or during cancer treatment. In research it shows how cells function, respond to treatments or change in different environments.

11. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

Why do we measure RNA to study gene expression?
Because many genes are regulated at transcription: the amount of mRNA of a gene reflects how strongly the cell uses it.
What are the two stages of qRT-PCR?
First reverse transcriptase makes a cDNA copy of the RNA; then the cDNA is amplified by PCR while a machine measures the DNA-binding dye after every cycle.
Why must samples be compared in the exponential phase?
Only while the product still doubles every cycle is its amount proportional to the starting RNA. Later a component runs out and the curve flattens, so the amounts no longer reflect the input.
Sample A crosses the threshold at cycle 25, sample B at cycle 35. Which had more RNA, and roughly how much more?
Sample A. Ten cycles earlier means about 2¹⁰ ≈ 1000 times more RNA (in the syllabus example: 1 ng versus 1 pg).
What is the main limitation of qRT-PCR?
It only detects the segment between the two primers, so other forms of the same RNA (for example alternatively spliced versions) are missed; it also needs good controls.
What is a reporter gene, and name three.
A gene whose product is easily detected, fused to the regulatory region of the gene you study: β-galactosidase (lacZ, coloured product), luciferase (light), GFP (green fluorescence); also the RNA reporter Spinach.
What is the difference between fusing a reporter to a promoter and to an ORF?
Promoter → reporter shows transcriptional regulation only. Reporter fused to the ORF is only made when the gene is transcribed from its own promoter and translated with its own sequences, so it shows transcription and translation.
How does a two-colour microarray work, and what does a yellow spot mean?
cDNA of sample 1 is labelled green (Cy3), of sample 2 red (Cy5); both are hybridized to the same chip of gene spots. Yellow = equal expression in both; red or green = higher in one sample.
Nerve cells and muscle cells express different genes. Which technique identifies these genes most efficiently?
A DNA microarray (exam question 15): one hybridization reports thousands of genes at once. PCR, DNA gels and isolating all proteins are less efficient or answer a different question.
What caveat applies to both microarrays and RNA-seq?
Both need a cDNA copy, and reverse transcriptase can fall off at RNA secondary structures or modified nucleotides, so such RNAs are missed (a weakness that is also used to map those sites).
Describe the Western blot in six steps.
SDS-PAGE; transfer to a membrane with an electric field; blocking with BSA; primary antibody against the protein, wash; secondary antibody (against the primary, carrying radioactivity, a fluorophore or an enzyme such as horseradish peroxidase), wash; detection of the band.
Why is a control lysate without the protein included in a Western blot?
To check the specificity of the primary antibody: polyclonal antibodies may cross-react with other proteins, which would show up as a band in the control.
What does flow cytometry measure, and give two medical uses.
Cells pass a laser one by one; scattered light gives size and shape, fluorescence shows tagged markers. Uses: diagnosing leukemia from abnormal blood cells, monitoring the immune system in HIV or during cancer treatment.

12. To memorize

Learn these by heart
  • Gene expression is measured as RNA (most regulation is at transcription).
  • qRT-PCR = reverse transcription → PCR with a real-time dye; valid in the exponential phase; more RNA = fewer cycles to the threshold (100 ng ≈ 18, 1 ng ≈ 25, 1 pg ≈ 35).
  • qRT-PCR: extremely sensitive, but sees only the stretch between the primers.
  • Reporter genes: lacZ (β-galactosidase, colour), luciferase (light), GFP (green fluorescence), Spinach (fluorescent RNA).
  • Promoter → reporter = transcription only; reporter fused to the ORF = transcription + translation.

13. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. In qRT-PCR, what does the "RT" stand for, and which enzyme does that step?
    Answer

    Reverse transcription, done by reverse transcriptase (an enzyme from retroviruses, chapter 3) that copies RNA into cDNA. Only then can PCR amplify it.

  2. Three samples cross the threshold at cycles 18, 25 and 35. Which contained the least RNA?
    • the one at cycle 18
    • the one at cycle 25
    • the one at cycle 35
    • they are equal; the cycle number does not depend on the amount
    Answer

    c. Less starting RNA needs more doublings to reach the threshold. In the syllabus: 100 ng ≈ 18, 1 ng ≈ 25, 1 pg ≈ 35 cycles.

  3. True or false: "In qRT-PCR you can compare samples at any cycle, as long as you use the same machine."
    Answer

    False. Only in the exponential phase is the product proportional to the input. Once a component becomes limiting the curves flatten and no longer reflect the starting amounts.

  4. Name the reporter that gives (a) a blue colour, (b) light, (c) green fluorescence without any added substance.
    Answer

    (a) β-galactosidase (lacZ) with a galactose derivative, (b) luciferase, (c) GFP, green fluorescent protein from a jellyfish.

  5. You fuse GFP to the promoter of gene X and see green cells. What do you know, and what do you not know?
    Answer

    You know the promoter of X is active (transcription) in those cells at that moment. You do not know whether X's own mRNA is translated efficiently: for that you would fuse GFP to the ORF of X.

  6. Which reporter is itself an RNA rather than a protein?
    Answer

    Spinach: a selected RNA that fluoresces when it binds a specific compound. The other reporters (lacZ, luciferase, GFP) are proteins.

  7. On a two-colour microarray (sample 1 green, sample 2 red), a spot is bright red. What does that mean?
    • the gene is not expressed in either sample
    • the gene is expressed equally in both
    • the gene is expressed more in sample 2
    • the gene is expressed more in sample 1
    Answer

    c. Red is the dye of sample 2's cDNA, so more red means more mRNA of that gene in sample 2. Equal amounts give yellow (red + green); a dark spot means expressed in neither.

  8. Which technique would you choose to find the genes that a liver cell expresses but a skin cell does not, in one experiment?
    Answer

    A DNA microarray (or RNA-seq): label liver cDNA with one colour and skin cDNA with the other, hybridize to one chip, and read the spots that differ. This is example exam question 15.

  9. Why can an RNA with a strongly folded structure be missed by a microarray or by RNA-seq?
    Answer

    Both need a cDNA copy, and reverse transcriptase can fall off at a secondary structure or a modified nucleotide, so no full cDNA is made and the RNA is under-counted.

  10. Put the Western blot steps in order: secondary antibody, SDS-PAGE, blocking, primary antibody, detection, transfer to a membrane.
    Answer

    SDS-PAGE → transfer to a membrane → blocking → primary antibody → secondary antibody → detection. Washes follow each antibody step.

  11. What is the job of the secondary antibody, and why is it needed?
    Answer

    It binds the primary antibody and carries the label (radioactivity, a fluorophore or an enzyme such as horseradish peroxidase). This way one labelled secondary antibody can be used with many different primary antibodies, and the signal is amplified.

  12. A Western blot shows a band in the lysate of cells that are known to lack the protein. What is the most likely explanation?
    Answer

    The primary antibody is not specific: it cross-reacts with another protein (common with polyclonal antibodies). This is exactly why such a control is run.

  13. Which technique counts how many cells in a blood sample carry a given surface protein?
    • Western blot
    • flow cytometry
    • qRT-PCR
    • FISH
    Answer

    b. Tag the protein with a fluorescent antibody and send the cells one by one past the laser; the instrument counts the fluorescent cells. A Western blot sees the protein in an extract, not per cell.

  14. Give one medical use of flow cytometry and explain it in one sentence.
    Answer

    Leukemia diagnosis: abnormal blood cells are recognised by their size, shape and markers as they pass the laser. Or: HIV monitoring, counting the immune cells that the virus destroys.
