4.8 Separating molecules on gels, and weighing them by mass spectrometry

In one sentence: an electric field pulls DNA fragments (always negative) or SDS-coated proteins (made negative) through a gel, so that small ones run far and big ones stay behind, which sorts them by size; and a mass spectrometer identifies a molecule by weighing its ions with extreme precision.

Most of what the syllabus describes was learned from experiments on purified molecules. To purify a molecule you must separate it from the thousands of others in a cell (syllabus 4.7), and then find out what you have (4.8). This page covers the two work-horses of every lab: gel electrophoresis for DNA, RNA and proteins, and mass spectrometry for identifying proteins and other molecules. The example exam (question 11) checks that you can pair "electrophoresis" with "separation of DNA fragments". The gel of this page is also the read-out of Sanger sequencing (page 4.9) and of the Western blot (page 4.13).

1. Separating molecules: by what? (syllabus 4.7)

To study one molecule you must separate it from the rest. You do that by using a property in which it differs from the others: its mass, shape, overall charge, or its ability to bind a specific reagent. A trick that comes back on later pages: build a tag (a known extra piece) into the protein or nucleic acid by cloning, and then catch the tagged molecule with something that binds the tag. This page uses charge and size.

Samenvatting

Scheiden kan op massa, vorm, lading of binding aan een specifiek reagens; of je bouwt een tag in het molecuul en vangt die.

2. Gel electrophoresis of DNA and RNA (4.7.1, Figure 4-24)

Begin state: a tube with a mixture of DNA fragments of different lengths (for example after a restriction digest, page 4.4, or a PCR, page 4.3). Goal: sort them by size and see them.

The principle rests on two facts. First, DNA and RNA are negatively charged (the phosphates), so in an electric field they move toward the positive pole ("electrophoresis" = carried by electricity). Second, if they have to move through a porous gel, a jelly full of tiny holes, small molecules slip through faster than large ones. Put together: in a given time, short fragments travel far and long fragments stay near the start. This is gel electrophoresis.

  1. Pour a slab of gel made of agarose (a sugar polymer from seaweed) or polyacrylamide, with small wells (slots) at one end.
  2. Put the gel in a tank of buffer (salt solution) and connect a power supply: the end with the wells at the negative pole, the far end at the positive pole.
  3. Pipette the DNA samples into the wells (at the negative end).
  4. Switch on the voltage. All DNA moves toward the positive pole; small fragments move fastest.
  5. Switch off, soak the gel in ethidium bromide (page 4.7) and look under UV light: every size appears as a glowing band. A lane with fragments of known sizes (a "ladder") is run next to the samples to read off sizes.

stepspour gel → samples in wells at the − end → voltage on → DNA runs to + → stain → look under UV → read sizes against the ladder

3. SDS-PAGE: separating proteins by size (4.7.2, Figure 4-25)

Proteins can be run on a gel too, but they are not as simple as DNA. DNA always has the same charge per length; proteins vary in charge and in shape (some are positive, some negative, some round, some long), so they would not move in a predictable way. The trick: make every protein the same shape and the same kind of charge, so that only size decides.

  1. Denature the protein: add the strong detergent SDS (sodium dodecyl sulfate; a negatively charged soap) and usually heat the sample. The protein unfolds and SDS molecules bind all along its hydrophobic (fat-loving) parts.
  2. Often also add a reducing agent, which breaks the disulfide bonds, so that nothing holds the chain folded or holds chains together.
  3. Now every protein is an unstructured chain coated with negative charge, in proportion to its length. Big protein, more SDS, more charge, but also more drag in the gel: the net effect is that they separate by molecular weight.
  4. Run them on a polyacrylamide gel: this is SDS-PAGE (SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis). The gel has two parts: a stacking gel on top that squeezes each sample into one tight band, then a resolving gel that separates by size.
  5. Stain the proteins with the dye Coomassie Brilliant Blue. Run a lane of proteins of known mass as markers; their sizes are given in kilodaltons (kDa).

The syllabus heading also mentions two-dimensional gels, which separate by charge in one direction and then by size in the other; the text gives no details.

4. Mass spectrometry: weighing molecules to identify them (4.8, 4.8.1)

Once a molecule is purified (for example a band cut out of an SDS-PAGE gel), you want to know what it is. Mass spectrometry (MS) identifies the widest range of molecules, even in small amounts: proteins first of all, but also carbohydrates, nucleic acids and small molecules. It determines the molecular weight of a molecule with very high accuracy by measuring its mass-to-charge ratio (m/z). Begin state: a small amount of the sample. Goal: its exact mass, and from that its identity.

  1. Make gas-phase ions: the molecules must fly through a vacuum as charged particles. Two ways: MALDI (matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization: the sample is embedded in a chemical matrix and hit with a laser, which kicks charged molecules loose) or ESI (electrospray ionization: a fine spray of the sample in a strong electric field).
  2. Separate the ions by their mass-to-charge ratio (m/z): in the instrument, light ions and heavy ions behave differently in electric or magnetic fields.
  3. Detect: the ions strike a detector; the result is a spectrum: a plot of signal intensity against m/z, with one peak per kind of ion.

For small molecules the mass is found to within a single dalton; for large molecules somewhat less accurately. That is why big molecules are usually first cut into pieces.

Identifying a protein

  1. The protein is digested (cut) with a specific protease such as trypsin, which cuts at predictable places. The result is a set of peptides.
  2. The mass of each peptide is measured. Because trypsin cuts predictably, a computer can calculate, for every protein that the organism's genome can encode, which peptide masses it should give. For an organism with a known genome, matching the measured masses to the predicted ones is usually enough to identify the protein.
  3. If that is not enough, tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS): a peptide is first weighed, then broken further into its amino acids and those are weighed too, which gives the exact amino acid composition and sequence of the peptide.

Mass spectrometry can therefore give not only the sequence of a protein but also whether its amino acids are modified, for example by phosphorylation (a phosphate group adds a known mass). It works after purification, but also on complex mixtures of hundreds of proteins: the composition of a large protein complex, or how growth conditions change the abundance of all the cell's proteins.

7. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

Why do DNA fragments move toward the positive pole in a gel?
Because DNA is negatively charged: every phosphate in the backbone carries a negative charge. In an electric field it is pulled toward the plus pole.
Why do short fragments end up farther from the wells than long ones?
The gel is a network of tiny pores. Small molecules slip through the pores faster than large ones, so in the same time they travel farther.
Where do you load the samples, and how do you see the result?
In the wells at the negative end of the gel. After the run the gel is stained with ethidium bromide (or a safer dye) and viewed under UV light; each band is a set of fragments of one size, read against a ladder of known sizes.
Why can proteins not simply be run on a gel like DNA?
Proteins vary in charge (some positive, some negative) and in shape, so they would not migrate in a predictable way. They must first be made uniform.
What do SDS, heat and the reducing agent each do in SDS-PAGE?
SDS is a negatively charged detergent that binds along the unfolded protein and coats it with negative charge. Heat helps the protein unfold so SDS can bind. The reducing agent breaks disulfide bonds. Together they turn every protein into an unstructured, negatively charged chain, so the gel separates by molecular weight only.
What are the stacking gel and the resolving gel?
The stacking gel is the top part, which compacts each sample into a single tight band; the resolving gel below separates the proteins according to size.
How are proteins seen on an SDS-PAGE gel, and how is their size read?
They are stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue. A marker lane with proteins of known mass (in kilodaltons) is run alongside; a sample band is compared with the marker bands.
What does a mass spectrometer actually measure?
The mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) of ions. From that the molecular weight is obtained, for small molecules to within about one dalton.
Name the two ways of making ions, and the three stages of the instrument.
MALDI (laser on a sample in a matrix) and ESI (electrospray in a strong electric field). Stages: ionization, separation of the ions by m/z, detection (giving a spectrum of peaks).
How is an unknown protein identified by mass spectrometry?
It is digested with trypsin into peptides whose masses are measured. Because trypsin cuts predictably, the masses are compared with the peptide masses predicted for every protein of the organism's known genome; the matching protein is the identity. If needed, tandem MS breaks the peptides into amino acids to read their sequence.
What else can mass spectrometry tell you about a protein, and can it work in a mixture?
Whether amino acids are modified, for example phosphorylated (extra mass). Yes: it can analyse complex mixtures of hundreds of proteins, such as a whole protein complex or how growth conditions change the abundance of the cell's proteins.
Why cut a big protein into peptides before weighing?
Because the mass of large molecules is measured less accurately; small fragments can be weighed to one dalton, and their individual masses identify the protein unambiguously.

8. To memorize

Learn these by heart
  • Separate by mass, shape, charge or binding; or add a tag.
  • DNA/RNA is negative (phosphates) → runs to the + pole; small fragments run farthest.
  • Gel = agarose or polyacrylamide; samples in wells at the − end; stain (ethidium bromide), UV; ladder = ruler.
  • Exam Q11: electrophoresis = separation of DNA fragments (correct); ligase glues, it does not cut.
  • Proteins vary in charge and shape → SDS (negative detergent) + heat unfold and coat them; reducing agent breaks disulfide bonds.
  • SDS-PAGE separates proteins by molecular weight; stacking gel compacts, resolving gel separates.
  • Stain with Coomassie Brilliant Blue; marker lane in kDa (1 kDa = 1,000 daltons).
  • Mass spectrometry measures m/z → molecular weight to ≈ 1 dalton; ionize by MALDI (laser) or ESI (spray) → separate → detector.
  • Protein ID: trypsin → peptides → masses matched to the genome's predicted proteins; MS/MS = sequence of the peptide.
  • MS also sees modifications (phosphorylation) and works in complex mixtures.

9. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. On a DNA gel, which band is closest to the wells?
    • the smallest fragment
    • the largest fragment
    • the most abundant fragment
    • the most negatively charged fragment
    Answer

    b. Large fragments are held back most by the pores. All fragments have the same charge per length, so charge does not decide.

  2. At which end of the gel are the wells, and why?
    Answer

    At the negative end. DNA is negative and moves away from the minus pole toward the plus pole, so it must start at the minus side.

  3. Which of these tool–use pairs is wrong?
    • electrophoresis – separation of DNA fragments
    • DNA ligase – cutting DNA to create sticky ends
    • DNA polymerase – PCR
    • reverse transcriptase – making cDNA from mRNA
    Answer

    b. Ligase joins DNA ends; cutting and sticky ends are the work of restriction enzymes. This is example exam question 11.

  4. You forgot to add SDS to your protein samples before the gel. What goes wrong?
    Answer

    The proteins keep their own shape and charge, so they migrate unpredictably (some may not even move toward the plus pole). The gel no longer sorts by size.

  5. What does the reducing agent do in SDS-PAGE?
    Answer

    It breaks the disulfide bonds (sulfur–sulfur bridges between cysteines), so that nothing holds a chain folded or two chains together; every polypeptide becomes a separate, fully unfolded chain.

  6. A protein band lies between the 50 kDa and 70 kDa marker bands. What do you conclude?
    Answer

    Its molecular weight is roughly between 50 000 and 70 000 daltons. The marker lane is the ruler; position in SDS-PAGE depends on size only.

  7. What is the stacking gel for?
    Answer

    To compact each loaded sample into one tight band before it enters the resolving gel, so that the bands are sharp.

  8. Which dye stains DNA on a gel, and which stains proteins?
    Answer

    DNA: ethidium bromide (or SYBR Safe, GelRed), seen under UV. Proteins: Coomassie Brilliant Blue.

  9. Mass spectrometry measures:
    • the colour of a molecule
    • the mass-to-charge ratio of ions
    • the length of DNA fragments in base pairs
    • the absorbance at 280 nm
    Answer

    b. The molecules are made into gas-phase ions, separated by m/z and detected; from m/z the mass follows.

  10. Name the two ionization methods and say in one phrase how each works.
    Answer

    MALDI: the sample embedded in a matrix is hit by a laser. ESI: the sample is sprayed in a strong electric field. Both produce charged molecules flying in a vacuum.

  11. Why is trypsin used before identifying a protein by MS?
    Answer

    It cuts the protein at predictable places into peptides. Small peptides are weighed accurately, and because the cutting is predictable, a computer can calculate what peptide masses each protein of the genome should give and find the match.

  12. What does MS/MS add to a single MS measurement?
    Answer

    After weighing a peptide, it breaks the peptide further and weighs the pieces (down to amino acids), giving the exact amino acid composition and sequence when the peptide masses alone are not enough.

  13. True or false: "Mass spectrometry only works on a single purified protein."
    Answer

    False. It also identifies proteins in complex mixtures of hundreds of proteins, such as a whole protein complex or all the proteins of a cell under different growth conditions, and it can detect modifications such as phosphorylation.
