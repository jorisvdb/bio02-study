4.7 Detecting molecules: absorbance, fluorescent dyes and activity assays

In one sentence: you can tell that DNA or protein is present, and how much, by shining light through the sample (molecules absorb light at their own wavelengths), by attaching a dye that glows, or by measuring what an enzyme produces instead of the enzyme itself.

Pages 4.3 to 4.6 made and changed DNA. But how do you know a tube contains DNA at all, how much, and whether it is clean? This page (syllabus 4.6) gives the basic measuring methods used in every lab. The most important one, the Beer–Lambert law, is the only formula in the whole chapter: it links how much light a sample absorbs to how concentrated it is. The A260/A280 ratio from this page is how every DNA sample is checked before sequencing or PCR. The ethidium bromide dye comes back on page 4.8 (gels).

1. Two ways to detect a molecule (syllabus 4.6)

To study or purify a cellular component you must be able to detect it (notice that it is there) and tell it apart from the other molecules in the mixture, and often also measure how much there is (quantify). The syllabus names two general strategies:

  • Use an intrinsic property of the molecule (intrinsic = its own, built in): its biochemical activity (what it does) or its spectroscopic properties (how it interacts with light).
  • Attach another molecule with a convenient property (a colour, fluorescence, a chemical reaction) and detect that instead: a label.
Populations, not single molecules

Almost all these methods look at a population of molecules, millions at once, not at one molecule. When the syllabus says "the properties of a protein" it means the average of a whole population of that protein. Keep this in mind for the whole chapter.

Samenvatting

Een molecuul detecteren kan via een eigen eigenschap (activiteit, of hoe het licht absorbeert) of via een aangehecht label (kleurstof, fluorescentie). Bijna altijd meet je een hele populatie moleculen, niet één molecuul.

2. Absorbance: molecules have a light signature (4.6.1)

Biological molecules absorb light (take it in, so less comes out the other side), but only at certain wavelengths, specific to the molecule. If you measure how much is absorbed at every wavelength you get the absorption spectrum of the molecule: a curve with one or more peaks. The peak is a chemical "signature": it tells you which kind of molecule is there.

  • DNA and RNA have their absorbance maximum at about 260 nm (in the UV). The absorbing parts are the bases (A, C, G and T or U): flat rings.
  • Proteins usually have a peak at about 280 nm, caused by the aromatic amino acids tryptophan, tyrosine and phenylalanine.
  • Both also absorb at shorter wavelengths, because of the backbone (the sugar-phosphate chain of DNA, the peptide chain of proteins).
  • Some small molecules absorb visible light. The cofactor FAD (flavin adenine dinucleotide, a helper molecule that some enzymes need) and its reduced form FADH₂ both have peaks at 380 and 450 nm, so a protein that carries FAD can be followed by measuring those wavelengths.

A molecule that absorbs visible light (400 to 700 nm) looks coloured. A group that gives a protein its colour is called a chromophore (Greek chromo = colour, phore = carrying). Example: the red colour of blood comes from the heme group (an iron-containing ring) bound in the protein globin; heme is the chromophore of hemoglobin.

The spectrophotometer

Absorbance is measured in a spectrophotometer (spectro = of the spectrum, photo = light, meter = measurer). The liquid sample is put in a small transparent vessel with flat sides, the cuvette, and placed in the instrument. A beam of light of the chosen wavelength is sent through the cuvette, and the instrument compares the intensity of the light that comes out (I) with the intensity of a beam that went through air or through pure water or buffer (I₀, "I zero"). The less light comes out, the more the sample absorbed.

The Beer–Lambert law

The amount of light absorbed depends on exactly three things, and the law puts them in one line:

Aλ = ελ · l · c

SymbolRead asMeaningUnit
Aλabsorbance at wavelength λwhat the spectrophotometer measuresno unit
ελepsilon at λ: the extinction coefficient (also: molar absorptivity)a constant for each substance at that wavelength: how strongly one molar of it absorbs; for a protein at 280 nm it is set mainly by the number of tryptophans and tyrosinesM⁻¹ cm⁻¹ (per molar, per cm)
lpath lengthhow far the light travels through the liquid = the width of the cuvettecm (usually 1)
cconcentrationhow much of the molecule is in the liquidM (mol per litre)

The key term is ε: it varies with wavelength and is a property of the molecule. If you know ε for your molecule, one absorbance reading gives you the concentration: c = A / (ε · l). That works best when no other molecule in the tube absorbs at that wavelength.

Worked example (numbers invented for practice)

A protein has ε₂₈₀ = 20 000 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹. In a 1 cm cuvette you measure A280 = 0.50. Then c = A / (ε · l) = 0.50 / (20 000 × 1) = 0.000025 M = 25 µM (micromolar, millionths of a molar). Double the concentration and A doubles to 1.00; use a 2 cm cuvette and A doubles too. That is why the law is a simple multiplication: A grows in proportion to both c and l.

Rules of thumb and the purity check

  • At 260 nm, an absorbance of 1.0 corresponds to about 50 µg/ml of double-stranded DNA, or 40 µg/ml of single-stranded RNA.
  • At 280 nm, an absorbance of 1.0 corresponds to about 1 mg/ml of an "average" protein (it depends on the amino acid composition: more tryptophan and tyrosine means more absorbance).
  • Purity: compare the absorbance at two wavelengths. The ratio A260 / A280 is about 2 for pure DNA and about 0.6 for a pure protein preparation. If a DNA sample has a ratio clearly below 2, it is probably contaminated with protein (the protein adds absorbance at 280 nm, which pulls the ratio down).

3. Fluorescent dyes: making DNA glow (4.6.2)

Besides its own absorbance, a molecule can be detected by binding or attaching a second molecule that fluoresces or has a clear colour. For DNA (and sometimes RNA) the classic dye is ethidium bromide. It is a flat molecule that intercalates: it slides in between the stacked base pairs of the double helix. Once intercalated, it fluoresces under UV light: shine UV on the gel and every place where there is DNA glows orange. This is how DNA bands on agarose and polyacrylamide gels are made visible (page 4.8).

Ethidium bromide is effective but it is a mutagen (it causes mutations, exactly because it sits inside DNA) and a health and environmental risk. Safer alternatives with the same job: SYBR Green, SYBR Safe, GelRed (and GelGreen).

Samenvatting

Ethidiumbromide is een vlak molecuul dat tussen de basenparen van DNA schuift (intercaleren) en dan onder UV-licht fluoresceert: zo zie je DNA-banden op een gel. Het is een mutageen; veiliger alternatieven zijn SYBR Green, SYBR Safe en GelRed.

4. Activity assays: measure what the enzyme makes (4.6.3)

To study how an enzyme works, and how its activity changes with conditions or mutations, scientists often do not detect the enzyme itself but measure its activity: how fast it turns its substrate (the molecule it works on) into product. The products are often easier to detect than the enzyme, especially in tiny amounts or in an impure sample. Such a measurement is an assay (a test that gives a number).

  • Direct: a kinase (an enzyme that attaches a phosphate group to its target protein, BIO01 chapter 2) can be followed by giving it radioactive phosphate and measuring how much radioactivity ends up on the target.
  • Coupled reactions: some products are not easy to measure. Then the reaction of interest is linked to another reaction whose product is easy to measure.

The syllabus example (Figure 4-23). Begin state: topoisomerase II (an enzyme that untangles DNA and uses ATP for it; BIO01 chapter 4) breaks ATP down into ADP and phosphate. Goal: follow how fast it works. ADP itself is hard to measure directly. So:

  1. Other enzymes and chemicals are added that use the ADP produced in a chain of reactions.
  2. That chain consumes NADH (a small energy-carrying molecule) and turns it into its oxidised form NAD⁺.
  3. NADH absorbs light at 340 nm; NAD⁺ does not.
  4. So as topoisomerase II makes ADP, NADH is used up and the absorbance at 340 nm drops. Reading A340 in a spectrophotometer over time gives the speed of the topoisomerase reaction.
principlemeasure the product (or something coupled to it) instead of the enzyme
what you needthe enzyme (even impure), its substrate, a detectable product or a coupled reaction ending in something detectable (radioactivity, absorbance)

7. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

What is an absorption spectrum, and what is it used for?
The absorbance of a molecule measured over a range of wavelengths, drawn as a curve. Its peaks are a signature of the molecule, so it can be used to identify and to quantify the molecule in solution.
At which wavelengths do DNA and proteins absorb most, and which parts of the molecules are responsible?
DNA and RNA at about 260 nm, because of the bases (A, C, G, T/U). Proteins at about 280 nm, because of the aromatic amino acids tryptophan, tyrosine and phenylalanine.
What is a chromophore? Give an example.
A group in a protein that absorbs visible light (400 to 700 nm) and so makes the otherwise colourless protein coloured. Example: the heme group of hemoglobin makes blood red; FAD is another (peaks at 380 and 450 nm).
What does a spectrophotometer compare?
The intensity of a light beam after it has passed through the sample in the cuvette (I) with the intensity of a beam that passed through air, water or buffer (I₀). From the ratio the absorbance is calculated.
Write the Beer–Lambert law and name its parts.
A = ε · l · c: the absorbance at a wavelength equals the extinction coefficient ε (a constant for that molecule at that wavelength, in M⁻¹ cm⁻¹) times the path length l (in cm) times the concentration c (in M).
You know ε of your protein at 280 nm and you measure A280 in a 1 cm cuvette. What can you calculate, and under which condition?
The concentration: c = A / (ε · l). It is reliable only if no other molecule in the sample also absorbs at 280 nm.
A DNA sample has A260 = 1.0 and an A260/A280 ratio of 1.5. What do you conclude?
About 50 µg/ml of double-stranded DNA, but the ratio is well below 2, so the sample is probably contaminated with protein (protein adds absorbance at 280 nm, lowering the ratio).
Why does ethidium bromide make DNA visible, and why is it dangerous?
It intercalates between the stacked base pairs and then fluoresces under UV light, so DNA bands on a gel glow. Because it sits inside DNA it is a mutagen; SYBR Green, SYBR Safe and GelRed are safer replacements.
Why would you measure an enzyme's activity instead of detecting the enzyme itself?
The products are often easier to detect, especially in small quantities or in an impure sample, and the activity is what you want to compare between conditions or mutants.
Explain the coupled assay for topoisomerase II.
Topoisomerase II turns ATP into ADP. The ADP drives a chain of added reactions that consume NADH and make NAD⁺. NADH absorbs at 340 nm and NAD⁺ does not, so the absorbance at 340 nm falls as the enzyme works; the rate of the fall is the enzyme's activity.
How can a kinase be followed directly?
By giving it radioactive phosphate and measuring how much radioactivity is attached to its target protein.

8. To memorize

Learn these by heart

  • DNA/RNA peak ≈ 260 nm (bases); protein peak ≈ 280 nm (tryptophan, tyrosine, phenylalanine); FAD 380 + 450 nm.
  • Chromophore = colour-giving group; heme makes blood red.
  • Spectrophotometer: light through a cuvette; compares I (out) with I₀ (in).
  • Beer–Lambert: A = ε · l · c; ε = extinction coefficient (constant per molecule), l = path length (cm), c = concentration (M).

9. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. You measure a clear liquid at 260 nm and at 280 nm. A260 is high, A280 is half of it. What is most likely in the tube?
    • pure protein
    • pure DNA
    • DNA with protein contamination
    • water
    Answer

    b. A260/A280 = 2 is the signature of pure DNA. Protein would give a ratio of about 0.6 (A280 higher than A260); contaminated DNA would give something clearly below 2.

  2. Which amino acids make a protein absorb at 280 nm?
    Answer

    The aromatic ones: tryptophan, tyrosine and phenylalanine. Their flat ring side chains absorb UV light; the extinction coefficient at 280 nm is set mainly by the number of tryptophans and tyrosines.

  3. In A = ε · l · c, which symbol is a constant that belongs to the molecule, and which two depend on your sample and cuvette?
    Answer

    ε (epsilon, the extinction coefficient) belongs to the molecule at that wavelength. l (path length) depends on the cuvette and c (concentration) on the sample.

  4. A sample in a 1 cm cuvette gives A = 0.40. You pour it into a 2 cm cuvette. What is A now?
    Answer

    0.80. Absorbance is proportional to the path length: the light passes through twice as many molecules.

  5. ε = 10 000 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹, l = 1 cm, A = 0.25. What is the concentration?
    Answer

    c = A / (ε · l) = 0.25 / 10 000 = 0.000025 M = 25 µM.

  6. A260 of a double-stranded DNA solution is 2.0. Roughly how much DNA is there?
    Answer

    About 100 µg/ml: A260 = 1.0 corresponds to 50 µg/ml of dsDNA, and absorbance is proportional to concentration.

  7. True or false: "A protein absorbs light at 260 nm."
    Answer

    Partly true but misleading. Its peak is at 280 nm; it absorbs some light at 260 nm too (and more at shorter wavelengths, from the backbone). That is why protein contamination changes the A260/A280 ratio of a DNA sample.

  8. What is I₀ in a spectrophotometer measurement?
    Answer

    The intensity of the light beam before the sample, measured through air, water or buffer. The absorbance is calculated by comparing I (after the sample) with I₀.

  9. Why does hemoglobin look red?
    Answer

    Because of its heme group, a chromophore: it absorbs part of the visible light, so the protein appears coloured. Most proteins have no chromophore and are colourless.

  10. How does ethidium bromide attach to DNA, and what do you need to see it?
    Answer

    It intercalates: slips between the stacked base pairs. You need UV light; the intercalated dye then fluoresces.

  11. Why are SYBR Safe and GelRed used instead of ethidium bromide?
    Answer

    Ethidium bromide is a mutagen (it causes mutations because it sits inside DNA) and a health and environmental risk. The alternatives do the same job, making DNA visible, but are less toxic.

  12. In the topoisomerase II coupled assay, the absorbance at 340 nm goes down over time. Why down and not up?
    • the enzyme destroys the cuvette
    • NADH, which absorbs at 340 nm, is consumed and becomes NAD⁺, which does not absorb
    • ADP absorbs at 340 nm and is used up
    • ATP fluoresces
    Answer

    b. The ADP made by the enzyme drives coupled reactions that use NADH. Less NADH means less absorbance at 340 nm, so the signal falls as the enzyme works.

  13. Why is a coupled reaction needed at all in that example?
    Answer

    Because the direct product, ADP, is not easy to measure. Linking its production to the NADH → NAD⁺ change turns it into something a spectrophotometer can read.
