4.5 DNA libraries, forward and reverse genetics, and changing a genome

In one sentence: a library is a collection of thousands of clones that together hold a whole genome or all the mRNAs of a tissue; with such a library you can find which gene is broken in a mutant, and once you have a gene in hand you can change it on purpose, in a plasmid or in the genome itself.

Page 4.4 showed how to cut one DNA fragment out and glue it into a plasmid (cloning). This page scales that up: instead of one fragment you clone all fragments of a genome at once (a library), and it introduces the two directions in which biologists work: from a strange-looking mutant to its gene (forward genetics), or from a gene to the effect of breaking it (reverse genetics). It ends with why you often want to change the gene in the chromosome rather than on a plasmid, which prepares page 4.6 (RNAi, CRISPR and knockout mice). The example exam asks what a transgenic organism is; that word is defined here.

1. Site-directed mutagenesis: changing a gene on purpose (syllabus 4.4.4)

Pages 4.3 and 4.4 cloned DNA to make exact copies. But often you want the opposite: to change a gene, or to inactivate it (switch it off), to learn what it does. Introducing mutations at chosen places is called site-directed mutagenesis (site = place, directed = aimed, mutagenesis = making mutations). That is all the syllabus says about it: two sentences, no method.

Not in the syllabus text, only so you can picture it: the usual way is to make a primer (page 4.3) that carries the wanted wrong letter in the middle, and use it in PCR; the copies then all carry the change. Compare with page 4.3, where PCR under "bad" conditions makes random mutations; here the mutation is chosen.

Samenvatting

Gerichte mutagenese (site-directed mutagenesis) = op een gekozen plaats een mutatie aanbrengen, om een gen te veranderen of uit te schakelen. De syllabus geeft geen methode.

2. A library of clones (4.4.5)

In molecular biology a library (bibliotheek) is a collection of DNA fragments, each cloned into its own plasmid (or virus), where every clone carries a different fragment. Like a real library: thousands of books, each with a different text, and together they cover a whole subject. Three kinds:

LibraryBegin stateHow it is madeWhat it contains
genomic librarythe total genomic DNA of an organismthe genome is broken into fragments (by mechanical shearing: tearing the long DNA by force, or by a restriction enzyme); every fragment is ligated into its own plasmideverything: genes, introns, the DNA between genes; together the whole genome
cDNA librarythe mRNAs of one cell type or tissue, at one momentreverse transcriptase copies every mRNA into cDNA (page 4.3); each cDNA goes into a plasmidonly the expressed genes (the genes that were switched on), without introns; a library per tissue or per condition
synthetic librarysequences designed on a computerchemically made DNA, clonedwhatever you designed

What a library is used for: finding an unknown gene

Begin state: you have a mutant yeast strain with an interesting phenotype, but you do not know which gene is mutated. Goal: identify that gene.

  1. Take a genomic library of normal yeast: thousands of plasmids, together carrying every yeast gene.
  2. Transform the mutant strain with the library, so that each cell receives one plasmid with one random piece of normal DNA.
  3. Look for cells in which the phenotype has gone back to normal. In those cells the plasmid must carry a good copy of the broken gene.
  4. Isolate that plasmid and sequence its insert: that is the gene.

Restoring a lost function by bringing in a good copy is called complementation (the good copy "complements", completes, the broken one). A cDNA library made from one tissue or one condition (for example, cells under heat stress) lets you find and study the genes that are expressed in exactly that situation.

Genomic versus cDNA

Genomic library: all DNA, with introns and the DNA between genes; one library per organism. cDNA library: only expressed genes, no introns; one library per tissue or condition. If a question mentions "expressed", "mRNA", "a certain tissue" or "no introns", it is about a cDNA library.

Samenvatting

Een bibliotheek (library) = een verzameling klonen, elk met een ander DNA-fragment. Genomische bibliotheek: het hele genoom in stukken (scheren of knippen), elk stuk in een eigen plasmide; bevat alles, ook introns. cDNA-bibliotheek: alle mRNA's van één weefsel, gekopieerd tot cDNA (reverse transcriptase), zonder introns; alleen de genen die aan staan. Gebruik: een mutante giststam transformeren met de bibliotheek; de kloon die het normale fenotype herstelt (complementatie) bevat het gezochte gen.

3. Why change the genome itself, not a plasmid (4.5 intro)

Section 1 put mutations in a cloned gene. But a gene expressed from a plasmid or a virus often is not expressed at its normal level: more copies, a different promoter, no normal regulation. And to truly eliminate a gene's function you must inactivate the chromosomal copy (or copies: a diploid organism has two). So scientists often want to put mutations into the genome itself. Three approaches, each with its own page or section:

  • RNA interference (RNAi): not changing the DNA at all, but destroying the mRNA, so the gene is "knocked down" (knock down = turned down, not off). Page 4.6.
  • Homologous recombination: replace or delete a specific region of the genome using a DNA piece with matching ends; this is how knockout mice are made. Page 4.6.
  • CRISPR: bring a cutting protein to a chosen sequence and let the cell's repair do the rest. Page 4.6.
The syllabus warns

"None of these approaches are 100 % accurate." Every method sometimes cuts or changes the wrong place.

Samenvatting

Een gen op een plasmide wordt vaak niet op het normale niveau gemaakt, en om een gen echt uit te schakelen moet je de chromosomale kopie (bij diploïden: beide) aanpakken. Daarom verandert men het genoom zelf: via RNAi (mRNA afbreken, "knock-down"), homologe recombinatie (een stuk vervangen of verwijderen) of CRISPR. Geen van de methoden is 100 % nauwkeurig.

4. Forward and reverse genetics

Two directions of working, named after where you start:

Forward geneticsReverse genetics
start witha phenotype (an existing or newly made mutant that is defective in some process)a gene (already cloned, sequence known)
questionwhich gene is responsible?what does this gene do?
methodtreat cells to make random mutations; screen for the phenotype; identify the mutated gene, for example by complementation with a genomic libraryalter the sequence (mutate, delete) and express the altered gene in vivo; analyse the changes in phenotype
syllabus exampleDNA repair: repair mutants should be sensitive to radiation, so screen for radiation-sensitive cellsa new protein found because it binds a known protein: delete its gene and see what happens

Transgenesis

Sometimes you want to add a specific gene to a genome in a non-random way, for example a gene from another species. Putting a specific gene into the genome of an organism is called transgenesis (trans = across, genesis = making), and the result is a transgenic organism: it carries a gene it did not have before, often from another species. (In page 4.6 the same word "transgenic mouse" is used for the engineered mice.)

Exam link

Example exam question 14: "Which of the following would be considered a transgenic organism?" The answer is a rat with rabbit insulin genes: a foreign gene was put into its genome. The other options are not: a bacterium treated with a compound that changes gene expression (no new gene), a fern grown from a single root cell (same genome, just cloning a plant), and a human treated with insulin made by E. coli (the human receives the protein, not the gene; the bacterium is the transgenic one).

Samenvatting

Forward genetics: begin bij het fenotype (maak willekeurige mutanten, zoek er met een bepaald kenmerk) en zoek dan het gen (bv. door complementatie met een genomische bibliotheek). Reverse genetics: begin bij het gen (al gekloneerd), verander of verwijder het, en kijk naar het fenotype. Transgenese = een specifiek gen in het genoom van een organisme brengen; het resultaat is een transgeen organisme (bv. een rat met konijnen-insulinegenen).

7. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

What is a DNA library?
A collection of cloned DNA fragments, usually in plasmids, where each clone carries a different fragment. Together the clones cover a genome or all the expressed genes of a tissue.
How is a genomic library made?
The total genomic DNA is broken into fragments by shearing or by restriction-enzyme digestion; each fragment is ligated into its own plasmid, so every plasmid carries a different piece of the genome.
How is a cDNA library made, and what is special about it?
The mRNAs of one cell type are copied into cDNA by reverse transcriptase and the cDNAs are cloned. It contains only the genes that were expressed in that tissue at that time, and the cDNAs have no introns.
You have a yeast mutant with an interesting phenotype but no idea which gene is mutated. What do you do?
Transform the mutant with a genomic library of normal yeast. The transformed cells whose phenotype returns to normal carry a plasmid with a good copy of the broken gene (complementation). Isolate that plasmid and sequence the insert.
What is the difference between forward and reverse genetics?
Forward: start from a phenotype (a mutant) and find the gene. Reverse: start from a gene you already have, alter or delete it, and look at the phenotype.
Give the syllabus example of forward genetics.
Studying DNA repair: researchers expected repair mutants to be sensitive to radiation, made random mutants, screened for radiation-sensitive cells, and then identified the mutated genes, for example by complementing with a genomic library.
Why not simply express a mutated gene from a plasmid to study it?
Because expression from a plasmid or virus is often not at the normal level, and because the normal chromosomal copy (or copies, in a diploid) is still there and still working. To eliminate a function you must inactivate the chromosomal copy.
What is a transgenic organism?
An organism into whose genome a specific gene has been introduced on purpose (transgenesis), often a gene from another species; for example a rat carrying rabbit insulin genes.
Is a person who injects insulin made by E. coli transgenic?
No. The person receives the insulin protein; their genome is unchanged. The bacterium that carries the human insulin gene is the transgenic organism.
Name three ways to change the use of a gene in the genome.
RNA interference (knock-down of the mRNA, DNA unchanged), homologous recombination (delete or replace a region), and CRISPR systems (targeted cutting followed by repair). None is 100 % accurate.
What is site-directed mutagenesis?
Introducing mutations at chosen sites in a gene, to change its function or inactivate it. The syllabus gives no method.

8. To memorize

Learn these by heart
  • Library = many clones, each with a different fragment.
  • Genomic library: whole genome, shear or restriction digest, every fragment in its own plasmid; has introns.
  • cDNA library: mRNAs of one tissue → reverse transcriptase → cDNAs; only expressed genes, no introns.
  • Finding a mutant's gene: transform the mutant with a genomic library → the clone that complements (restores the phenotype) holds the gene.
  • Forward genetics: phenotype → gene (random mutants, screen, identify). Example: radiation-sensitive repair mutants.
  • Reverse genetics: gene → phenotype (alter or delete the gene, look in vivo).
  • Transgenesis = a specific gene put into a genome → transgenic organism (rat with rabbit insulin genes).
  • Plasmid expression ≠ normal level; to eliminate a function, hit the chromosomal copy.
  • Three ways: RNAi (knock-down), homologous recombination (delete/replace), CRISPR.
  • Site-directed mutagenesis = mutation at a chosen site; none of the methods is 100 % accurate.

9. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. What do all the clones of one library have in common, and what is different between them?
    Answer

    They all use the same vector (plasmid); each carries a different insert. Together the inserts cover the genome (genomic library) or the expressed genes (cDNA library).

  2. Which library contains introns?
    • genomic library
    • cDNA library
    • both
    • neither
    Answer

    a. A genomic library holds raw genomic DNA, introns included. cDNA is copied from mature mRNA, from which the introns were already spliced out.

  3. A liver cDNA library and a muscle cDNA library of the same person differ. Why? Would two genomic libraries differ?
    Answer

    cDNA libraries reflect the mRNAs, and liver and muscle express different genes. Genomic libraries would be the same: every cell carries the same genome.

  4. Which enzyme is essential for making a cDNA library but not for a genomic library?
    Answer

    Reverse transcriptase, which copies RNA into DNA. A genomic library starts from DNA, so it only needs a restriction enzyme (or shearing) and ligase.

  5. Put the steps of finding a mutant yeast gene in order: (1) sequence the insert, (2) transform the mutant with a genomic library, (3) pick the cells with a normal phenotype, (4) isolate their plasmid.
    Answer

    2 → 3 → 4 → 1. The library goes in, the complementing clone shows itself by restoring the normal phenotype, its plasmid is isolated and its insert sequenced.

  6. What is complementation?
    Answer

    A good copy of a gene, brought in on a plasmid, restores the function that a mutation had destroyed. The clone that does this must contain the mutated gene's normal version.

  7. "Researchers made thousands of random mutants and kept the ones that died after a small dose of radiation." Forward or reverse genetics?
    Answer

    Forward. They start from a phenotype (radiation sensitivity) and go looking for the genes (DNA repair genes). Reverse would start from a known gene.

  8. "A protein was found because it sticks to a known protein. The researchers delete its gene and watch what happens to the cell." Forward or reverse?
    Answer

    Reverse. The gene is in hand first; its function is studied by altering it and looking at the phenotype in vivo.

  9. Which is a transgenic organism?
    • a fern grown in culture from one root cell
    • a rat with rabbit insulin genes
    • a human treated with insulin made in E. coli
    • a bacterium treated with a chemical that changes the expression of many genes
    Answer

    b. Only the rat has a foreign gene in its genome. The fern is a clone, the human got a protein, the bacterium got a chemical; none has a new gene.

  10. Why is a gene on a plasmid a poor way to study its normal role?
    Answer

    Its expression level is often different from normal (copy number, promoter, regulation), and the chromosomal copy is still there, so you cannot see what losing the gene does. That is why the genome itself is changed.

  11. True or false: "RNA interference changes the DNA sequence of the gene it silences."
    Answer

    False. RNAi destroys or blocks the mRNA; the DNA is untouched. That is why it is a knock-down, not a knockout.

  12. Why does the syllabus insist that a diploid organism has "copies" (plural) to inactivate?
    Answer

    A diploid cell has two copies of every gene, one on each homolog. Inactivating one leaves a working copy; to eliminate the function, both must be inactivated (page 4.6: crossing heterozygotes to get homozygous knockouts).

  13. What does "site-directed" mean in site-directed mutagenesis?
    Answer

    That the mutation is made at a chosen place (site) in the gene, unlike random mutagenesis (radiation, chemicals, or sloppy PCR) where the mutations land anywhere.
