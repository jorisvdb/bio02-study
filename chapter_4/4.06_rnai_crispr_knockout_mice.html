4.6 Switching genes off: RNA interference, CRISPR-Cas9 and knockout mice

In one sentence: to learn what a gene does, you switch it off, either softly by destroying its mRNA (RNA interference), or for good by cutting or replacing the gene in the genome (CRISPR-Cas9, homologous recombination), and in a mouse that means growing a whole animal from engineered embryonic stem cells.

Page 4.5 ended with three ways to change the use of a gene in the genome. This page explains them one by one, always starting from what the cell already has. RNA interference and CRISPR are both borrowed from nature: RNAi is a control system of plants, animals and fungi, and CRISPR-Cas is the immune system of bacteria. The syllabus explains each in an "intermezzo" (a short inserted text) and then shows how scientists turned them into tools. The knockout mouse is the oldest of the three methods and still the standard way to test what a gene does in a mammal.

1. RNA interference: silencing a gene by destroying its mRNA (intermezzo, 4.5.2)

RNA interference (RNAi) is a mechanism in which a cell uses a gene's own sequence to turn the gene off. Turning a gene off this way is called silencing (het zwijgen opleggen). It occurs in many organisms: animals, plants and fungi. Begin state: a cell with mRNAs being translated, and somewhere a double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) appears whose sequence matches one of those mRNAs. Goal of the cell: stop that gene.

  1. An enzyme called Dicer (a nuclease; "to dice" = to cut into small cubes) cuts the long dsRNA into small pieces of about 21 nucleotides, still double-stranded. These are the small interfering RNAs (siRNAs).
  2. An siRNA attaches to a protein of the Argonaute family (named after a sea animal; a protein that can hold an RNA and cut another).
  3. One of the two strands is removed. The remaining strand stays in Argonaute and is now free to pair with a matching mRNA (A with U, G with C).
  4. When the siRNA strand has found and paired with its mRNA, Argonaute either destroys the mRNA or blocks it so it cannot be used to make protein.

Result: the gene is still in the DNA and is still transcribed, but its mRNA never becomes protein. This is how the cell controls which genes are active; it is also a defence against viruses (which make dsRNA). The miRNAs of BIO01 chapter 6 work through the same machinery.

RNAi as a tool: knocking a gene down (4.5.2)

Instead of disrupting a gene in the DNA, a scientist can use this pathway to destroy a chosen mRNA. You order synthetic siRNAs: short double-stranded RNAs with the sequence of the gene you want to silence, already the right size, so Dicer is not needed. Put into cells, they load into Argonaute and the mRNA of that gene is destroyed. The gene's expression goes down, not necessarily to zero: this is called a knock-down. Libraries of siRNAs (page 4.5) can knock down every gene of a genome one at a time.

used forquickly testing what happens when a gene is turned down; genome-wide screens

Het DNA verandert niet. Als werktuig: synthetische siRNA's tegen een gekozen gen geven een knock-down (minder expressie, niet nul).

2. CRISPR-Cas: the immune system of bacteria (intermezzo)

Bacteria are attacked by viruses (bacteriophages, chapter 3). Some bacteria have a memory of past attacks. Begin state: a bacterium infected by a virus; it survives.

  1. The bacterium captures a small piece of the virus's DNA and stores it in a special region of its own chromosome. That region is called CRISPR: clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats. In plain words: a row of short repeated sequences (the repeats), and between them the stored virus pieces. A stored piece is called a spacer (it sits in the space between two repeats). The whole row is the CRISPR array (array = ordered row). This is the bacterium's memory of the virus.
  2. The array is transcribed into RNA, which is cut into short pieces, one per spacer: the crRNAs (CRISPR RNAs).
  3. Each crRNA attaches to a Cas protein (CRISPR-associated: a nuclease that cuts DNA) and guides it. If the same virus attacks again, the crRNA pairs with the matching virus DNA, and Cas cuts and destroys it. The infection stops.

So: a memory (the spacer in the DNA), a guide (the crRNA) and scissors (Cas). Scientists realised that the guide can be any sequence they like.

Samenvatting

CRISPR-Cas is het immuunsysteem van bacteriën. Een stukje virus-DNA wordt als spacer opgeslagen tussen herhalingen in de CRISPR-array (geheugen). De array wordt afgeschreven tot korte crRNA's; elk crRNA leidt een Cas-eiwit (DNA-schaar) naar het passende virus-DNA bij een volgende aanval, en Cas knipt het kapot.

3. CRISPR-Cas9 as a tool: molecular scissors with a GPS (4.5.3)

The syllabus' image: a pair of molecular scissors guided by a GPS. The scissors are the protein Cas9 (one particular Cas nuclease); the GPS is a guide RNA (gRNA, also called sgRNA, single guide RNA) that the scientist designs with the sequence of the target. Begin state: a eukaryotic cell (a cell with a nucleus: plant, animal, human) whose DNA you want to change at one chosen place. Goal: edit exactly there.

  1. Design a guide RNA that matches the chosen DNA sequence; bring the guide RNA and Cas9 into the cell.
  2. The guide RNA pairs with the complementary DNA sequence, so the Cas9 it carries sits exactly at the target.
  3. Cas9 makes a double-strand break there (both strands cut).
  4. The cell's own repair machinery steps in. Two routes (Figure 4-19):
    • Non-homologous end joining (NHEJ): the two broken ends are simply glued back together, without a model. This is sloppy ("more mutagenic"): letters get lost or added at the joint, which usually wrecks the gene. Good if you want a deletion or knockout.
    • Homologous recombination: if a DNA fragment is supplied whose ends match the DNA on either side of the break, the cell uses that fragment as a model to repair the break, and copies in whatever is in the middle of the fragment. Good if you want to add, remove or replace a precise piece.
  5. With two guide RNAs aimed at two places on a chromosome, two cuts are made: the piece in between can be lost (a large deletion) or the pieces rejoined wrongly (a chromosomal rearrangement).
principlea designed guide RNA aims the nuclease Cas9 at one DNA sequence; the cell's repair of the cut makes the change
what you needCas9 protein (or its gene), a guide RNA matching the target, optionally a DNA template for precise repair
stepsdesign guide → deliver guide + Cas9 → pairing at the target → double-strand break → NHEJ (sloppy, knockout) or homologous recombination (precise, with template)
used forknockouts, precise edits, large deletions (two guides); faster, cheaper and more accurate than older methods
trapCas9 cuts, it does not edit: the result depends on which repair route the cell uses; and no method is 100 % accurate (cuts elsewhere happen)

Applications and ethics

  • Medicine: correcting defective genes in genetic disorders such as cystic fibrosis (a disease of thick mucus in the lungs), sickle cell anemia (misshapen red blood cells) and some forms of blindness; cancer therapy: modifying a patient's immune cells so they recognise and attack the tumour (CAR-T cells).
  • Agriculture: crops resistant to pests, diseases and harsh weather, for example wheat resistant to fungal infection, rice that grows in drought.
  • Infectious disease: changing the DNA of mosquitoes so they can no longer transmit the malaria parasite.
  • Ethical questions: editing human embryos, and changes that are passed on to future generations (changes in the germ line: the cells that become eggs and sperm). The syllabus: use this powerful tool responsibly.
Samenvatting

CRISPR-Cas9 = moleculaire schaar (Cas9) met een GPS (guide-RNA, ontworpen door de onderzoeker). Het guide-RNA paart met de doelsequentie, Cas9 maakt daar een dubbelstrengsbreuk, en de cel herstelt die: via NHEJ (uiteinden direct aan elkaar, slordig → deletie, knock-out) of via homologe recombinatie met een meegeleverd DNA-stuk (precieze verandering). Twee guides → grote deletie of herschikking. Toepassingen: erfelijke ziekten, CAR-T-cellen tegen kanker, gewassen, malariamuggen. Ethiek: embryo's en erfelijke veranderingen.

4. Knockout mice: switching a gene off in a whole mammal (4.5.1, Figure 4-18)

A knockout mouse is a mouse in which one or more genes have been disrupted (knocked out) in every cell. These mice are important tools for studying the role of a gene in mammals. The method relies on homologous recombination in ES cells (page 4.2) and on coat colour as a visible marker. Begin state: ES cells from a white mouse, growing in culture, and a targeting vector: a piece of DNA in which the gene of interest has been replaced by a selectable marker (resistance to the drug G418, an antibiotic that kills mammalian cells) flanked by DNA matching the genome on both sides. Goal: a mouse in which both copies of the gene are gone.

  1. Grow ES cells from a white-coated mouse in culture.
  2. Introduce the targeting vector. In a few cells, homologous recombination swaps the normal gene for the marker: the matching ends line up with the genome and the middle is exchanged (the gene is replaced by the resistance gene).
  3. Select: grow the cells in medium with G418. Only cells that took up the marker correctly survive; grow them as a pure population.
  4. Inject these ES cells into a blastocyst of a black mouse. (A blastocyst is a very early embryo, a hollow ball of a few hundred cells.) The injected ES cells join the embryo's own cells and contribute to the tissues of the animal that develops.
  5. Implant the blastocyst in the uterus of a surrogate black female mouse (a surrogate = a stand-in mother).
  6. Chimeric pups are born: mice with a mixed black-and-white coat, because some skin patches came from the black embryo and some from the white engineered ES cells. (Chimera: a creature made of parts of different animals, from Greek mythology. The mother's own, untouched blastocysts simply give black mice.)
  7. Backcross the chimeras to black mice. If the engineered ES cells also contributed to the germ line (the cells that make eggs and sperm), some pups are white: they inherited the ES-cell genome, with one knocked-out copy (heterozygous).
  8. Cross these heterozygous mice with each other: a quarter of their pups are homozygous, with both copies knocked out in every cell. These are the knockout mice.
Why two coat colours

The ES cells come from a white mouse and the host blastocyst from a black mouse. A pup with patches of both colours proves that the engineered ES cells contributed to its body (a chimera). A white pup from a chimera crossed to a black mouse proves that the ES cells also made its germ cells: only then is the knockout heritable. Coat colour is simply a visible marker; the knockout gene itself is invisible.

8. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

What triggers RNA interference, and what is the result?
Double-stranded RNA whose sequence matches a gene. The result is silencing: the gene's mRNA is destroyed or blocked, so no protein is made, while the DNA stays unchanged.
What do Dicer and Argonaute do?
Dicer cuts long dsRNA into small interfering RNAs of about 21 nucleotides. Argonaute takes up an siRNA, removes one strand, and uses the other to find the matching mRNA, which it then destroys or blocks.
How do scientists use RNAi?
They introduce synthetic siRNAs (short double-stranded RNAs that need no processing by Dicer) with the sequence of a chosen gene. The gene's mRNA is degraded, so its expression is reduced: a knock-down.
What is CRISPR in a bacterium?
A region of the bacterial chromosome where pieces of DNA from viruses that attacked earlier are stored as spacers between short repeats. It is a memory. When the array is transcribed, each spacer gives a crRNA that guides a Cas nuclease to cut the same virus on a later attack.
In the "scissors with a GPS" picture, what is the scissors and what is the GPS?
The scissors are the Cas9 protein, which cuts both DNA strands. The GPS is the guide RNA, designed by the scientist, which pairs with the target sequence and so tells Cas9 where to cut.
Cas9 has cut the DNA. What happens next, and how does the scientist steer it?
The cell repairs the double-strand break. Without a template it uses non-homologous end joining, which is error-prone and usually wrecks the gene (knockout). If the scientist supplies a DNA fragment with matching ends, the cell repairs by homologous recombination and copies the fragment in: a precise addition, removal or replacement.
What can you do with two guide RNAs?
Make two cuts on one chromosome. The piece in between can be lost (a large deletion) or the pieces can be rejoined in the wrong order (a chromosomal rearrangement).
Name three applications of CRISPR-Cas9 and the main ethical concern.
Correcting genes in cystic fibrosis, sickle cell anemia or some forms of blindness; CAR-T cells against cancer; pest- and drought-resistant crops; mosquitoes that cannot transmit malaria. Concern: editing human embryos and making changes that are passed on to future generations.
Why are the ES cells and the blastocyst taken from mice of different coat colours?
So that the contribution of the engineered ES cells can be seen by eye: a patchy chimera shows the ES cells joined the body; a white pup from a chimera crossed to a black mouse shows the ES cells also made germ cells, so the knockout is heritable.
What does the targeting vector do, and how are the right ES cells found?
By homologous recombination its matching ends line up with the genome and the gene of interest is replaced by a G418-resistance gene. Growing the cells in G418 kills all cells without the marker; the survivors are grown as a pure population.
A chimeric mouse is born. Is it a knockout mouse?
No. Only some of its cells carry the knockout, and maybe not its germ cells. It is backcrossed to a black mouse; white pups are heterozygous (one copy knocked out). Crossing heterozygotes gives homozygous pups with both copies knocked out in every cell: those are the knockout mice.
What is the difference between NHEJ and homologous recombination?
NHEJ glues the two broken ends directly together, without a model, so letters are often lost or added. Homologous recombination uses a matching DNA sequence (the intact copy, or a supplied template) as a model, so the repair is exact.

9. To memorize

Learn these by heart
  • RNAi: dsRNA → Dicer cuts → siRNA ≈ 21 nt → Argonaute, one strand kept → pairs with mRNA → mRNA destroyed or blocked = silencing. DNA unchanged.
  • Synthetic siRNAs = knock-down (less, temporary); a knockout changes the DNA.
  • CRISPR-Cas in bacteria = immune memory: virus piece stored as a spacer in the array → crRNA → guides Cas to cut that virus next time.
  • CRISPR-Cas9 tool: guide RNA (GPS) + Cas9 (scissors) → double-strand break at the chosen sequence.
  • Repair decides the outcome: NHEJ = sloppy, letters lost → knockout; homologous recombination + supplied template → precise edit.
  • Two guides → large deletion or rearrangement.
  • Applications: cystic fibrosis, sickle cell, blindness, CAR-T, crops, malaria mosquitoes. Ethics: embryos, heritable changes.
  • Knockout mouse: white-mouse ES cells + targeting vector (gene → G418 marker) by homologous recombination → select → inject into black blastocyst → surrogate mother.
  • Chimera = patchy coat; backcross to black → white pups = germ line, heterozygous; cross them → homozygous knockout (1 in 4).
  • Coat colour is only a visible marker for which cells came from the ES cells.
  • None of these methods is 100 % accurate.

10. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. Put in order: Argonaute binds; dsRNA appears; mRNA destroyed; Dicer cuts; one strand removed; siRNA pairs with mRNA.
    Answer

    dsRNA appears → Dicer cuts (siRNAs) → Argonaute binds → one strand removed → siRNA pairs with mRNA → mRNA destroyed (or blocked).

  2. How long is an siRNA, roughly?
    • about 21 nucleotides
    • about 200 nucleotides
    • about 2,000 nucleotides
    • one nucleotide
    Answer

    a. Dicer cuts the long double-stranded RNA into pieces of about 21 building blocks, the same size class as the miRNAs of BIO01.

  3. True or false: "After RNAi, the gene is no longer transcribed."
    Answer

    False. The gene is still transcribed; its mRNA is destroyed or blocked afterwards. RNAi acts after transcription, and the DNA is untouched.

  4. Why is an RNAi experiment called a knock-down rather than a knockout?
    Answer

    Because expression is reduced, not abolished, and the effect lasts only while siRNA is present. A knockout inactivates the gene in the DNA, permanently and completely.

  5. In a bacterium, what is stored in the CRISPR array and why?
    Answer

    Short pieces of DNA of viruses that attacked before (spacers), between repeats. They are a memory: transcribed into crRNAs, they guide Cas to destroy the same virus on its next attack.

  6. Which part of the CRISPR-Cas9 tool does the scientist design?
    • the Cas9 protein's shape
    • the guide RNA sequence
    • the repair enzymes
    • nothing: it is all from the bacterium
    Answer

    b. The guide RNA carries the sequence of the target. Cas9 is always the same protein, and the repair is done by the cell's own enzymes.

  7. You want to simply destroy a gene with CRISPR-Cas9. Which repair route do you count on, and what do you supply?
    Answer

    Non-homologous end joining: you supply only Cas9 and the guide. The sloppy repair of the cut loses or adds letters, which breaks the gene (a knockout).

  8. You want to change one letter of a gene precisely. What extra do you supply?
    Answer

    A DNA template whose ends match the DNA on both sides of the cut and that carries the wanted letter in the middle. The cell repairs by homologous recombination and copies the template in.

  9. What is a CAR-T cell?
    Answer

    A patient's own immune cell (a T cell) that has been genetically modified so it recognises and attacks the patient's tumour cells. The syllabus lists it as a CRISPR application in cancer therapy.

  10. In making a knockout mouse, what does the G418 step achieve?
    Answer

    Selection. G418 kills mammalian cells; only ES cells in which the targeting vector was incorporated (bringing the resistance gene in place of the gene of interest) survive, so you get a pure population of correctly targeted cells.

  11. A pup from the surrogate mother is entirely black. What do you conclude?
    Answer

    The engineered white-mouse ES cells did not contribute to that pup (or it came from one of the mother's own, uninjected blastocysts). Only patchy, chimeric pups are useful.

  12. A chimera is crossed to a black mouse and some pups are white. Are those white pups knockouts?
    Answer

    Not yet. They are heterozygous: one knocked-out copy (from the ES-cell germ line) and one normal copy (from the black parent). Crossing two such mice gives homozygous knockouts in a quarter of the pups.

  13. Which method changes the genome in every cell of a whole animal?
    • synthetic siRNA
    • knockout mouse via ES cells
    • a cDNA library
    • a plasmid with the gene
    Answer

    b. Because the change is made in ES cells that go on to form the germ line, the offspring carry it in every cell. siRNA only knocks down mRNA in the treated cells; a library or plasmid does not touch the genome.

  14. Correct this statement: "Cas9 edits the gene."
    Answer

    Cas9 only cuts. The cell's repair (NHEJ or homologous recombination) makes the actual change. That is why the outcome depends on the repair route and on whether a template was supplied.
