4.3 PCR and cDNA: making millions of copies of one piece of DNA

In one sentence: PCR copies one chosen stretch of DNA over and over by heating (strands apart), cooling (two short primers bind) and letting a heat-proof DNA polymerase extend them, so that 20 cycles turn a few molecules into more than a million; and when you start from RNA, a reverse transcriptase first turns it into DNA (cDNA).

Almost every other technique in this chapter starts with "amplify the DNA by PCR" (amplify = make many copies). Cloning, sequencing, measuring gene expression, detecting a virus: all need more material than a cell gives you. Three example exam questions are about this page (the dodo DNA, the ingredients of a PCR, and what reverse transcriptase does).

1. What PCR is for (4.3, 4.3.1)

Begin state: a sample with very little DNA, maybe only a few molecules, in which you are interested in one particular stretch (for example one gene). Goal: millions of copies of exactly that stretch, so you can see it, sequence it or clone it. The method is the polymerase chain reaction, PCR (read "P-C-R"). "Chain reaction" because each round doubles the number of molecules and the products of one round are the templates of the next.

Virtually any DNA segment can be amplified. The trick is to choose the boundaries: you decide where the copied piece starts and ends with two primers.

The primers

A primer here is a short piece of single-stranded DNA made chemically in a machine: a synthetic oligonucleotide (oligo = few: a chain of a few nucleotides, typically about 20 bases long). You order two of them:

  • primer 1 is complementary to the 3′ end of one strand of the stretch,
  • primer 2 is complementary to the 3′ end of the other strand.

Because DNA polymerase can only extend a primer at its 3′ end and build 5′ → 3′, each primer starts a new strand that runs towards the other primer. Everything between the two primers gets copied; nothing outside. Primers must be designed with care (there are computer programs for this), because a primer that also fits somewhere else by partial complementarity would copy the wrong piece.

Samenvatting

2. One cycle: three temperatures (Figures 4-10, 4-11)

Everything is mixed in one tube: the template DNA, the two primers (in large excess), the four dNTPs, and a DNA polymerase. The tube goes into a thermocycler (a machine that switches between set temperatures automatically). One cycle has three steps:

  1. Denaturation (denatureren = losmaken), 94 to 98 °C (°C = degrees Celsius): the heat breaks the hydrogen bonds between the base pairs, and the double helix falls apart into two single strands.
  2. Annealing (hybridisation; aanhechten), 50 to 65 °C: the sample cools and the primers find and bind their complementary sites on the single strands. (The two old strands could also re-pair, but the primers are short, fast and present in huge excess, so they win.)
  3. Extension (verlenging), typically 72 °C: DNA polymerase uses each primer as a starting point and adds dNTPs to its 3′ end, copying the template strand.

After one cycle there are two copies of the chosen sequence; the cycle is repeated, and each time the number of template strands doubles: 2, 4, 8, 16 ... After 20 cycles one double-stranded template has given more than a million copies (2²⁰ ≈ 1 million); a run is usually 20 to 40 cycles. The temperatures and how long each step lasts decide how efficient and how specific the reaction is.

Why the polymerase must be heat-stable

Every cycle heats the tube to about 95 °C, which would destroy ("cook") an ordinary enzyme. PCR therefore uses a DNA polymerase from a bacterium that lives in hot springs, Thermus aquaticus; its enzyme is called Taq polymerase and survives the repeated heating. That is what made PCR automatic: you add the enzyme once and let the machine cycle.

Caveats (the syllabus names three)

  1. Wrong binding site: a primer can hybridise to a site that only partly matches; then the wrong DNA is amplified. Careful primer design prevents this.
  2. Copying errors: the polymerase sometimes inserts a wrong base (misincorporation). A mistake made in an early cycle is copied in every later cycle and ends up in a large share of the product.
  3. Length: fragments longer than about 4000 to 5000 base pairs (bp = base pairs, the unit of DNA length) need special polymerases and conditions.

Point 2 can be turned into a tool: under special conditions (changed salt concentration, which lowers the polymerase's accuracy, or unequal amounts of the four dNTPs, which favours wrong insertions) PCR makes random mutants of the fragment. Such a mutant collection can be put into cells and screened for altered protein function.

3. From RNA to DNA: reverse transcription and cDNA (4.3.2, Figure 4-12)

Sometimes the molecule you want to copy or sequence is an RNA: for example all the mRNAs a cell is making right now. PCR only works on DNA. The solution is to first make a DNA copy of the RNA. Begin state: a sample of mRNAs. Goal: double-stranded DNA with the same sequences, ready for PCR.

  1. The enzyme reverse transcriptase (taken from a retrovirus, chapter 3: it is the enzyme HIV uses to copy its RNA genome into DNA) reads each mRNA as a template and builds a complementary DNA strand. This first DNA strand is a reverse transcript, and the DNA is called complementary DNA, cDNA. At this point each molecule is an RNA–DNA hybrid (one strand RNA, one strand DNA).
  2. The mRNA strand is degraded by adding a specific enzyme that breaks down RNA, leaving the single DNA strand.
  3. A DNA polymerase builds the second DNA strand, complementary to the first. The result is double-stranded cDNA.
  4. The cDNA is amplified by PCR as usual.
cDNA has no introns

The mRNA was already spliced in the cell, so it contains only exons. cDNA made from it therefore has no introns, unlike the gene in the genome. This is useful: a cDNA of a human gene can be put into bacteria, which cannot splice, and still give the right protein. It also means cDNA reflects which genes the cell was expressing and how much (page 4.13: qRT-PCR, microarrays, RNA-seq).

5. What the quiz and exam expect from this section

Learning objective: "The student describes the principle, methodology and applications of different forms of nucleic acid based technologies, including PCR ..." and "utilizes fundamental concepts of nucleic acid-based technologies ... to solve problems".

  1. Explain the principle of PCR: two primers set the boundaries, each cycle doubles the DNA.
  2. Name the ingredients (template, two primers, dNTPs, heat-stable polymerase) and recognise wrong ones (ribosomes, tRNA, amino acids, ligase).
  3. Give the three steps with their temperatures and what happens in each; 20–40 cycles; 20 cycles → over a million copies.
  4. Say why Taq polymerase from Thermus aquaticus is used.
  5. Know the caveats (primer mis-binding, early errors amplified, length limit) and that PCR can be used for random mutagenesis.
  6. Choose PCR when the problem is "too little DNA" (dodo question).
  7. Explain reverse transcription: reverse transcriptase makes cDNA from mRNA; RNA degraded; second strand; no introns; then PCR.

6. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

What problem does PCR solve?
Too little DNA. From a few molecules it makes millions of copies of one chosen stretch, enough to analyse, sequence or clone.
How do the primers decide what gets copied?
Each primer is complementary to the 3′ end of one strand of the target. DNA polymerase extends each primer towards the other, so only the region between the two primers is copied.
What are the three steps of a PCR cycle, with temperatures?
Denaturation at 94–98 °C (strands separate), annealing at 50–65 °C (primers bind), extension at 72 °C (polymerase builds the new strands).
Why is the number of copies after 20 cycles about a million?
Each cycle doubles the number of templates: 2 to the power 20 is about 1 048 000.
Why Taq polymerase?
It comes from Thermus aquaticus, a hot-spring bacterium, and survives the repeated heating to about 95 °C that would destroy an ordinary polymerase. So the enzyme is added once and the machine can cycle automatically.
What do you add to a DNA fragment to amplify it?
The two primers, the four nucleotides (dNTPs) and a DNA polymerase (plus buffer). Not ribosomes, tRNA or amino acids (those make protein) and not ligase (that joins fragments).
Why is a copying error in cycle 2 worse than one in cycle 25?
An error made early is copied in every following cycle and ends up in a large fraction of the product; a late error is in only a few molecules.
How can PCR be used to make mutants?
Run it under conditions that lower the polymerase's accuracy (changed salt concentration) or favour wrong bases (unequal dNTP ratios); the product is a collection of random mutants to screen for altered protein function.
Why can PCR not be used directly on mRNA?
DNA polymerase copies DNA. The RNA must first be reverse-transcribed into cDNA by reverse transcriptase.
Describe how double-stranded cDNA is made.
Reverse transcriptase copies the mRNA into a complementary DNA strand (RNA–DNA hybrid); an enzyme degrades the RNA; DNA polymerase makes the second DNA strand. Then PCR amplifies it.
Why has cDNA no introns?
It is copied from mature mRNA, from which the cell already removed the introns by splicing.
Which pairing is wrong: electrophoresis–separation, DNA ligase–cutting DNA, DNA polymerase–PCR, reverse transcriptase–cDNA from mRNA?
DNA ligase–cutting: ligase joins DNA; restriction enzymes cut it. The other three are correct.

7. To memorize

Learn these by heart
  • PCR = millions of copies of one DNA stretch; the two primers set the boundaries.
  • Primer = synthetic oligonucleotide, ≈ 20 bases, complementary to the 3′ end of each strand.
  • Cycle: denature 94–98 °C → anneal 50–65 °C → extend 72 °C; 20–40 cycles in a thermocycler.
  • Each cycle doubles: 20 cycles → > 1 million copies.
  • Ingredients: template, primers, dNTPs, polymerase (not ribosomes, tRNA, amino acids, ligase).
  • Taq polymerase from Thermus aquaticus: heat-stable, survives the cycles.
  • Caveats: primer binds elsewhere; early errors are amplified; > 4000–5000 bp needs special conditions.
  • Error-prone PCR (salt, dNTP ratios) → random mutants for screens.
  • "Increase the amount of old DNA" = PCR (dodo question).
  • Reverse transcriptase (retrovirus) copies mRNA → cDNA; RNA degraded; DNA polymerase makes strand 2; then PCR.
  • cDNA has no introns (made from spliced mRNA).

8. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. Put the three PCR steps in order and give the temperature of each.
    Answer

    Denaturation (94–98 °C) → annealing (50–65 °C) → extension (72 °C). Hot to separate, cooler to let primers bind, warm for the polymerase to work.

  2. Which component is NOT needed for PCR?
    • primers
    • dNTPs
    • DNA ligase
    • heat-stable DNA polymerase
    Answer

    c. Ligase joins DNA pieces; in PCR nothing is joined. The strand is built nucleotide by nucleotide from a primer by the polymerase.

  3. Why do you need two primers and not one?
    Answer

    Each strand needs its own starting point, and the two primers together mark both ends of the region. With one primer only one strand would be copied and the product would not double each cycle.

  4. After 10 cycles, how many copies has one template become?
    Answer

    About 2¹⁰ = 1024. Doubling every cycle; 20 cycles give about a million, 30 about a billion.

  5. True or false: "The high temperature of the denaturation step would destroy an ordinary DNA polymerase."
    Answer

    True. That is why PCR uses Taq polymerase from Thermus aquaticus, which survives repeated heating to about 95 °C.

  6. What happens during annealing, and why do the primers win against the old strands re-pairing?
    Answer

    The primers bind (hybridise) to their complementary sites on the single strands. They are short, find their sites quickly and are present in huge excess, so they bind before the long strands can find each other.

  7. A primer is designed carelessly and partly matches a second site in the genome. What goes wrong?
    Answer

    The primer can hybridise there too, so the wrong DNA sequence is amplified along with (or instead of) the intended one. Primer design programs exist to avoid this.

  8. A forensic scientist has a tiny bloodstain. Which technique first?
    • gel electrophoresis
    • PCR
    • SNP analysis
    • reverse transcription
    Answer

    b. Too little DNA → amplify it first. Electrophoresis and SNP analysis analyse DNA you already have; reverse transcription is for RNA.

  9. You want to amplify a 9000-bp region in one piece. What should you expect?
    Answer

    Trouble: fragments over about 4000–5000 bp need specialised polymerases and conditions.

  10. How would you deliberately make random mutants of a gene with PCR?
    Answer

    Run the PCR under conditions that make the polymerase sloppy: an altered salt concentration (lower fidelity) or unequal dNTP ratios (more misincorporation). Then put the mutant pool into cells and screen.

  11. What is a reverse transcript?
    Answer

    A DNA strand made by reverse transcriptase on an RNA template: the first strand of cDNA (complementary DNA).

  12. Put in order: RNA degraded; reverse transcriptase makes the first strand; PCR; DNA polymerase makes the second strand.
    Answer

    Reverse transcriptase makes the first strand → RNA degraded → DNA polymerase makes the second strand → PCR.

  13. A cDNA and the genomic gene it came from have different lengths. Why?
    Answer

    The cDNA was copied from spliced mRNA and therefore lacks the introns; the gene in the genome still contains them, so it is longer.

  14. Which statement about reverse transcriptase is correct?
    • it translates RNA into protein
    • it uses RNA as a template to make DNA
    • it cuts DNA at specific sites
    • it makes RNA from RNA
    Answer

    b. RNA → DNA, the reverse of the usual direction; the enzyme comes from retroviruses such as HIV (chapter 3, exam question 8).
