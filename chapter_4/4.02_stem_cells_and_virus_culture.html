4.2 Stem cells, iPS cells and growing viruses

In one sentence: a stem cell can divide forever and turn into other cell types, which makes it both a medical hope (replace lost cells) and a risk (tumours); viruses cannot grow on their own, so they are grown inside cultured cells and counted as clear holes (plaques) in a layer of cells.

Page 4.1 ended with the problem of ordinary cells: they stop dividing. This page is about the exception, stem cells, and about the opposite extreme: viruses, which cannot divide at all without a host cell. One example exam question (a fill-in-the-blank) asks you for the two words totipotent and pluripotent, so the definitions in section 1 matter.

1. Stem cells: the cells that can become anything (4.2.3)

A stem cell (stamcel) is a cell that can give rise to many different cell lineages. The syllabus uses two words for how many:

  • Totipotent (toti = all): can give rise to every cell type of the body. The syllabus applies this to embryonic stem (ES) cells, which are taken from early embryos.
  • Pluripotent (pluri = several): can give rise to a restricted set of cell lineages. Such cells exist during development and also in the adult. Adult stem cells (from a fully grown organism) are usually pluripotent; how many cell types they can make depends on the kind of stem cell.
Note on the two words

Follow the syllabus for the exam: totipotent = any cell type (ES cells), pluripotent = a subset. Be aware that many other books use the words slightly differently (there "pluripotent" is the usual word for ES cells, and "multipotent" for adult stem cells). The example exam question 10 uses the syllabus meaning: "totipotent [cells] can give rise to any type of cell whereas pluripotent [cells] can give rise to a subset of cell types."

Adult stem cells have been studied in the skin, in hair follicles (the small pockets in the skin that a hair grows from) and in the intestine, but the best-characterised ones are those of the blood (section 2).

Two properties that make a stem cell

  1. Unlimited division. Stem cells typically divide without limit, in sharp contrast to differentiated cells, which do not divide at all or only a limited number of times (page 4.1).
  2. Asymmetric division (Figure 4-7). When a stem cell divides, the two daughters are not the same: one daughter stays a stem cell (this is called self-renewal) and the other differentiates into a particular cell type. So the stem cell population keeps itself going while it produces specialised cells.

How do you prove a cell is a stem cell?

The best test is to isolate the cell and implant it into a recipient animal. A true stem cell will give rise to the entire set of differentiated cells in that new setting. This transplantation test is the same principle as a bone marrow transplant used to treat blood diseases and cancer (section 2).

2. Blood stem cells: the best-understood example (4.2.3, Figure 4-8)

Blood cells have a short life: some types last only a day. So blood must be renewed continuously, all through life. The process of making new blood cells every day is called hematopoiesis (hemato = blood, poiesis = making; bloedaanmaak), and the stem cells that do it are hematopoietic stem cells. They normally live in the bone marrow (beenmerg, the soft tissue inside bones) and can be isolated from the marrow or from blood.

They can self-renew (make more stem cells) and they produce progenitor cells (progenitor = forerunner): cells that are already on their way and differentiate into many other cell types. The whole family tree, from stem cell via progenitors to red cells, platelets and the white cells, is one of the best understood hierarchies of stem-cell differentiation (a hierarchy = a tree with levels).

Bone marrow transplant: hematopoietic stem cells are isolated from the marrow of a donor and transferred to a patient whose own blood-forming system is diseased (blood diseases, cancer). In the patient they do what they always do: self-renew and rebuild the whole blood system.

Zij vernieuwen zichzelf en maken progenitorcellen (voorlopers) die uitgroeien tot rode bloedcellen, bloedplaatjes en witte bloedcellen: de best begrepen hiërarchie van stamceldifferentiatie. Een beenmergtransplantatie brengt zulke stamcellen van een donor over naar een patiënt.

3. ES cells and iPS cells in medicine (4.2.4, 4.2.5)

ES cells: hope and danger

Because ES cells are totipotent, they can be steered in the laboratory to become one specific cell type. Example: ES cells treated with certain growth factors (signal proteins that tell cells to grow or differentiate) in culture can make more than 99 % of the cells turn into neurons (nerve cells). The hope is to transplant such neurons into patients with Parkinson's disease, in which a specific group of neurons dies.

The danger: if less than 100 % of the cells differentiate, the remaining undifferentiated cells keep growing as ES cells and may later differentiate at random into a tumour. Indeed, injecting mouse ES cells directly into mice gives a tumour called a teratoma: a lump that contains a mixture of many different differentiated cell types (hair, teeth, muscle, gut tissue all jumbled together).

iPS cells: a stem cell made from a skin cell

Induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells are a newer way to make stem cells for medical research. Researchers found that putting four specific transcription factors into fully differentiated skin cells "reprograms" them: the factors switch on the set of genes that is normally active early in embryonic development, and the skin cells turn back into stem cells. (Induced = made to happen; the syllabus calls the result both "totipotent" and "pluripotent".) iPS cells seem to be equivalent to ES cells in most ways. Two big advantages:

  • The cells can come from the patient's own tissue. A transplant of cells derived from your own body is an autologous transplant (auto = self), and it is much less likely to be rejected by the immune system (the body's defence, which attacks foreign cells).
  • No human embryos are needed, which bypasses the ethical objections to ES cells.

The four transcription factors are usually brought into the skin cells with viral vectors: viruses used as delivery vehicles for genes (section 4 and page 4.4).

Exam link

Example exam question 10 (fill in two blanks): "___ can give rise to any type of cell whereas ___ can give rise to a subset of cell types." Answer, in the syllabus wording: totipotent (first blank) and pluripotent (second blank). The syllabus gives these as the properties of ES cells and of most adult stem cells respectively.

4. Viruses must be grown in living cells (4.2.6)

Viruses are useful twice over: studying them has revealed basic cell mechanisms, and they are a valuable way to bring genes into cells (viral vectors; that is how the iPS factors are delivered). Some viruses have a DNA genome, others (such as retroviruses, chapter 3) an RNA genome. But none of them is a free-living life form: a virus can only be propagated inside cells.

Most viruses infect only a few cell types (their host range, chapter 3). So to grow a virus you first need to be able to culture the right cell type. The syllabus gives the historical example: the most important step towards a vaccine against polio (a crippling disease) was learning to grow poliovirus in cultured human cells.

Harvesting the virus

Begin state: a culture of the right cells. Goal: pure virus particles. The virus infects the cells and uses their machinery to make many particles. Two ways to get them out:

  1. Harvest the virus-producing cells and break their membranes with detergents (soap-like molecules that dissolve membranes); then separate the particles from the cell debris.
  2. With lytic viruses (lysis = bursting: viruses that make the host cell burst open to get out), simply grow the infected cells until they burst and release the virus into the medium.

Counting viruses: the plaque assay (Figure 4-9)

An assay is a test that measures how much of something is present. To measure live virus:

  1. Grow a continuous layer of suitable cells on a plate (a "lawn" of cells).
  2. Add the sample that may contain the virus.
  3. Each virus particle infects one cell, makes many copies, bursts the cell, and the copies infect the neighbours. A growing zone of dead cells forms around the starting point.
  4. Stain the plate: live cells take up the dye (purple in the syllabus example); the zones of dead cells stay clear. Each clear circle is a plaque.
  5. Count the plaques: the number of plaques is proportional to the number of live viruses in the sample. The virus in one plaque can be picked up and purified: it is a pure strain, descended from one particle.

7. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

What is the difference between totipotent and pluripotent, according to the syllabus?
Totipotent cells can give rise to any cell type of the body (ES cells from early embryos). Pluripotent cells give rise to a restricted subset of cell lineages (most adult stem cells).
What happens when a stem cell divides?
An asymmetric division: one daughter remains a stem cell (self-renewal), the other differentiates into a particular cell type.
How do you prove that a cell is a stem cell?
Isolate it and implant it into a recipient animal; a stem cell gives rise to the whole set of differentiated cells there. This is the basis of bone marrow transplants.
Why does the body need hematopoiesis every day?
Blood cells have a limited lifespan, some only a day, so they must be continually replaced from hematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow.
What is a progenitor cell?
A forerunner cell produced by a stem cell that is already committed to differentiating into a group of cell types; it sits between the stem cell and the final cells in the hierarchy.
How could ES cells help in Parkinson's disease, and what is the risk?
Treated with growth factors, more than 99 % of ES cells become neurons that could replace the lost neurons. If not all cells differentiate, the leftover ES cells can keep growing and form a teratoma, a tumour of mixed cell types.
How are iPS cells made?
Four specific transcription factors are introduced (usually by viral vectors) into fully differentiated skin cells; they switch on the embryonic gene set and reprogram the cells into stem cells.
Why are iPS cells attractive for therapy?
They can be made from the patient's own cells, so the transplant is autologous and unlikely to be rejected, and no embryos are needed.
Why can a virus not be grown in a tube of nutrients?
A virus is not free-living: it has no machinery of its own and must be propagated inside cells it can infect.
What was the key advance for the polio vaccine?
Learning to grow poliovirus in cultured human cells.
Two ways to release virus particles from infected cells?
Break the cell membranes with detergents, or (for lytic viruses) let the cells burst by themselves and collect the medium.
What is a plaque and what does the number of plaques tell you?
A clear circle of dead cells in a stained lawn of living cells, started by one virus particle. The number of plaques is proportional to the number of live viruses in the sample.

8. To memorize

Learn these by heart
  • Totipotent = any cell type (ES cells); pluripotent = a subset (adult stem cells). Exam Q10 wording.
  • Stem cell = unlimited division + asymmetric division: one daughter stem cell (self-renewal), one differentiates.
  • Proof of a stem cell: transplant it; it rebuilds all cell types. Basis of bone marrow transplants.
  • Hematopoiesis: hematopoietic stem cells (bone marrow) → progenitors → red cells, platelets, white cells; blood cells live short (some 1 day).
  • ES cells + growth factors → >99 % neurons (Parkinson's hope); leftover ES cells → teratoma.
  • iPS cells: 4 transcription factors reprogram skin cells; delivered by viral vectors.

  • Viruses: not free-living → grown inside cultured cells of the right type (polio vaccine).

9. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. Fill in: "___ cells can give rise to any type of cell, whereas ___ cells can give rise to a subset of cell types."
    Answer

    Totipotent; pluripotent. In the syllabus, ES cells are totipotent and most adult stem cells pluripotent.

  2. Which cell is totipotent in the syllabus sense?
    • a skin cell
    • an embryonic stem cell
    • a red blood cell
    • a progenitor cell
    Answer

    b. ES cells, from early embryos, can give every cell type. A progenitor is already restricted; skin and red blood cells are differentiated.

  3. A stem cell divides. What are the two daughters?
    Answer

    One is a new stem cell (self-renewal) and the other differentiates into a particular cell type. This is asymmetric division.

  4. True or false: "Differentiated cells, like stem cells, divide without limit."
    Answer

    False. Differentiated cells do not divide or divide only a limited number of times. Unlimited division is what sets stem cells apart.

  5. Why is a bone marrow transplant a stem-cell transplant?
    Answer

    The marrow contains hematopoietic stem cells; transferred to the patient they self-renew and regenerate the whole set of blood cells, exactly the transplantation test for a stem cell.

  6. Put in order: progenitor cell, red blood cell, hematopoietic stem cell.
    Answer

    Hematopoietic stem cell → progenitor cell → red blood cell. The stem cell self-renews and makes progenitors, which differentiate into the final blood cell types.

  7. A batch of ES cells was treated with growth factors and 97 % became neurons. Why would a doctor hesitate to transplant them?
    Answer

    The remaining 3 % undifferentiated ES cells can keep growing and later differentiate at random into a teratoma. The syllabus says less than 100 % differentiation carries this risk.

  8. What is a teratoma?
    Answer

    A tumour that contains a mixture of many different differentiated cell types; it forms when ES cells are injected directly into a mouse.

  9. How do you turn a skin cell into a stem cell?
    Answer

    Introduce four specific transcription factors (usually with a viral vector). They switch on the early-embryo gene set and reprogram the skin cell into an iPS cell.

  10. Which is an advantage of iPS cells over ES cells?
    • they divide faster
    • they can be made from the patient's own cells, so the transplant is autologous and rarely rejected
    • they need no transcription factors
    • they never form tumours
    Answer

    b. Plus: no embryos are needed. (The syllabus does not claim iPS cells are tumour-free.)

  11. Why must you first be able to culture a specific cell type before you can grow a virus?
    Answer

    Viruses only multiply inside cells, and most viruses can infect only a few cell types. No suitable host cells in culture → no virus. Example: poliovirus in cultured human cells.

  12. A sample gives 40 plaques on a lawn of cells. What does that mean?
    Answer

    About 40 live, infectious virus particles were in the amount of sample added: each one infected a cell, spread to neighbours and left a clear zone of dead cells.

  13. How do you get a pure virus strain from a plaque assay?
    Answer

    Pick the virus from one plaque: all particles in it descend from the single particle that started it, so they are one strain.
