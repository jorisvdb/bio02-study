4.12 Finding a specific DNA sequence: restriction patterns, Southern blot, karyotypes, FISH and aCGH

In one sentence: to find out whether a particular piece of DNA is present, where it sits on a chromosome, or whether a region has been lost or doubled, you look at the chromosomes themselves (karyotype), light up one sequence on them with a fluorescent probe (FISH), or compare a patient's DNA with normal DNA spot by spot on a chip (aCGH).

Pages 4.9 to 4.11 read sequences letter by letter. Often the question is simpler and bigger: is this sequence there, how many copies, and where? This page is about those questions, from the coarsest view (a whole chromosome under the microscope) to the finest (copy number of small regions across the genome). These methods diagnose exactly the conditions of chapter 1 (trisomies, deletions, translocations) and chapter 2 (the rearranged genomes of cancer cells). FISH is named in the exam objectives.

1. Restriction patterns and Southern blot (4.10.1, 4.10.2: empty headings in the syllabus)

The syllabus lists these two sections but gives no text for them. What follows is the basic idea only, in a few lines each, so that the words mean something when you meet them (for example in the cheatsheet or in chapter 2). Marked not in the syllabus text: learn the idea, not details.

  • Restriction enzyme digestion pattern (not in the syllabus text): cut a DNA sample with a restriction enzyme (page 4.4), run the pieces on an agarose gel (page 4.7). Because the enzyme cuts only at its recognition sequence, the number and sizes of the bands are fixed by the sequence. Two DNA samples that give the same band pattern are (probably) the same; a mutation that creates or destroys a cut site changes the pattern. This is a cheap way to tell DNA molecules apart without sequencing them.
  • Southern blot (not in the syllabus text): after a gel, the DNA bands are transferred ("blotted") from the gel onto a membrane, where they stay in the same positions. A labelled probe is then added; it hybridises only to the band that contains the sequence of interest, and the label shows which band that is and how big the fragment is. Named after its inventor, Edwin Southern; the Western blot for proteins (page 4.13) is a pun on that name.

Southern blot = de banden van een gel overbrengen op een membraan en met een gelabelde probe de band met de gezochte sequentie zichtbaar maken.

2. Karyotype analysis: looking at whole chromosomes (4.10.3, Figure 4-43)

Begin state: dividing cells (for example white blood cells, or cells cultured from a sample). Goal: see every chromosome and count and inspect them.

  1. Catch the cells in metaphase, when the chromosomes are condensed and visible.
  2. Stain them with Giemsa stain, a dye that binds more strongly to gene-poor, A/T-rich regions. The result is a pattern of dark and light bands that is characteristic for each chromosome (chromosome 1 always has the same bands, so it can be recognised).
  3. Photograph the chromosomes under the microscope and sort them in pairs by size and banding pattern: this picture is the karyotype.
  4. Inspect: are there extra or missing chromosomes (aneuploidy), gross deletions, or rearrangements such as translocations?

Syllabus Figure 4-43 shows the karyotype of a human stem cell with one X, one Y, two copies of every other chromosome, except chromosomes 12 and 17, present three times (trisomy 12 and 17; cultured cell lines often pick up such changes, page 4.2).

Where it is used

  • Medical diagnosis: aneuploidy and other chromosome aberrations (deletions, translocations) are frequent causes of birth defects, intellectual disability, miscarriages and other syndromes (chapter 1).
  • Prenatal screening (before birth): in amniocentesis (vruchtwaterpunctie), fluid from the sac around the fetus is collected; it contains cells shed by the fetus. The cells are cultured and karyotyped, for example to detect trisomy 21.
  • Cancer and other diseases with rearrangements: for example the Philadelphia chromosome (the shortened chromosome 22 of chronic myelogenous leukemia, chapters 1 and 2) is visible in a karyotype.
Resolution

A karyotype shows only gross changes: whole chromosomes, large deletions, big rearrangements. A missing gene or a point mutation is far too small to see. For smaller targets you need a probe (FISH) or a chip (aCGH).

Samenvatting

Gebruik: diagnose van syndromen, prenatale screening via vruchtwaterpunctie (foetale cellen kweken en karyotyperen), kankers met herschikkingen (Philadelphia-chromosoom).

3. FISH: lighting up one sequence on the chromosomes (4.10.4, Figure 4-44)

FISH = fluorescence in situ hybridisation: a fluorescent probe hybridises "in place", on the chromosomes of a cell fixed on a slide. Begin state: cells, and a probe for a known sequence. Goal: see where that sequence is on the chromosomes, and whether it has moved.

  1. Fix the cells on a glass slide (fixing = treating them so that they stay in place and keep their structure).
  2. Permeabilise them (make holes in the membranes) so that the probe can get in, and denature the chromosomal DNA so that the probe can pair.
  3. Add the fluorescent DNA probe; it hybridises to its complementary sequence on the chromosome. With more than one probe, each gets a dye of a different colour.
  4. Stain all the DNA with DAPI, a dye that binds DNA and fluoresces blue, so the chromosomes themselves are visible.
  5. Look in a fluorescence microscope: blue chromosomes with coloured spots where the probes sit.

Reading the picture: the translocation example

Syllabus Figure 4-44 uses two probes that flank (lie on either side of) a known breakpoint: one probe for a region on chromosome 4 (say green), one for a region on chromosome 11 (say red).

  • Healthy cell: each probe gives two spots, one on each of the two homologous chromosomes: two green spots far from two red spots.
  • Leukaemia cell with a reciprocal translocation between one chromosome 4 and one chromosome 11: the unaffected chromosomes still give one green and one red spot, but on the two rearranged chromosomes the regions are now next to each other, so green and red overlap and appear as a merged yellow signal.

Uses: locating a gene or sequence on a chromosome; diagnosing genetic diseases and cancers with known chromosome abnormalities (translocations, specific deletions or amplifications); it can also detect specific RNA transcripts in cells (page 4.13).

Limits: you must know in advance which region to look at, to make the probe, and FISH is not sensitive enough for small deletions or amplifications.

Samenvatting

Gezonde cel: per probe twee stippen (twee homologen). Translocatie 4;11: op de herschikte chromosomen komen groen en rood naast elkaar → gele samengesmolten stip. Beperkingen: je moet vooraf weten welke regio, en kleine deleties of amplificaties zie je niet.

4. Array CGH: copy number across the whole genome (4.10.5, Figure 4-45)

aCGH = array comparative genomic hybridisation: "comparative" because a patient's DNA is compared with normal DNA, "array" because the comparison happens on a microarray with fragments from the whole genome. It is quantitative: it measures how much of each region there is. Begin state: DNA from the patient's cells and DNA from normal cells. Goal: find every region that is deleted or amplified, genome-wide, without knowing in advance where to look.

  1. Label the test DNA (patient) with one fluorophore, for example fluorescein, which emits green light, and the reference DNA (normal) with another, for example rhodamine, which emits red. (Which colour is which is a choice; the syllabus text and figure swap them, so learn the logic, not the colours.)
  2. Mix the two labelled DNAs and co-hybridise them to a DNA microarray: a chip with DNA fragments from the entire genome spotted in rows and columns. At every spot, patient and reference fragments compete to pair.
  3. Scan the array with a fluorescence scanner and compare the two colours at each spot:
    • same copy number in patient and reference → equal amounts of green and red → the spot looks yellow
    • deletion in the patient → less patient DNA → the reference colour dominates (red, with the labelling above)
    • duplication / amplification in the patient → more patient DNA → the patient colour dominates (green)
  4. The scanner plots the ratio of the two intensities for every spot along each chromosome: a flat line at "equal", with steps up (gain) and down (loss).

Syllabus Figure 4-45 (a study on canine, that is dog, cancer): the tested cell line had an extra chromosome 10 and only one copy of chromosomes 15 and 22. The method also uncovers deletions and amplifications of short chromosomal regions, which FISH and karyotypes miss.

Samenvatting

aCGH (array comparative genomic hybridisation): patiënt-DNA en normaal referentie-DNA met twee kleuren labelen (bv. fluoresceïne groen, rhodamine rood), mengen en samen hybridiseren op een microarray met fragmenten van het hele genoom. Per stip: gelijk → geel; deletie bij de patiënt → de referentiekleur wint; duplicatie → de patiëntkleur wint. De scanner zet de verhouding uit per chromosoom. Genoombreed, kwantitatief, geen voorkennis nodig, ook korte stukken. Voorbeeld: hondenkanker-cellijn met een extra chromosoom 10 en één kopie van 15 en 22.

Learning objective: "... nucleic acid based technologies, including PCR, FISH, microarrays, sequencing technologies ..." and "utilizes fundamental concepts ... to solve problems".

8. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

What is a karyotype and how is it obtained?
The complete chromosome set of a cell, seen as a picture. Metaphase cells are stained with Giemsa, which gives each chromosome a characteristic band pattern; the chromosomes are photographed and sorted in pairs by size and banding.
Why do the Giemsa bands appear?
Giemsa binds more strongly to gene-poor, A/T-rich regions, so those regions stain dark and gene-rich regions stay light.
What abnormalities can a karyotype reveal?
Extra or missing chromosomes (aneuploidy, such as trisomy 21), gross deletions and rearrangements such as translocations (the Philadelphia chromosome). Not small deletions or single-gene changes.
What is amniocentesis and what is it used for?
Collecting fluid from the sac around the fetus; it contains shed fetal cells, which are cultured and karyotyped. It is a prenatal screening method for chromosome abnormalities.
What does FISH stand for, and what is "in situ"?
Fluorescence in situ hybridisation. In situ means "in place": the fluorescent probe pairs with its sequence on the chromosomes themselves, fixed on a slide, so you see where the sequence lies.
List the steps of FISH.
Fix the cells on a slide; permeabilise them; hybridise with a fluorescent DNA probe (or several, each in its own colour); stain all DNA blue with DAPI; look in a fluorescence microscope.
In the leukaemia example, why does a yellow spot appear?
The two probes flank the breakpoint on chromosomes 4 and 11. After the reciprocal translocation the two regions lie next to each other on the rearranged chromosomes, so the green and red signals overlap and merge to yellow. The unaffected homologs still give separate green and red spots.
What are the two limitations of FISH?
You must know in advance which region to probe, and it is not sensitive enough to detect small deletions or amplifications.
How does aCGH work?
Patient DNA and reference DNA are labelled with different fluorophores (for example fluorescein green and rhodamine red), mixed and co-hybridised to a microarray carrying fragments from the whole genome. At each spot the colour ratio shows whether the patient has the same (yellow), less (deletion) or more (duplication) of that region; the ratios are plotted along the chromosomes.
Why is a spot yellow when the copy number is normal?
Because equal amounts of green-labelled and red-labelled DNA hybridise to it, and equal red and green fluorescence looks yellow.
What did aCGH show in the canine cancer example?
An extra chromosome 10 (gain) and only one copy of chromosomes 15 and 22 (loss).
What are the advantages of aCGH over FISH?
It is genome-wide, quantitative and needs no prior knowledge of where to look; it detects deletions and amplifications of short regions that FISH misses.
What is the idea of a Southern blot?
DNA fragments separated on a gel are transferred to a membrane; a labelled probe then hybridises only to the fragment containing the sequence of interest, showing its presence and size. (Idea only; the syllabus heading has no text.)

9. To memorize

Learn these by heart
  • Karyotype: metaphase cells, Giemsa banding (dark = gene-poor, A/T-rich), sort by size and bands.
  • Karyotype sees: aneuploidy, big deletions, translocations; not small changes.
  • Uses: birth defects, intellectual disability, miscarriages; prenatal screening by amniocentesis; cancers (Philadelphia chromosome).
  • FISH = fluorescence in situ hybridisation: fix → permeabilise → fluorescent probe → DAPI (blue) → microscope.
  • Healthy: 2 spots per probe. Translocation 4;11: flanking probes come together → merged yellow signal.
  • FISH limits: needs a known region; misses small deletions/amplifications.
  • aCGH: patient DNA + reference DNA, two colours, co-hybridised on a genome-wide array.
  • aCGH colours: equal = yellow; deletion = reference colour; duplication = patient colour; plotted per chromosome.
  • aCGH is genome-wide, quantitative, no prior knowledge, short regions; dog example: +10, −15, −22.
  • 4.10.1 and 4.10.2 are empty in the syllabus: restriction pattern = band pattern after cutting; Southern = gel → membrane → probe.
  • Choose: number/structure → karyotype; where/joined → FISH; gains and losses genome-wide → aCGH; letters → sequencing.

10. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. Why are cells in metaphase used for a karyotype?
    Answer

    In metaphase the chromosomes are maximally condensed and separate, so each one is visible as its own rod. In interphase the DNA is loose chromatin and individual chromosomes cannot be seen.

  2. What do the dark Giemsa bands correspond to?
    • gene-rich, G/C-rich regions
    • gene-poor, A/T-rich regions
    • the centromeres only
    • methylated regions
    Answer

    b. Giemsa binds gene-poor, A/T-rich DNA more strongly. Because every chromosome has its own distribution of such regions, the band pattern identifies it.

  3. A karyotype of a fetus shows 47 chromosomes with three copies of chromosome 21. How were the cells obtained, and what is the diagnosis?
    Answer

    By amniocentesis: fetal cells from the fluid around the fetus were cultured and karyotyped. The diagnosis is trisomy 21, Down syndrome (chapter 1).

  4. True or false: "A karyotype can detect a deletion of a single gene."
    Answer

    False. A single gene is far below the resolution of a stained chromosome. A karyotype shows gross changes only; small deletions need aCGH (or sequencing).

  5. What is the role of DAPI in FISH?
    Answer

    DAPI binds all DNA and fluoresces blue, so the chromosomes themselves are visible as a background on which the coloured probe spots can be placed.

  6. In a FISH experiment with one probe on a healthy diploid cell, how many spots do you expect, and why?
    Answer

    Two: one on each of the two homologous chromosomes that carry the probed region. (After S phase each may show as a doubled spot, but the number of chromosomes is still two.)

  7. Two probes, green and red, normally give separate spots. In a patient's cells one green and one red spot overlap as yellow. What happened?
    Answer

    A translocation brought the two probed regions next to each other on a rearranged chromosome, so their signals merge. The remaining separate green and red spots come from the unaffected homologs.

  8. Why can FISH not be used to screen a patient for "any deletion anywhere"?
    Answer

    Because a FISH probe only looks at the region it was made for, so you must know in advance where to look, and FISH is not sensitive to small deletions. For a genome-wide, unbiased search you use aCGH.

  9. In aCGH the patient DNA is green and the reference DNA red. A spot is bright green. What does it mean?
    • the region is deleted in the patient
    • the region is duplicated or amplified in the patient
    • the copy number is normal
    • the probe failed
    Answer

    b. More patient DNA than reference DNA hybridised to that spot, so the patient's colour dominates: the patient has extra copies of that region. Red would mean a deletion, yellow equal amounts.

  10. Why are the two DNAs hybridised to the same array at the same time, rather than to two arrays?
    Answer

    So that they compete for the same spots under identical conditions; the colour ratio at each spot then directly reflects the ratio of copy numbers, independent of how good each individual spot is.

  11. Name three advantages of aCGH over FISH.
    Answer

    Genome-wide (all regions at once), quantitative (how much more or less), no prior knowledge needed, and it detects short deletions and amplifications that FISH cannot.

  12. Which technique would you choose: (1) to check whether a leukaemia patient's cells carry the known 9;22 translocation; (2) to find unknown copy-number changes in a tumour; (3) to count a fetus's chromosomes?
    Answer

    (1) FISH with probes flanking the known breakpoint (a karyotype also shows the short chromosome 22). (2) aCGH, because the changes are unknown and may be small. (3) a karyotype after amniocentesis.

  13. What is the difference between a Southern blot and a Western blot?
    Answer

    A Southern blot detects a DNA fragment with a labelled DNA probe after transfer from a gel to a membrane. A Western blot (page 4.13) detects a protein with an antibody after the same kind of transfer. The names are a play on the inventor's name, Southern.
