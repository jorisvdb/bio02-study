4.4 DNA cloning: cutting a piece of DNA out and growing it in bacteria

In one sentence: to clone a piece of DNA you cut it and a carrier molecule (a plasmid) with the same restriction enzyme, glue them together with DNA ligase, put the result into bacteria, and let only the bacteria that took it up grow on a plate with an antibiotic.

PCR (page 4.3) gives you many copies of a fragment in a tube. Cloning goes one step further: the fragment is built into a DNA molecule that living cells copy for you, generation after generation, and that you can modify at will. This is the heart of recombinant DNA technology, the set of methods behind transgenic organisms, knockout mice (page 4.6) and insulin made by bacteria. Exam questions 11 and 14 come from this page.

1. What cloning is, and the vector that carries the DNA (4.4, 4.4.1)

DNA cloning = isolating a specific DNA region and propagating it (making many copies by letting cells multiply). The word "clone" means "identical copies from one original". Thanks to cloning, scientists can change the sequence and the expression of any gene.

Begin state: a DNA fragment you are interested in (the insert). Goal: that fragment inside a DNA molecule that bacteria copy independently of their own chromosome. The first step is to put the fragment into a vector (vector = carrier). Because the final molecule contains DNA from two sources (the insert and the vector), it is recombinant DNA (recombined = put together in a new combination), and the methods are recombinant DNA techniques.

Vectors: plasmids and viruses

A vector can be a plasmid (circular, double-stranded, replicates on its own) or a virus. A useful plasmid vector (Figure 4-13 shows the classic pBR322) has three kinds of parts:

  1. an origin of replication that the host's copying machinery recognises, so the plasmid is copied in the host (for E. coli: an E. coli origin);
  2. a selectable marker: a gene that lets only cells with the plasmid grow under chosen conditions. Usually an antibiotic resistance gene (pBR322 has two: resistance to ampicillin and to tetracycline); alternatively a gene for a metabolic enzyme that lets the cell grow on a medium that would otherwise not feed it;
  3. several unique restriction sites: places where a given restriction enzyme (section 2) cuts the plasmid exactly once, so it can be opened to insert DNA.

Host and insert size

E. coli is the organism of choice for most routine cloning (yeast and cultured cells are also used). Different vectors take inserts of different sizes: a typical bacterial plasmid up to about 15 kb (kb = kilobase, a thousand base pairs), while a special vector called a bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) can carry hundreds of kb.

What you can do once a gene is cloned

A cloned fragment is far easier to work with than the same sequence sitting in its native chromosome:

  • Complementation test: put the cloned gene into a cell in which that gene is mutated or deleted; if the cell's lost activity is restored, the cloned gene "complements" the mutation, which proves which gene was broken.
  • Fuse it to a reporter gene, for example the gene for green fluorescent protein (GFP, a jellyfish protein that glows green under blue light), or to a short peptide tag that makes the protein easy to detect or to purify.
  • Put it behind a strong promoter to make the cell produce large amounts of the protein, which makes purification easier.

Two ways to build the recombinant molecule

Usually the insert is first amplified by PCR. Then there are two strategies:

  • Ligation: cut both ends of the insert with restriction enzymes and glue it into the vector with DNA ligase (sections 2 and 3).
  • Recombination: let recombination enzymes insert the fragment into the vector by sequence homology (the fragment's ends match sequences in the vector, and the enzymes swap it in). The syllabus only names this route.

2. Restriction enzymes: the scissors (4.4.2, Figure 4-14)

Restriction enzymes (restrictie-enzymen) are proteins from bacteria that recognise a specific short DNA sequence and cut the double-stranded DNA there. Bacteria use them as a defence against foreign DNA (for example from a virus): they chop it up. Scientists adopted them as tools; they were among the first tools for manipulating DNA.

  • The recognised sequence is a restriction site, typically 4 to 8 bp long and palindromic. A palindrome in language reads the same backwards ("level"). In DNA it means: the sequence on one strand, read 5′ → 3′, is the same as the sequence on the other strand, read 5′ → 3′. Example: 5′-GAATTC-3′ on the top strand has 3′-CTTAAG-5′ underneath, which read 5′ → 3′ is again GAATTC.
  • There are hundreds of restriction enzymes, each with its own site and its own cutting position.
  • The cut can leave blunt ends (both strands cut at the same place: a straight cut) or overhangs (the two strands cut a few bases apart, leaving a short single-stranded tail at each end; a 5′ overhang or a 3′ overhang). Overhangs are nicknamed sticky ends, because two matching overhangs can base-pair and stick together.

The classic example is EcoRI (an enzyme from E. coli): it recognises GAATTC and cuts after the G on both strands. Because the site is a palindrome, the two overhangs it makes (AATT) are complementary to each other: any two EcoRI ends can pair up again.

Restriction enzymes are also used for diagnostics: a mutation that creates or destroys a site changes the pattern of fragments (page 4.12).

3. Cloning by ligation, step by step (4.4.3, Figure 4-15)

Begin state: the insert (cut out of larger DNA with a restriction enzyme, or more often made by PCR with primers that carry the restriction site built into their ends, so the PCR product has the site at both ends) and the circular vector. Goal: bacteria that carry and copy vector + insert.

  1. Cut the insert with the chosen restriction enzyme(s). This usually gives single-stranded overhangs.
  2. Cut the vector with the same enzyme(s), so its overhangs are complementary to those of the insert. The circle is now open.
  3. Anneal: mix insert and vector; the compatible sticky ends base-pair with each other. This holds the insert in place but the backbones are still broken (nicks).
  4. Ligate: DNA ligase covalently joins the 5′ and 3′ ends, sealing the backbone: one closed circular recombinant plasmid.
  5. Transform: introduce the plasmid into bacteria. Cells do not take up DNA easily; they must first be made competent (able to take up DNA) by treating them with salts or exposing them to an electric field. The uptake is called transformation.
  6. Select: spread the cells on a plate with the antibiotic. Only cells that received the plasmid, and with it the resistance gene, survive and grow into colonies. Each colony is a clone carrying the recombinant plasmid.

Question 14 (which is a transgenic organism): a rat with rabbit insulin genes. Transgenic = carrying a gene introduced from elsewhere into its genome (page 4.5). Not transgenic: a bacterium treated with a chemical that changes gene expression (no new gene); a fern grown from one root cell (a clone, same genes); a human treated with insulin that E. coli made (the human's genome is unchanged; the bacterium that got the human insulin gene was the transgenic one).

6. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

What is DNA cloning?
Isolating a specific DNA fragment and propagating it: putting it into a vector that cells copy, so you get many identical copies and can modify it.
Why is the product called recombinant DNA?
Because the final molecule combines DNA from two sources: the insert and the vector.
What three features must a plasmid vector have?
An origin of replication recognised by the host, a selectable marker (usually an antibiotic resistance gene), and unique restriction sites to open it for the insert.
What is a selectable marker for?
To let only cells that carry the vector grow: on a plate with the antibiotic, cells without the resistance gene (so without the plasmid) die.
How much DNA fits in a plasmid, and in a BAC?
A typical bacterial plasmid takes inserts up to about 15 kb; a bacterial artificial chromosome takes hundreds of kb.
What is a restriction enzyme and what do bacteria use it for?
A bacterial protein that recognises a specific palindromic sequence of 4–8 bp and cuts the DNA there. Bacteria use it to destroy foreign DNA, such as viral DNA.
What does "palindromic" mean for a restriction site?
Reading each strand 5′ → 3′ gives the same sequence. GAATTC on the top strand has CTTAAG beneath it, which read 5′ → 3′ is GAATTC again.
What are sticky ends and why are they useful?
Short single-stranded overhangs left when the two strands are cut a few bases apart. Two ends made by the same enzyme have complementary overhangs, so they base-pair and hold insert and vector together before ligation.
Why must insert and vector be cut with the same enzyme?
So that their overhangs are complementary and can anneal. Ends made by different enzymes generally do not match.
What does DNA ligase do in cloning?
It covalently joins the adjacent 5′ and 3′ ends of insert and vector, sealing the backbone into one closed recombinant plasmid. It does not cut.
What is transformation and what are competent cells?
Transformation is a bacterium taking up a plasmid from its surroundings. Competent cells have been treated with salts or an electric field so that they can take DNA up.
How do you find the bacteria that received the plasmid?
Plate them on medium with the antibiotic; only the cells with the plasmid (and its resistance gene) grow into colonies.
Which of these is transgenic: a rat with rabbit insulin genes, or a human treated with insulin made by E. coli?
The rat: its genome carries a gene from another species. The treated human's genome is unchanged; the transgenic organism in that story is the bacterium carrying the human insulin gene.

7. To memorize

Learn these by heart
  • Cloning = insert into a vector (plasmid or virus) → recombinant DNA → copied by cells (usually E. coli).
  • Vector needs: origin of replication, selectable marker (antibiotic resistance), unique restriction sites. Example: pBR322 (amp, tet).
  • Plasmid insert up to 15 kb; BAC hundreds of kb.
  • Uses: complementation, fusion to GFP or a tag, strong promoter for lots of protein.
  • Restriction enzymes (bacterial defence) cut at palindromic sites of 4–8 bp; blunt ends or sticky overhangs.
  • EcoRI: GAATTC, cuts after G → AATT overhangs that pair with each other.
  • Steps: cut insert → cut vector (same enzyme) → anneal → ligate → transform → select.
  • Restriction enzyme cuts, DNA ligase joins (exam Q11).
  • Competent cells (salts or electric field) take up DNA = transformation.
  • Selection: antibiotic plate, only plasmid-carrying cells grow; each colony = one clone.
  • Transgenic = carries a gene from another organism (rat with rabbit insulin gene, exam Q14).

8. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. Which tool is incorrectly paired with its use?
    • restriction enzyme – cutting DNA at a specific site
    • DNA ligase – creating sticky ends
    • DNA polymerase – PCR
    • reverse transcriptase – cDNA from mRNA
    Answer

    b. Sticky ends are made by restriction enzymes; ligase joins DNA ends. (This is exam question 11.)

  2. Name the three parts every plasmid vector needs.
    Answer

    An origin of replication (so the host copies it), a selectable marker (antibiotic resistance gene, so plasmid-carrying cells can be picked out) and unique restriction sites (to open it in one place for the insert).

  3. A plasmid is cut with EcoRI and gives one linear piece. What does that tell you?
    Answer

    The plasmid has exactly one EcoRI site: one cut opens the circle into one linear molecule. That is what "unique restriction site" means and why it is useful for inserting DNA.

  4. Why is GAATTC called a palindrome?
    Answer

    Its partner strand is CTTAAG, and read in the 5′ → 3′ direction that is again GAATTC: both strands read the same. Consequence: the overhangs EcoRI makes are complementary to each other.

  5. EcoRI cuts GAATTC after the G on each strand. What does each end look like?
    Answer

    A 5′ overhang with the single-stranded sequence AATT: a sticky end. Any two EcoRI ends can anneal.

  6. Blunt end or sticky end: what is the difference?
    Answer

    Blunt: both strands cut at the same position, no tail. Sticky: the strands are cut a few bases apart, leaving a short single-stranded tail that can base-pair with a matching tail.

  7. Put the cloning steps in order: transform, cut vector, ligate, select, cut insert, anneal.
    Answer

    Cut insert → cut vector (same enzyme) → anneal → ligate → transform → select. Cut, pair, seal, put in, pick out.

  8. You cut the insert with EcoRI and the vector with BamHI. What happens at the annealing step?
    Answer

    Nothing useful: the overhangs come from different enzymes and are not complementary, so insert and vector do not pair. Use the same enzyme for both.

  9. How does a PCR product get restriction sites at its ends?
    Answer

    The primers are designed with the restriction site built into their 5′ ends, so the amplified fragment carries a site at both ends and can be cut like any other DNA.

  10. What does DNA ligase need to have happened first, and what does it do?
    Answer

    The sticky ends of insert and vector must already be annealed (base-paired), which leaves nicks in the backbone. Ligase then covalently joins the 5′ and 3′ ends and closes the circle.

  11. What is a competent cell?
    • a cell that can divide
    • a cell treated with salts or an electric field so it can take up DNA
    • a cell that carries a resistance gene
    • a cell that makes restriction enzymes
    Answer

    b. Bacteria normally do not take up DNA easily; the treatment makes them competent for transformation.

  12. After transformation you plate the cells on medium with ampicillin. What do the colonies that appear have in common?
    Answer

    They all carry the plasmid with the ampicillin resistance gene (the selectable marker); cells without the plasmid were killed. Each colony is a clone of one transformed cell.

  13. Which of these is a transgenic organism?
    • a bacterium treated with a compound that affects many of its genes
    • a fern grown from a single root cell
    • a human treated with insulin produced by E. coli
    • a rat with rabbit insulin genes
    Answer

    d. Only the rat carries a gene introduced from another species in its genome. The treated bacterium has no new gene, the fern is a clone with its own genes, and the human's genome is unchanged (exam question 14).

  14. Why put a cloned gene behind a strong promoter?
    Answer

    To make the host transcribe it a lot, so the cell produces large amounts of the protein, which makes purifying it much easier.
