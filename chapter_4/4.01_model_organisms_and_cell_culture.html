4.1 Model organisms and cell culture: where the experiments are done

In one sentence: molecular biologists study a few easy-to-grow organisms and cells grown in a dish, because what they find there (how DNA is copied, read, repaired) is the same in almost every living thing, including us.

Chapter 4 is a toolbox chapter: it describes the techniques behind everything in BIO01 and in chapters 1 to 3 of this course. Six of the fifteen example exam questions come from this chapter, so it is worth learning well. Before any technique can be used, you need material: organisms or cells that grow reliably in the laboratory. That is what this first page is about. The pages after it cover the techniques themselves: copying DNA (PCR), cutting and pasting it (cloning), changing genomes (CRISPR), detecting molecules, reading sequences, and so on.

0. Two ideas that run through the whole chapter

TermMeaning
in vivoLatin for "in the living": an experiment inside a living cell or organism
in vitroLatin for "in glass": an experiment in a test tube, with purified components (molecules taken out of cells and cleaned up). You rebuild a cell process, for example making mRNA, with only the parts you add. That tells you the minimal requirements: the smallest set of molecules that is enough. In vitro work is limited (a test tube is not a cell) but it is the best way to measure things precisely and to find the core players.

The chapter's learning objectives: describe the principle, method and applications of nucleic-acid techniques (PCR, FISH, microarrays, sequencing, metagenomics, transcriptomics) and of protein and metabolite techniques (mass spectrometry, chromatography), and use them to solve problems. Every technique page therefore gives you: the principle, what you need, the steps in order, what it is used for, and one trap.

1. Model organisms: a few species for all of biology (4.1)

Millions of species exist, but only a few dozen are used in most research. They are called model organisms (modelorganismen). They are not chosen because they are economically important or cause disease, but because they are easy to grow and still show the general molecular processes, which can then be extrapolated (carried over) to other organisms, including humans and their diseases. That works because so many molecules and processes are conserved by evolution. The syllabus quotes the biologist Jacques Monod: "What is true for E. coli is true for the elephant."

What makes a good model organism

  • simple, well-defined nutritional requirements (you know exactly what to feed it) and a known life cycle, so it grows safely in large amounts;
  • a short generation time (time from one generation to the next: minutes for a bacterium, weeks for a mouse) and a small size;
  • stocks can be stored for years (frozen, or as seeds);
  • genetic analysis is possible: mutations that reduce or change a gene's function can be made, kept, found and characterised;
  • modified genes can be introduced into the organism (recombinant DNA technology, page 4.4), so the effect of any mutation can be tested;
  • its genome is sequenced, and the research community keeps databases: genome sequences, mutant strains and their phenotypes, gene expression, enzyme activities, protein–protein interactions.

One warning from the syllabus: the naming rules for genes and proteins differ from organism to organism (nomenclature = the system of names). The same gene can be written in capitals, italics or lower case depending on the species.

How to choose: it depends on the question and on the tools available. Phenomena unique to eukaryotes must be studied in a eukaryote (yeast for the nucleus); how a body is built from an egg needs a higher eukaryote (fly, worm); something unique to mammals needs a mammal (mouse). The syllabus says model organisms were covered in chapter 1 (of the lecturer's slides) and refers you to those slides for the details.

The logic in one line

Conserved processes + an organism that is cheap, fast and genetically tractable (easy to manipulate) = a model. Study the process in the model, then carry the answer over to humans.

2. Growing single cells: liquid culture and agar plates (4.2, 4.2.1)

Biochemistry needs isolated molecules, ideally from a homogeneous population of cells (all the same), so that every copy of a protein or DNA is identical. You can take proteins from a large uniform organ (a cow's heart), but most foundational experiments used one cell type grown in culture: cells kept alive and multiplying outside the body, in a flask or on a plate. This is called cell culture (celkweek). Growing cells either float in liquid (suspension) or sit on a solid surface.

Single-celled organisms are the easiest. E. coli and yeast grow in two ways (Figure 4-4):

  • Liquid culture: a liquid medium (a mixture of nutrients) buffered at the right pH (a buffer keeps the acidity constant). As the cells multiply the liquid turns turbid (cloudy, troebel): about 10⁸ yeast cells per millilitre, read "ten to the eighth" = 100 million.
  • Solid medium: the same nutrients with agar added. Agar is a polysaccharide (a long sugar chain, BIO01 chapter 2) from algae (seaweed) that is liquid when hot and sets into a gel when cool, like jelly. The cells are spread on the agar surface at low density (few cells, far apart). Each cell grows and divides on the spot, and its millions of descendants form a visible dot: a colony (kolonie). One colony = the offspring of one cell = genetically identical.

Why culture is useful: the conditions are under your control. You can bubble air through the liquid or shake it (more oxygen), grow the cells with no oxygen at all, change the temperature, or change the food source. Bacteria, algae, fungi and some archaea can all be grown this way.

The limit: many organisms (those from unusual environments, even some soil organisms) cannot be grown in the laboratory, because nobody knows which conditions they need. So our biochemical knowledge is biased towards culturable organisms. Information about the non-culturable ones now comes from sequencing their genomes directly (metagenomics, page 4.11).

Eencelligen (E. coli, gist) groeien in vloeibaar medium (gebufferde voedingsoplossing, wordt troebel, ~10⁸ cellen/ml) of op een agarplaat (agar = gel uit zeewier). 3. Cells from a multicellular organism: primary cells and cell lines (4.2.2)

Cells from an animal can also be cultured, but it takes more care. You start with a piece of tissue and dissociate it (break it up into single cells). These freshly isolated cells are primary cells. They are grown in a plastic flask or plate (Figure 4-5) under conditions that let them adhere (stick) to the surface; some cell types, such as blood cells, can be grown floating in liquid for a short time. The medium is often pink because of a pH indicator dye (a dye whose colour shows the acidity).

Why primary cells stop growing

  1. Contact inhibition (contactinhibitie): when cells on a solid surface touch each other, the contact triggers a cell-cycle arrest (they stop dividing; cell cycle = BIO01 chapter 7). So they form one layer, a monolayer, and then stop.
  2. A limited number of divisions: most primary cells can divide only a fixed number of times. The main reason is telomere shortening. Reminder from BIO01 chapter 4: a telomere is the repeated DNA at the end of a chromosome; it gets a little shorter at every division, because DNA polymerase cannot copy the very end. When the telomeres become too short the cell enters senescence (senescentie, "ageing": a permanent stop of division). The enzyme telomerase can rebuild telomeres, but most normal body cells do not make it.

Cell lines: cells that never stop

To avoid these limits, researchers often use special strains of cells that bypass senescence: cell lines (cellijnen). They are usually derived from tumours; such cells actively express telomerase and have other genetic changes that remove the normal brakes on division (chapter 2 explains why cancer cells behave like this). Cells can also be made to grow continuously by infecting them with a cancer-causing virus. The syllabus (Figure 4-6) lists commonly used cell lines; they have been central to the study of eukaryotic cells.

Caution with cell lines (the syllabus insists on this)
  • The very changes that let them grow forever also make them different from cells in a real tissue.
  • While they are kept in culture their genomes acquire new mutations or rearrangements.
  • Cell lines have been mixed up when passed between laboratories, so the cells you receive may not be what the label says.

So: be vigilant about which cells you have, and remember that results may not hold for intact tissue.

Ze stoppen door contactinhibitie (cellen raken elkaar → celcyclus stopt) en na een beperkt aantal delingen door telomeerverkorting → senescentie (geen telomerase). Let op: ze verschillen van echt weefsel, krijgen nieuwe mutaties en worden soms verwisseld.

6. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

What does "in vitro" mean, and what is the point of such experiments?
In glass: a process rebuilt in a test tube from purified components. It shows the minimal set of molecules needed and allows precise, quantitative measurements. Its limit: a tube is not a cell.
Why can a result from a bacterium tell us something about humans?
Because core molecular processes are conserved: they were inherited from a common ancestor and have changed little. "What is true for E. coli is true for the elephant."
Name five properties of a good model organism.
Simple, defined nutritional needs and a known life cycle; short generation time; small size; stocks can be stored; genetic analysis and introduction of modified genes are possible; a sequenced genome with community databases.
Which model organism would you choose to study (a) DNA replication, (b) the nucleus, (c) embryonic development, (d) a human disease of mammals?
(a) E. coli, a bacterium; (b) yeast, a single-celled eukaryote; (c) the fruit fly or the roundworm C. elegans; (d) the mouse.
What is a colony on an agar plate?
A visible spot of millions of cells that all descend from one cell that landed there, so they are genetically identical.
Why is our knowledge of biochemistry biased?
Because it comes mostly from organisms that can be grown in culture. Many organisms cannot be cultured; for those, information now comes from sequencing their genomes.
What are primary cells and why do they stop dividing?
Cells freshly dissociated from a tissue. They stop through contact inhibition (touching cells arrest the cell cycle, giving a monolayer) and through telomere shortening, which after a limited number of divisions causes senescence.
What is a cell line and how does it escape senescence?
A strain of cells that divides indefinitely. Usually from a tumour: it expresses telomerase and has lost the normal brakes on division. Cells can also be immortalised by infection with a cancer-causing virus.
Give three reasons for caution when using cell lines.
They differ in fundamental ways from cells in tissue; their genomes acquire mutations and rearrangements in culture; cell lines have been mixed up between laboratories.
Why is the medium in a flask of mammalian cells pink?
It contains a pH indicator dye; the colour shows whether the acidity is still right.

7. To memorize

Learn these by heart
  • In vivo = in the living organism; in vitro = in a tube with purified components → minimal requirements.
  • Model organisms work because processes are conserved: "what is true for E. coli is true for the elephant".
  • Good model: easy to feed, short generation time, small, storable, genetics possible, genome sequenced, databases.
  • E. coli bacterium (replication, transcription, translation; cloning host) · yeast (nucleus) · C. elegans worm and Drosophila fly (development) · Arabidopsis (plant) · mouse (mammal).
  • Gene naming (nomenclature) differs per organism.
  • Liquid culture: buffered broth, turbid, ~10⁸ cells/ml. Agar plate: one cell → one colony.
  • Many organisms are not culturable → known only from genome sequencing.
  • Primary cells stop by contact inhibition (monolayer) and telomere shortening → senescence.
  • Cell lines: from tumours, telomerase on, divide forever; or immortalised by a cancer virus.
  • Cell-line cautions: differ from tissue, gain mutations, get mixed up.

8. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. A researcher mixes purified RNA polymerase, DNA and nucleotides in a tube and gets mRNA. Is this in vivo or in vitro, and what has she learned?
    Answer

    In vitro (in a tube, purified components). She has learned the minimal requirements for making mRNA: these components alone are enough.

  2. Which is NOT a reason to pick an organism as a model?
    • short generation time
    • it causes an important human disease
    • its genome is sequenced
    • mutants can be made and kept
    Answer

    b. Models are chosen for practicality and general insight, not because they are medically or economically important. (Most E. coli strains are not pathogens.)

  3. Why was so much about DNA replication learned first in bacteria?
    Answer

    Bacteria were among the first models: huge numbers of cells are easy to obtain and easy to mutate. Later it turned out that much of the process is universal, which validated the choice.

  4. You want to study how a fertilized egg becomes an animal with organs. Which organism?
    Answer

    A higher eukaryote such as the fruit fly or the roundworm C. elegans. Yeast is single-celled and has no development of a body; E. coli is not even a eukaryote.

  5. True or false: "A gene has the same written name in every model organism."
    Answer

    False. The syllabus warns that the nomenclature for gene and protein names varies from organism to organism.

  6. What is agar and why is it used?
    Answer

    A polysaccharide from algae that is liquid when hot and sets as a gel when cool. Added to medium it gives a solid surface on which spread-out cells grow into separate colonies.

  7. A plate shows 200 colonies. How many cells were spread on it, roughly, and why?
    Answer

    About 200: at low density each cell grows into exactly one colony, so colonies count the original living cells.

  8. Name two conditions you can control in a liquid culture.
    Answer

    Oxygen supply (bubbling air or shaking, or no oxygen at all), temperature, and the nutrient source.

  9. What is contact inhibition?
    Answer

    When cultured cells on a surface touch each other, the contact triggers a cell-cycle arrest: they stop dividing. The result is a single layer (monolayer).

  10. Why do primary cells stop dividing after a limited number of divisions, even with space to grow?
    Answer

    Their telomeres shorten at every division (no telomerase in most body cells). Very short telomeres trigger senescence, a permanent stop.

  11. Which property do tumour-derived cell lines have that lets them divide indefinitely?
    • they have no DNA
    • they actively express telomerase and have lost normal brakes on division
    • they are larger
    • they do not need nutrients
    Answer

    b. Telomerase keeps the telomeres long, and other genetic changes remove the normal inhibitions on continued division.

  12. Give a second way, besides using tumour cells, to make cells grow continuously in culture.
    Answer

    Infect them with a cancer-causing virus, which switches off the normal controls on division.

  13. A laboratory receives a cell line from a colleague. Name two things that can go wrong with it.
    Answer

    It may not be the cell line it is labelled as (mix-ups when distributing lines), and it may have acquired mutations or rearrangements during culture. Also, in general, it differs from cells in intact tissue.
