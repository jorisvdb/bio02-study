4.9 Reading the letters of DNA: Sanger sequencing, and BLAST to compare sequences

In one sentence: Sanger sequencing reads the order of the bases in a piece of DNA by making copies that stop at random at one kind of base, and BLAST then compares that sequence with all known sequences to guess what the gene does.

The previous pages were about separating and identifying molecules. This page is about determining the composition of DNA: its sequence, the exact order of the four letters A, C, G and T. Sanger sequencing, invented in the 1970s, was the method for decades and is still used for single genes. Page 4.10 explains the fast "next generation" methods that followed. The second half of this page is about what you do with a sequence once you have it: compare it with a database using BLAST. The exam objective for this chapter is that you can describe the principle, methodology and applications of sequencing technologies, so for each method you should be able to say what you need, the steps, and what it is for.

1. The trick: a nucleotide that stops the chain (4.8.2, Figure 4-27)

Begin state: we have many identical copies of a DNA fragment (for example after PCR, page 4.3) and we want to know the order of its bases. The idea: let DNA polymerase copy the fragment, but mix in a few "poisoned" building blocks that stop the copy. If the copies stop at every position where, say, an A belongs, the lengths of the stopped copies tell us where the A's are.

The poisoned building block is a dideoxynucleotide, written ddNTP (read "d-d-N-T-P"; "dideoxy" = two oxygens missing). A normal dNTP has one oxygen missing from its sugar compared with the RNA sugar (that is why it is "deoxy"). A ddNTP also lacks the oxygen of the 3′ OH: on carbon 3 it has only an H instead of an OH. DNA polymerase can still build a ddNTP into the growing strand, but after that there is no 3′ OH to attach the next nucleotide to. The chain is terminated at that point, for good. That is why the method is called chain-termination sequencing (Sanger sequencing, after Frederick Sanger who invented it).

Must remember

A ddNTP can be built in (it still has its 5′ phosphates) but nothing can be built onto it (no 3′ OH). One ddNTP = one stop. The polymerase picks a normal dNTP or a ddNTP by chance, so in a big population of copies the stops happen at every possible position of that base.

2. The Sanger reaction, step by step (Figure 4-28)

What you needWhy
template DNAmany copies of the fragment to read (single-stranded during the reaction)
one primerpairs just before the region to read; gives the 3′ OH to start
DNA polymerasethe copying enzyme (DNA polymerase I in the syllabus figure; reverse transcriptase if RNA is being read)
all four dNTPsnormal building blocks, in large amounts
a small amount of ddNTPthe stoppers; small so that most positions get a normal dNTP and the copies reach every length
a labelradioactive dNTPs (classic), or fluorescent ddNTPs with a different colour per base (modern); without a label you cannot see the fragments

Classic version: four reactions, four lanes, read by eye

  1. Split the mixture into four tubes. Each tube gets all four dNTPs plus a small amount of one ddNTP: ddA in the first, ddC in the second, ddG in the third, ddT in the fourth. The dNTP-to-ddNTP ratio is chosen so that the stopper is built in only now and then.
  2. The primer anneals and the polymerase extends it. In the ddA tube, every copy stops at some A position, chosen by chance. Together the copies form a pool of fragments ending at every A; the same for C, G and T in the other tubes.
  3. Run the four tubes in four lanes of a polyacrylamide gel (page 4.8). The fragments sort by length: the shortest at the bottom, each next band one nucleotide longer.
  4. Make the bands visible (radioactive label: lay a photographic film on the gel, autoradiography).
  5. Read from the bottom to the top: the lowest band tells you the first base after the primer, the next band up the second base, and so on. The lane the band is in tells you which letter.

Automated version: one reaction, four colours

The modern form uses fluorescently labelled ddNTPs: each of the four stoppers carries a dye of a different colour (a fluorophore, a molecule that glows in a chosen colour when a laser shines on it). Now all four ddNTPs can go into one tube, because the colour of a fragment says at which base it stopped. The fragments are sorted by capillary gel electrophoresis: the same principle as a slab gel, but the gel is inside a thin glass tube (a capillary) and it separates one sample at a time. As each fragment passes the laser, its colour is recorded. The output is a chromatogram: a row of coloured peaks, one per base, which the computer translates into letters. One reaction reads up to about 900 bases.

Where to use it

Sanger sequencing is accurate and simple, so it is still the standard for reading one gene or one PCR product, or for checking a cloned fragment. For a whole genome it is far too slow: one machine reads about 1000 reactions a day, and the first human genome took 100 machines about two years (page 4.10).

3. BLAST: what does this sequence look like? (4.8.3)

Begin state: you have a new sequence (DNA, RNA or protein) and no idea what it does. The idea: if it looks like a gene whose job is known, it probably does something similar, because the two are homologs: they come from one common ancestor gene and kept most of their letters.

Comparing two sequences letter by letter is called an alignment (uitlijning): the sequences are written under each other so that matching positions line up. The computer then gives the alignment a score: points for identical letters, fewer points for similar ones (for proteins: amino acids with similar properties), and a penalty (strafpunten) for every gap that had to be inserted in one sequence to keep the rest aligned.

BLAST, the standard program

  • BLAST = Basic Local Alignment Search Tool, free at the NCBI website (the US National Center for Biotechnology Information). "Local" means it looks for pieces that match well, not necessarily the whole length.
  • It makes pairwise alignments: your query (the sequence you ask about) against each sequence in a database, one pair at a time.
  • BLASTN aligns DNA (N = nucleotide); it starts from a string of 11 nucleotides that matches exactly and then extends outward.
  • BLASTP aligns proteins (P = protein); it starts from a string of 3 amino acids and then looks in a "window" next to it for more similarity.
  • The result for each hit is a score and an E value: the probability that an alignment this good could have appeared by chance. The lower the E value, the better the match (an E value of 10⁻⁵⁰ is excellent; 1 means "you would expect one such hit by chance").

From a match to a function

A good hit tells you the two sequences are probably homologs, so they probably do similar things. The similarity may cover the whole protein or only one domain, for example a kinase domain (a kinase is an enzyme that attaches phosphate groups), a zinc-finger DNA-binding domain (a small fold held by a zinc ion that grips DNA) or a carbohydrate-binding domain. Finding a known domain in an unknown protein is often the first clue to its job. The syllabus example: a search with the Hermes transposase of the housefly (a transposase is an enzyme that cuts a jumping piece of DNA out and pastes it elsewhere) found the Hobo transposase of the fruit fly, an ortholog (the same gene in another species, BIO01 chapter 1).

Be critical

Similar is not the same. A protein can share a whole domain, even the same 3D shape, with an enzyme and still be inactive, because one or two active-site amino acids (the ones that do the chemistry) are substituted. So "BLAST says it looks like a kinase" is a hypothesis to test, not a conclusion. There are many other programs for domains, modification sites and interaction domains, and more and more database entries also include structural information.

7. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

What is a dideoxynucleotide and why does it terminate DNA synthesis?
A nucleotide whose sugar has no 3′ OH group (only an H on carbon 3). DNA polymerase can add it to the chain, but the next nucleotide must be attached to a 3′ OH, and there is none, so the chain ends there.
Why is only a small amount of ddNTP added?
So that at each position most copies get a normal dNTP and keep growing, while a few stop. Across the whole population the stops then occur at every position of that base, giving fragments of every length.
What do the four tubes of a classic Sanger reaction contain?
Each tube has template, primer, polymerase and all four dNTPs; tube 1 also has a little ddATP, tube 2 ddCTP, tube 3 ddGTP, tube 4 ddTTP. Each tube produces fragments that all end at one kind of base.
How do you read the sequence from the gel?
From the bottom to the top. The shortest fragment (lowest band) ends at the first base after the primer; each band above it is one nucleotide longer. The lane of the band gives the letter. The result is the newly made strand, 5′ → 3′.
How does the automated method avoid four separate lanes?
Each ddNTP carries a different fluorescent colour, so all four can be in one reaction. Capillary gel electrophoresis sorts the fragments by length, and a detector reads the colour of each fragment as it passes the laser; the output is a chromatogram.
How many bases can one Sanger reaction read?
Up to about 900 bases. For a whole genome many thousands or millions of reads are needed (page 4.10).
What is an alignment, and how is it scored?
Two sequences laid under each other so that matching positions line up. The score rewards identical and similar residues and subtracts a penalty for each gap inserted; it also grows with the length of the similar region.
What is the E value, and is a high or a low value good?
The probability that an alignment this good would occur by chance. A low E value is good: the match is unlikely to be accidental, so the sequences are probably homologs.
What is the difference between BLASTN and BLASTP?
BLASTN aligns DNA sequences and starts from an exactly matching string of 11 nucleotides; BLASTP aligns protein sequences and starts from a string of 3 amino acids, then widens the search in a window next to it.
How can an alignment suggest the function of an unknown gene?
Homologs share an ancestor and usually a function. If the unknown protein aligns with a protein of known function, or contains a known domain (kinase, zinc finger, carbohydrate-binding), that is a clue to what it does.
Why must BLAST results be interpreted with care?
A protein can resemble an enzyme over a whole domain and still lack its activity if key active-site amino acids are substituted. Similarity suggests, it does not prove.
What did the BLAST search with Hermes transposase find?
Other versions of Hermes in the database, and the Hobo transposase of Drosophila (the fruit fly), an orthologous protein.

8. To memorize

Learn these by heart
  • ddNTP = no 3′ OH → can be built in, nothing can follow → chain termination.
  • Sanger needs: template, primer, DNA polymerase, 4 dNTPs, a little ddNTP, a label.
  • Classic: 4 tubes, 4 lanes (ddA, ddC, ddG, ddT); fragments end at every position of that base.
  • Read the gel bottom → top: shortest fragment = first base after the primer.
  • Automated: 4 fluorescent ddNTPs in 1 tube, capillary electrophoresis, chromatogram of coloured peaks.
  • One Sanger read ≈ 900 bases; good for one gene, too slow for a genome.
  • Alignment = sequences lined up; score = identical + similar − gap penalties.
  • BLAST (NCBI): BLASTN DNA, 11-nucleotide seed; BLASTP protein, 3-amino-acid seed; pairwise query vs database.
  • E value = chance match probability; lower = better.
  • Homology → shared ancestor → probably similar function; domains (kinase, zinc finger) are clues.
  • Be critical: same domain, one changed active-site residue, no activity. Hermes (housefly) found Hobo (fruit fly) = orthologs.

9. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. Which part of a ddNTP is different from a dNTP, and what is the consequence?
    Answer

    The 3′ OH is missing (an H instead). The polymerase can still add the ddNTP, but it cannot add anything after it, because the next nucleotide must be joined to a 3′ OH. The chain is terminated.

  2. In the ddG tube, where do the copies stop?
    • all at the first G
    • each copy at a random G position, so together at every G
    • at every base
    • at the last G only
    Answer

    b. At each G position the polymerase picks a dNTP or a ddNTP by chance. Because the ddNTP is rare, each copy stops at a different G; the whole population covers every G.

  3. A classic Sanger gel shows, from bottom to top, bands in the lanes T, T, A, G, C. What is the sequence of the new strand?
    Answer

    5′-TTAGC-3′. The lowest band is the shortest fragment, so the first base added after the primer. Each higher band is one base longer.

  4. True or false: "In the automated Sanger method the four ddNTPs must still be kept in four separate reactions."
    Answer

    False. Each ddNTP has its own fluorescent colour, so all four are in one reaction. The detector reads the colour of each fragment as it leaves the capillary, which tells it the last base.

  5. What is the role of the primer in a sequencing reaction?
    Answer

    It pairs with the template just before the region to be read and provides the free 3′ OH the polymerase needs to start. It also fixes where reading begins, so all fragments share the same start.

  6. Why does a chromatogram have exactly one peak per base?
    Answer

    Because the fragments differ by exactly one nucleotide in length. Capillary electrophoresis lets them pass the laser one length after the other, and each length has the colour of the ddNTP that stopped it.

  7. Why is Sanger sequencing not used to read a whole human genome today?
    Answer

    One reaction reads at most about 900 bases, and a machine handles about 1000 reactions a day. The first human genome needed about 70 million reads, 100 machines and two years. Massively parallel methods (page 4.10) are far faster and cheaper.

  8. Which BLAST result is the better match?
    • E value 0.5
    • E value 10⁻³⁰
    • E value 5
    • they are equal; only the score counts
    Answer

    b. The E value is the probability that the match arose by chance. 10⁻³⁰ (one in a million trillion trillion) is essentially impossible by chance; 0.5 or 5 means such a hit is expected by accident.

  9. What is a gap in an alignment, and why does it cost points?
    Answer

    A space inserted in one sequence so that the following letters line up again (as if a few residues were inserted or deleted during evolution). Each gap lowers the score, otherwise the program could "match" anything by inserting gaps everywhere.

  10. You have an unknown protein sequence. Which program do you use, and what does it start from?
    Answer

    BLASTP, the protein version. It starts from a string of 3 amino acids that matches a database sequence and then examines the neighbouring window. (BLASTN, for DNA, starts from 11 nucleotides.)

  11. BLAST finds that your protein contains a kinase domain. What may you conclude, and what may you not?
    Answer

    You may conclude it is probably a homolog of kinases and may add phosphate groups to proteins. You may not conclude that it is an active kinase: if active-site amino acids are substituted, the protein can look like a kinase and do nothing. The guess must be tested.

  12. In a BLASTP output the query starts at position 9 and the subject at position 57. Is that a problem?
    Answer

    No. BLAST makes local alignments: it reports the stretch that matches, wherever it lies in each protein. The numbers simply say where the matching region begins in each sequence.

  13. Correct this statement: "Hermes and Hobo transposases are paralogs."
    Answer

    They are orthologs: the same gene in two species (housefly and fruit fly), separated by the birth of the species. Paralogs are copies inside one genome (BIO01 chapter 1).
