4.10 Reading a whole genome: shotgun assembly, coverage, Illumina and Nanopore

In one sentence: no machine can read a chromosome from end to end, so a genome is cut into millions of random pieces, every piece is read many times over, and a computer puts the pieces back together by finding where their sequences overlap.

Page 4.9 showed how one Sanger reaction reads about 900 bases. A human genome has three thousand million bases. This page is about the strategy that makes whole genomes possible (shotgun sequencing), the arithmetic behind it (over-sequencing and depth of coverage), and the two newer technologies that the syllabus describes in detail: Illumina (second generation) and Nanopore (third generation). The exam objective is to describe the principle, methodology and applications of sequencing technologies, and the example exam has several questions on choosing the right tool.

1. A short history (4.9, Figure 4-30)

  • 1970s, Ghent: Professor Walter Fiers (1931–2019) and his team at Ghent University sequenced the first gene and later the first complete genome, that of bacteriophage MS2 (a tiny virus that infects bacteria). They did it with Sanger sequencing, fragment by fragment, by hand.
  • Milestones after that: the first prokaryote (a bacterium), the first eukaryote (Saccharomyces cerevisiae, baker's yeast), the first multicellular organism (Caenorhabditis elegans, a roundworm) and the first plant (Arabidopsis thaliana, a small weed used as a model plant).
  • 1990s to 2003, the Human Genome Project: the whole human genome with Sanger methods: more than ten years and billions of dollars. It showed that much faster, high-throughput methods were needed ("high-throughput" = handling very many samples at once).
  • Early 2000s onward: new technologies read vast numbers of DNA molecules in parallel. What took years now takes days, and genomes are sequenced across the whole tree of life.
Samenvatting

Het Human Genome Project met Sanger duurde meer dan tien jaar en kostte miljarden; daarom kwamen vanaf 2000 de snelle, massaal parallelle methoden.

2. Shotgun sequencing: cut, read, reassemble (4.9.1, Figure 4-31)

Begin state: the DNA of a whole genome, far too long to read in one go. Goal: its complete sequence in the right order.

  1. Fragment the genomic DNA at random: by mechanical shearing (breaking it by force) or by cutting with a restriction enzyme (page 4.4). This gives thousands to millions of random, overlapping fragments. At this point nobody knows where each fragment came from, and that is fine.
  2. Attach linkers (adapters) to all the ends.
  3. Amplify every fragment by PCR, using primers that match the linkers. (For the first human genome the fragments were cloned in E. coli as a library instead, page 4.4; today PCR is used.)
  4. Sequence all the fragments.
  5. Assemble: the computer removes the linker sequences and looks for reads whose ends overlap (share the same letters). Overlapping reads are chained into longer and longer pieces until the whole genome is reconstructed in order.

The name "shotgun" comes from the random fragmentation at the start, like the scattered pellets of a shotgun (hagelgeweer).

Samenvatting

Shotgun: knip het genoom in willekeurige, overlappende fragmenten (schudden of restrictie-enzym), plak linkers aan de uiteinden, vermenigvuldig met PCR, lees alle fragmenten, en laat de computer de overlappende reads aaneenketenen tot het hele genoom. Bij de start hoef je niet te weten waar een fragment vandaan komt.

3. Why you must read much more than the genome: over-sequencing and coverage (4.9.2, 4.9.3)

To get a complete genome you need to sequence far more bases than the genome contains. The syllabus gives three reasons:

  1. Errors. Sequencing is not error-free. If the same region is read several times independently, an error in one read stands out against the others and can be corrected.
  2. Random fragments must cover everything. Because the fragments are random, some regions are read many times by chance and others not at all. In theory you need eight genome equivalents to be sure that 99.9 % of the genome is read at least once. For the 4.6 Mbp E. coli chromosome that is about 37 Mbp of sequence. In practice even more is read, because that also improves accuracy (reason 1).
  3. Enough overlaps for assembly. The computer needs many overlapping reads to work out the correct order.

The hard part: repeats

Repetitive DNA occurs in many places of the genome. A read that lies entirely inside a repeat matches many places at once, so the computer cannot tell where it belongs. If a repeated region is longer than the reads, the task becomes nearly impossible: no read reaches the unique sequence on either side. That is why the first genome sequences, including the human one, had gaps. Three things help: smarter assembly programs, genetic linkage data (knowing from inheritance which markers lie near each other), and longer reads that span the repeat and connect the unique sequences on both sides (Nanopore, section 6).

Depth of coverage

The depth of coverage (or sequencing depth) of a base is the number of independent reads that contain it. It is written as a multiple: a base present in five reads has 5X coverage ("five-fold", "five times"). Higher depth means more confidence when a difference from the reference is found (variant calling, deciding that a difference is real), especially for rare variants, where a single sequencing error could otherwise be mistaken for a mutation. The depth needed depends on the experiment: the quality and amount of the sample, how many variants are expected and how frequent they are, and what similar studies used. Page 4.11 gives typical numbers (more than 30X for a whole genome, more than 500X for a small target).

Two kinds of "how much"

Genome equivalents describe the total: 8 genome equivalents = 8 × the genome length in reads. Coverage depth describes one base: 5X = that base is in 5 reads. Reading 8 genome equivalents gives an average depth of about 8X, but random fragments make some bases 1X and others 15X.

Samenvatting

Je moet veel meer lezen dan het genoom lang is, om drie redenen: fouten corrigeren, willekeurige fragmenten moeten alles raken (8 genoomequivalenten voor 99,9 %; E. coli 4,6 Mbp → 37 Mbp), en genoeg overlap voor de assemblage. Repeats zijn het probleem: een read binnen een repeat past op veel plaatsen; langer dan de reads = bijna onoplosbaar → gaten in de eerste genomen; lange reads helpen. Dekkingsdiepte: 5X = een base zit in vijf reads; meer diepte = betrouwbaarder varianten vinden, vooral zeldzame.

The NGS workflow in four steps (Figure 4-35)

  1. DNA extraction: isolate high-quality DNA from the sample and check it.
  2. Library preparation: fragment the DNA and attach adapters; the collection of adapter-tagged fragments is the "library". The adapters are needed for amplification and for the sequencing steps.
  3. Sequencing: load the library on the machine; millions of fragments are read at the same time, giving raw sequence data.
  4. Data analysis: a bioinformatician checks the quality, aligns the reads to a reference genome and calls variants (finds the differences).

NGS-workflow: DNA isoleren, bibliotheek maken (fragmenteren + adapters), sequencen, analyseren (aligneren op een referentie, varianten bepalen).

5. Illumina: one coloured base per cycle (4.9.4.1, Figures 4-36, 4-37)

The key molecule: a reversible terminator. Like the ddNTP of Sanger it has a block on its 3′ end, so the polymerase can add only one base and then must stop. Unlike the ddNTP, the block is a chemical group that can be removed afterwards, so the chain can go on. Each of the four bases also carries its own fluorescent colour.

Steps

  1. Prepare the fragments: break the genomic DNA, ligate adapters to the ends, denature to single strands (page 4.3: denature = separate the two strands with heat).
  2. Capture on a surface: the single strands hybridise to a glass surface densely coated with short DNA pieces complementary to the adapters (a "lawn" of primers). Each fragment sticks by one end.
  3. Bridge amplification: the free end of the fragment bends over and pairs with a neighbouring surface primer, forming a bridge. The polymerase copies it; the strands are separated; each copy bends over again. After many rounds every spot holds a cluster of thousands of identical copies. This is needed because one molecule gives too little light to see; a cluster gives a strong signal.
  4. Sequence by synthesis, one cycle: add a primer, DNA polymerase and the four reversible terminators, each with a different colour. Because of the 3′ block, exactly one base is added to every strand in every cluster. Wash away the unused nucleotides.
  5. Image: a laser excites the dyes; a camera photographs the whole surface and records the colour of every cluster, that is, which base was added.
  6. Reset: chemically remove the dye and the 3′ block, so the 3′ OH is free again.
  7. Repeat steps 4 to 6 for 75 to 150 cycles. Each cycle gives one base for millions of clusters at the same time.

Limitation: Illumina reads are short, 75 to 150 bp. Short reads overlap less and cannot span repeats, so assembly needs more depth of coverage. That is why Illumina is mostly used with a reference genome (page 4.11), where reads only need to be placed, not assembled from scratch.

Samenvatting

Illumina: omkeerbare terminatoren (3′ geblokkeerd zoals een ddNTP, maar de blokkering kan weer verwijderd worden; elke base een eigen kleur). Fragmenten met adapters hechten aan een glasplaat vol primers; brugamplificatie maakt per plek een cluster van identieke kopieën (genoeg licht). Per cyclus: één gekleurde base inbouwen, wassen, foto nemen (kleur = base), kleur en blokkering verwijderen, herhalen (75 tot 150 cycli). Nadeel: korte reads, dus meer dekking nodig.

6. Nanopore: reading the current (4.9.4.2, Figure 4-38)

The idea: pull a single DNA strand through a tiny hole and measure how the electrical current through the hole changes; each base blocks the hole a bit differently. No copying, no colours, no amplification.

  1. The sensor: a nanopore is a hole of a few nanometres (a nanometre is a millionth of a millimetre), formed by a protein sitting in a membrane. The membrane separates two chambers filled with a salt solution that conducts electricity. A voltage across the membrane makes ions (charged salt particles) flow through the pore: a steady ionic current.
  2. Translocation: a motor protein attached to the pore unwinds the double helix and feeds one single strand through the pore at a controlled speed. While a base is inside the pore it partly blocks the current.
  3. Signal: each base (A, T, C, G, and also chemically modified bases such as methylated C) has its own size and chemistry, so each disturbs the current in its own way. The electrical trace is decoded into the sequence.
  4. Real time: the data come out while the DNA is passing, so analysis can start immediately, which matters when speed is critical (for example identifying a pathogen during an outbreak).

Advantages and uses

  • Very long reads, even entire chromosomes: ideal for repeats and for structural variants (large rearrangements such as the ones in chapter 1 and in cancer genomes).
  • Portable: the MinION device fits in the palm of a hand, so sequencing can be done in the field.
  • Direct RNA sequencing: RNA can be read without first converting it to DNA.
  • Epigenetics: modified bases such as methylated C give their own signal, so DNA methylation is detected directly.
  • Applications: genome organisation, pathogen detection in outbreaks, epigenetics, cancer research (mutations and rearrangements in tumours).
Samenvatting

Nanopore: een eiwit-porie in een membraan tussen twee kamers met zoutoplossing; een spanning laat ionen door de porie stromen. Een motoreiwit ontwindt het DNA en voert één streng door de porie; elke base blokkeert de stroom op haar eigen manier; het stroomsignaal wordt vertaald in de sequentie, in realtime. Voordelen: zeer lange reads (repeats, structurele varianten), draagbaar (MinION), RNA rechtstreeks, methylering zichtbaar. Geen PCR, geen kleurstoffen.

10. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

Why can a genome not simply be read from one end to the other?
No technology reads an entire chromosome in one reaction (Sanger ~900 bases, Illumina 75–150). So the genome is broken into many short pieces that are read separately and then put back together.
What are the steps of shotgun sequencing?
Fragment the genome at random (shearing or restriction enzyme); attach linkers; amplify by PCR (or clone in a library); sequence every fragment; let a computer find overlaps and assemble the sequence in order.
Why does assembly work even though the fragments are random?
Because the genome is fragmented in many copies, so the fragments overlap. Two reads that share the same letters at their ends must come from neighbouring places, and chaining such overlaps reconstructs the order.
Give the three reasons for over-sequencing.
(1) Errors in single reads can be recognised and corrected when the region is read several times; (2) random fragments: to be sure every region is read at least once you need about 8 genome equivalents for 99.9 %; (3) enough overlapping reads are needed to determine the correct order.
How much sequence is needed for the E. coli chromosome, and why?
About 37 Mbp for a 4.6 Mbp chromosome: eight genome equivalents, to cover 99.9 % of it. In practice even more, for accuracy.
What does "a base has 5X coverage" mean, and why does depth matter?
The base is contained in five independent reads. More depth makes variant calling more confident, especially for rare variants, where a sequencing error in one read could otherwise look like a real mutation.
Why are repeats a problem, and what helps?
A read lying inside a repeat matches many places in the genome, so it cannot be placed; if the repeat is longer than the reads, nothing connects it to unique sequence. Better algorithms, genetic linkage data and long reads that span the repeat help.
What distinguishes the three generations of sequencing?
First: Sanger, one read per reaction. Second (NGS): massively parallel sequencing of PCR-amplified fragments, short reads. Third: single molecules read directly, no PCR, very long reads.
What is a reversible terminator, and why is it needed in Illumina?
A fluorescent nucleotide with a removable block on its 3′ end. The block ensures that only one base is added per cycle, so the camera can read one base at a time; removing the block lets the next cycle proceed.
Why does Illumina make clusters?
A single molecule gives too little fluorescence to detect. Bridge amplification copies each fragment at its spot thousands of times, so every cluster gives a strong, readable signal.
How does a nanopore "read" a base?
Ions flow through the pore under a voltage. When a strand is pushed through by the motor protein, each base inside the pore blocks the current by a characteristic amount. The sequence of current levels is decoded into bases.
Name four advantages of Nanopore sequencing.
Very long reads (repeats, structural variants); portable device (MinION); direct RNA sequencing; detection of modified bases such as methylation. Also real-time output and no PCR.
Who sequenced the first genome, and which one was it?
Walter Fiers and his team at Ghent University, in the 1970s, with Sanger sequencing: the genome of bacteriophage MS2 (after sequencing the first gene).

12. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. Why is the method called "shotgun" sequencing?
    Answer

    Because it starts with random fragmentation of the genome, like the scattered pellets of a shotgun. The pieces are read without knowing where they came from and reassembled afterwards by their overlaps.

  2. Two reads end and begin with the same 60 letters. What does the computer conclude?
    Answer

    That they overlap: they come from neighbouring places in the genome and can be chained into one longer piece (a contig). Finding such overlaps for millions of reads is the assembly.

  3. How many genome equivalents are needed in theory to cover 99.9 % of a genome at least once?
    • 1
    • 2
    • 8
    • 100
    Answer

    c. Because the fragments are random, one genome length of reads leaves many regions unread. About eight times the genome length is needed for 99.9 %; for E. coli (4.6 Mbp) that is 37 Mbp.

  4. A base is found in 12 reads. What is its depth of coverage, and what does that buy you?
    Answer

    12X. If one read shows a different letter and the other eleven agree, the odd one is probably a sequencing error; if six show it, it is probably a real variant (for example from one of the two chromosomes). Depth gives confidence in variant calling.

  5. True or false: "Reading 8 genome equivalents gives every base exactly 8X coverage."
    Answer

    False. It gives an average of about 8X. Because fragments are random, some bases are covered once and others many times; that randomness is exactly why over-sequencing is needed.

  6. Why did the first human genome sequence have gaps?
    Answer

    Because of repetitive DNA. Reads inside a repeat fit many places; where a repeat was longer than the reads, no read connected it to the unique sequence on either side, so those regions could not be assembled.

  7. Which generation of sequencing reads single molecules without PCR, and what is its main advantage?
    Answer

    The third generation (for example Nanopore). Its main advantage is long reads, which span repeats and reveal structural variants; a second advantage is that there are no PCR errors.

  8. Put the four steps of an NGS workflow in order: data analysis, sequencing, DNA extraction, library preparation.
    Answer

    DNA extraction → library preparation (fragment + adapters) → sequencing → data analysis (align to a reference, call variants).

  9. What is the difference between a ddNTP (Sanger) and an Illumina reversible terminator?
    Answer

    Both block the 3′ end so that synthesis stops after one addition. The ddNTP lacks the 3′ OH permanently; the reversible terminator carries a chemical block that is removed after imaging, so the strand can grow on in the next cycle. Each reversible terminator also has its own colour.

  10. In Illumina, what is a cluster and why is it needed?
    Answer

    A spot on the surface holding thousands of identical copies of one fragment, made by bridge amplification. One molecule would give too little light; a cluster gives a signal the camera can see.

  11. An Illumina run uses 100 cycles. How long are the reads?
    Answer

    About 100 bases: each cycle adds and reads exactly one base in every cluster. Typical Illumina reads are 75 to 150 bp, short compared with Sanger and very short compared with Nanopore.

  12. What does the motor protein do in Nanopore sequencing?
    Answer

    It sits on the pore, unwinds the double-stranded DNA and feeds one single strand through the pore at a controlled speed, so that the current changes can be assigned to one base after another.

  13. You must identify an unknown pathogen in a remote village within hours and later look at its large rearrangements. Which technology, and why?
    Answer

    Nanopore (MinION): portable, works in the field, gives results in real time, and its long reads reveal structural variants. Illumina would need a lab and gives short reads; Sanger is far too slow.

  14. Correct this statement: "The Human Genome Project used Illumina sequencing."
    Answer

    It used Sanger sequencing (about 70 million reads, 100 machines, two years, more than ten years in total and billions of dollars). Illumina and other NGS methods came in the early 2000s, after the project showed how badly faster methods were needed.
