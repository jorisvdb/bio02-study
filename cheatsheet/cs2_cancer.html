A · What cancer isA society of cells, and one clone that breaks the rules and evolves.

Chapter 2 › Cells as a society

Social controls: a cell rests, grows, divides, specialises or dies on command

Somatic cells sacrifice themselves for the germ cells; a mutant cell that grows "slightly more vigorously" founds a clone = the start of cancer.

Senescence = irreversible arrest (tumour-suppressive); quiescence = reversible rest (exam Q3 matching).

What cancer is › Benign, malignant, metastasis

Benign stays inside the basal lamina; malignant invades; metastasis = colonies elsewhere

tumour / neoplasm"new growth": cells dividing out of control
benignnot invasive; local removal cures
malignant = cancerinvades surrounding tissue
metastasissecondary tumour elsewhere; kills by organ failure

Two heritable properties define a cancer cell: proliferate against the rules AND invade/colonise. Invasiveness is essential.

What cancer is › Names by tissue

Cancer names by tissue of origin

Most human cancers are carcinomas because epithelia divide most and are exposed to damage.

What cancer is › One abnormal cell

Most cancers come from ONE cell: the Philadelphia proof

Breast tumour doubling time ≈ 100 days; 10⁸ cells visible on x-ray, 10⁹ palpable, 10¹² death.

What cancer is › One cell › Tumour progression, Knudson

Tumour progression: many hits over many years (Knudson)

smoking → lung cancerdecades later
Hiroshima radiation → leukemiarise after ~5 years
industrial carcinogen10–20+ years

"Multiple hit theory of Knudson": one mutation is never enough; the bigger the tumour, the faster the next hit comes.

What cancer is › One cell › Clonal evolution

Clonal evolution: mutation, selection, subclones (heterogeneity)

  1. One cell gets a mutation (or epigenetic change)
  2. Advantage → its progeny dominate (a clone)
  3. More cells → more chance of the next hit
  4. Next hit → new dominant subclone
  5. Final step: invasion, metastasis

Darwin inside the body: mutation + natural selection, within one lifetime. Harsh tumour environment (low oxygen, few nutrients) selects.

What cancer is › Genetic instability, chromothripsis

Genetic instability speeds evolution; chromothripsis = a chromosome shattered

type 1karyotype normal, many point mutations → repair failure
type 2karyotype disordered: breaks, deletions, duplications, amplifications

Instability is selected FOR: anything that speeds heritable change speeds evolution toward malignancy. It also creates heterogeneity.

What cancer is › Hallmarks of cancer

Hallmarks of cancer (Hanahan and Weinberg 2000 → 2011 → 2022)

Syllabus list of 6 attributes: faster growth than death · bypass limits · evade death signals · altered metabolism · manipulate tissue + evade immunity · escape home tissue (metastasis).

Angiogenesis = new blood vessels: it supplies blood (oxygen, nutrients) to the tumour (exam Q4). Not "growth factors", not "inhibits apoptosis".

What cancer is › Tumour microenvironment

The stroma helps: two-way talk, angiogenesis, immune escape

  1. Cancer cells secrete signals + proteases (matrix cutters)
  2. Stroma signals back: grow, divide
  3. Inflammation like an unhealed wound
  4. Angiogenesis: vessels for the hypoxic core
  5. Immunosuppressive niche: white cells blocked

Immune cells can destroy OR promote the tumour. Therapies that lift the immune block are "powerful tools".

What cancer is › Metastasis steps

Metastasis: a multistep journey; colonisation is the hard step

Metastasis = 90 % of cancer deaths; last step (colonising a distant site) is rate-limiting: 1 in thousands to millions succeeds. Micrometastases can stay dormant for years.

CTC clusters metastasise more than single CTCs (adhesion overrides death signals).

B · Cancer-critical genesAbout 300 genes; two classes; a handful of pathways; Rb and p53.

Genes › Two classes of cancer-critical genes

Proto-oncogene → oncogene (gas stuck) vs tumor suppressor (brakes lost)

exam Q6 matching
oncogenemutant gene whose overactive form causes cancer
proto-oncogenenormal gene; gain-of-function mutation drives toward cancer
tumor suppressor genenormal gene; loss-of-function contributes to cancer
p53tumor suppressor mutated in ~half of human cancers

Genome maintenance genes = subclass of tumor suppressors (their loss → instability). Both classes can give the same effect: "flip sides of the same coin".

Genes › Oncogenes › Three ways to make an oncogene

Three accidents that turn a proto-oncogene into an oncogene

  • regulatory region (promoter)
  • coding region
  • mutation / break

Genes › Oncogenes › Which mutations give an oncogene?

Which mutations can result in an oncogene? (syllabus 2.4, several answers)

deletion of the entire coding regionno: no protein at all
deletion of a silencer in the 3′ UTRyes: brake on expression removed → more
deletion of an enhancerno: less expression
deletion of a 3′ splice acceptorno: mRNA mis-spliced, protein broken
premature stop codonno: truncated protein (typical for tumor suppressors)
a point mutationyes if it makes the protein hyperactive (Ras)
translocation under a constitutive promoteryes: always-on production

Claude's reasoning, not an official key. Rule: an oncogene needs MORE or STRONGER product. UTR = untranslated region of the mRNA; silencer/enhancer = DNA elements that lower/raise transcription.

Genes › Tumor suppressors › Rb and retinoblastoma

Rb: first tumor suppressor, found in retinoblastoma (two hits)

  • 2 good copies
  • 1 good copy
  • 0 good copies → tumour
retinoblastomaeye tumour of children, retina neural precursor cells, 1 in 20 000
cluedeletion of a band on chromosome 13 → Rb gene mapped
Rb proteinuniversal brake on cell-cycle entry (G1); also lost in lung, breast, bladder carcinomas

Finding a LOST gene needs a different strategy than finding a hyperactive one (you cannot transform cells with something absent).

Genes › Tumor suppressors › Losing the second copy

Six ways to lose the remaining good copy (genetic and epigenetic)

First copy: usually a small specific event (deletion, point mutation). Second copy: usually a sloppy, non-specific event in an unstable cell.

Epigenetic silencing changes NO DNA letter, yet is permanent in all descendants (BIO01 ch 6: methylation of CG cytosines).

Genes › Sequencing › Mutation patterns

Sequenced genomes: oncogene = same missense spot; tumor suppressor = truncations anywhere

drivercausal; seen repeatedly in many patients
passengerby-product of instability; rare, no advantage
cancer-critical genes~300 = 1 % of genes; diverse (signalling, cell cycle, metabolism, chromatin, splicing)
resourcesThe Cancer Genome Atlas (>10 000 tumours, 33 types), COSMIC

Scans: whole genome, exome (21 000 coding genes), copy number, methylation, RNA levels, proteins by mass spectrometry (ch 4).

Genes › Three pathways

Three controls broken in nearly every cancer: cell cycle, proliferation, survival

Rasswitch protein passing growth-factor signals into the cell; mutated in ~30 % of cancers (Alberts)
RTKreceptor tyrosine kinase: membrane receptor that adds phosphates when a growth factor binds
PI 3-kinaseenzyme downstream of Ras (growth)

What matters is the broken CONTROL, not which gene: a tumour without an Rb mutation has another Rb-pathway gene mutated.

Genes › Tumor suppressors › p53

p53: the stress antenna → arrest, senescence or apoptosis

  1. Normally: p53 made but quickly degraded (little)
  2. Stress → p53 stabilised, level rises
  3. p53 = transcription regulator
  4. Switches on p21 → p21 blocks Cdk complexes
  5. No S phase: cycle arrest; or pro-apoptotic genes on
mutated in~50 % of cancers; most in its DNA-binding domain
knockout micenormal, but all get cancer before 10 months
apoptosis vs necrosisgood death (eaten, recycled) vs bad death (burst, inflammation)

Exam Q7: "guardian of the genome" = p53 (repair, arrest, apoptosis). RB1 only brakes S-phase entry; RAS is a signal switch.

Genes › Colorectal cancer: the order

Colorectal cancer: Apc early → K-Ras → p53 late

Apc firstsame high frequency in small polyps and big tumours
K-Ras laterrare in small polyps, common in large ones
p53 lastrare in polyps, common in carcinomas

Why p53 last: moderate Ras activation is not stressful; high activation IS → only without p53 can hyperactive oncogenes survive. Polyp = benign bump of gut lining; adenoma = benign gland tumour.
