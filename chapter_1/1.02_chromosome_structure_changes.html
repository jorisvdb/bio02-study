1.2 Changes in chromosome structure: deletion, duplication, inversion, translocation

In one sentence: a chromosome can break, and the broken piece can get lost, be added as an extra copy, be put back the wrong way round, or be stuck onto a different chromosome; even when no gene is lost, moving genes around can change how they work.

Page 1.1 was about the number of chromosomes. This page is about their structure (bouw): what happens when a chromosome breaks and the pieces are rejoined wrongly. There are only four possible outcomes, and the whole page hangs on those four words: deletion, duplication, inversion, translocation. Page 1.3 uses them for real human conditions: a deletion that causes cri du chat syndrome and a translocation that causes a leukemia. The example exam asks about these mechanisms when it lists "translocation", "duplication", "inversion" and "deletion" as answer options next to nondisjunction.

1. Where the breaks come from (1.1.2)

A chromosome does not change its structure by itself. First it has to break. The syllabus gives two causes:

  • Errors in meiosis, especially during crossing over, when the homologs are being cut and rejoined on purpose.
  • Damaging agents such as radiation (straling: for example X-rays), which snap the DNA.

After a break, the cell's repair machinery tries to glue the ends back together (BIO01 chapter 4 described DNA repair). If it glues the wrong ends, or loses a piece, one of the four changes below is the result.

Samenvatting

Eerst moet een chromosoom breken: door fouten in de meiose (vooral bij crossing-over) of door beschadiging zoals straling. Bij het herstellen kunnen de verkeerde uiteinden aan elkaar worden gelijmd, of een stuk gaat verloren.

2. The four possible outcomes (Figure 1-2)

Think of a chromosome as a row of lettered segments, A B C D E F G H, each segment holding some genes. A break happens at the red arrows. What can happen to the broken piece?

  1. Deletion (deletie): the fragment is lost. The chromosome is now missing certain genes.
  2. Duplication (duplicatie): the broken fragment becomes reattached as an extra segment to a sister or non-sister chromatid. That chromosome now has a part of itself twice.
  3. Inversion (inversie): the fragment reattaches to the original chromosome, but in the reverse orientation (turned around, omgekeerd). Nothing is lost or doubled; the order is changed.
  4. Translocation (translocatie): the fragment joins a non-homologous chromosome. Genes move to a different chromosome.
Two questions sort the four changes

Is any DNA lost or doubled? Deletion loses, duplication doubles. Does DNA stay on its own chromosome? Inversion: yes, but turned around. Translocation: no, it moves to a non-homologous chromosome. Inversion and reciprocal translocation keep all genes in their normal dose.

Samenvatting

Vier mogelijke uitkomsten na een breuk: deletie (stuk verloren, genen weg), duplicatie (stuk er extra bij op een zuster- of niet-zusterchromatide: dubbele dosis), inversie (stuk teruggeplakt maar omgekeerd: zelfde genen, andere volgorde), translocatie (stuk aan een niet-homoloog chromosoom). Bij een reciproke translocatie wisselen twee chromosomen stukken uit.

3. Unequal crossing over: how deletion and duplication arise together

Deletions and duplications are especially likely during meiosis, and the reason is crossing over. Normally, when two non-sister chromatids swap pieces, the pieces are the same size, so both partners end up with the same genes they had. Sometimes the two chromatids are not lined up exactly, and they exchange unequal-sized segments: one partner gives up more genes than it receives. This is an unequal crossover. Its products are always a pair:

  • one chromosome with a deletion (it gave more than it got), and
  • one chromosome with a duplication (it got more than it gave).
Samenvatting

Bij een ongelijke crossing-over liggen twee niet-zusterchromatiden niet precies tegenover elkaar en wisselen ze stukken van verschillende lengte uit: de ene geeft meer genen dan ze krijgt. Resultaat: altijd een paar: één chromosoom met een deletie en één met een duplicatie. Daarom komen deleties en duplicaties vooral tijdens de meiose voor.

4. How harmful is each change?

Deletions: usually lethal when nothing compensates

A deletion removes genes. Whether that matters depends on whether the other homolog still has a good copy. The syllabus describes the worst case: a diploid embryo that is homozygous for a large deletion (both homologs have the deletion), or a male with a single X chromosome that has a large deletion (a male has only one X, so there is no second copy to compensate). Such an embryo is "missing a number of essential genes", and this is typically lethal (dodelijk). Page 1.3 shows that even a heterozygous deletion (only one homolog affected) can cause severe problems, such as cri du chat syndrome: one copy of those genes is apparently not enough.

Duplications and translocations: also tend to be harmful

A duplication gives an extra dose of the duplicated genes; the syllabus simply says that duplications and translocations "also tend to be harmful".

Reciprocal translocations and inversions: the balance is normal, and yet

In a reciprocal translocation (segments are exchanged between two non-homologous chromosomes) and in an inversion, the balance of genes is not abnormal: all genes are present in their normal doses. Nevertheless they can alter the phenotype, because a gene's expression can be influenced by its location among neighbouring genes. A gene that is moved next to other control DNA may be switched on too strongly, switched off, or even fused to another gene; this can have devastating effects. The leukemia on page 1.3 (the Philadelphia chromosome) is exactly this: a translocation that fuses two genes into a new one.

Normal gene dose is not the same as a normal phenotype. Inversions and reciprocal translocations keep every gene, but where a gene sits decides how it is expressed. Moving it can be as bad as losing it.

8. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

What has to happen before a chromosome can change its structure?
It has to break. Errors in meiosis (especially during crossing over) and damaging agents such as radiation cause breakage; the wrong repair of the break gives the structural change.
Name the four types of structural change and say in one phrase what happens to the fragment.
Deletion: the fragment is lost. Duplication: it is reattached as an extra segment to a sister or non-sister chromatid. Inversion: it reattaches to the original chromosome in reverse orientation. Translocation: it joins a non-homologous chromosome.
Which two of the four keep all genes present in their normal dose?
Inversion and reciprocal translocation. Nothing is lost or doubled; genes only change place or order.
Why can an inversion or translocation still change the phenotype?
Because a gene's expression is influenced by its location among neighbouring genes. In a new neighbourhood the gene can be read more, less, or be fused to another gene, with possibly devastating effects.
What is an unequal crossover, and what does it produce?
A crossing over in meiosis in which two non-sister chromatids exchange segments of different size, so one partner gives up more genes than it receives. It produces one chromosome with a deletion and one with a duplication.
Why are deletions and duplications especially likely during meiosis?
Because crossing over, the exchange of pieces between homologs, happens in meiosis; when the exchange is unequal, a deletion and a duplication result.
When is a large deletion typically lethal?
When the embryo is homozygous for it (both homologs have the deletion) or when a male has it on his single X chromosome. In both cases essential genes are completely missing; no good copy remains.
What is a reciprocal translocation?
Two non-homologous chromosomes exchange segments with each other. All genes are still present, but part of each chromosome now sits on the other.
What is the difference between a translocation and a duplication?
A translocation moves a fragment to another, non-homologous chromosome (no extra copy). A duplication adds the fragment as an extra copy to a sister or non-sister chromatid, so the genes are present twice.
How is an inversion different from a translocation?
In an inversion the fragment stays on its own chromosome but is turned around; in a translocation it goes to a different chromosome.
Can a translocation cause an extra X chromosome (Klinefelter)?
No. A translocation moves a piece of a chromosome; it does not add a whole chromosome. Only nondisjunction gives a gamete with an extra X.

9. To memorize

Learn these by heart
  • Breakage comes from errors in meiosis or damaging agents (radiation); wrong rejoining gives the change.
  • Deletion = fragment lost; genes missing.
  • Duplication = fragment added as extra segment to a sister or non-sister chromatid; genes doubled.
  • Inversion = fragment back on the same chromosome, reversed; same genes, reversed order.
  • Translocation = fragment joins a non-homologous chromosome; reciprocal = two chromosomes swap pieces.
  • Unequal crossover (meiosis) → one deletion + one duplication, always as a pair.
  • Large deletion, homozygous or on a male's single X → essential genes missing → typically lethal.
  • Duplications and translocations also tend to be harmful.
  • Inversion and reciprocal translocation: gene balance normal, yet phenotype can change: expression depends on the neighbours.
  • Number change (nondisjunction) ≠ structure change (break): an extra whole X is nondisjunction, never translocation or duplication.

10. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. A chromosome A B C D E F G H becomes A B E F G H. Which change is this?
    Answer

    Deletion. The fragment C D is lost, so the chromosome misses the genes on it.

  2. A chromosome A B C D E F G H becomes A B E D C F G H. Which change?
    • deletion
    • duplication
    • inversion
    • translocation
    Answer

    c. The piece C D E is still there and still on the same chromosome, but in reverse order: an inversion. No gene is lost or doubled.

  3. A chromosome A B C D E F G H becomes A B C D C D E F G H. Which change, and what happens to the gene dose?
    Answer

    Duplication. Segment C D is present twice, so the genes in it have a double dose. The extra copy came from a sister or non-sister chromatid.

  4. Chromosome 1 (A B C D E F G H) ends as A B C D P Q and chromosome 2 (M N O P Q) ends as M N O E F G H. Which change, and is any gene lost?
    Answer

    Reciprocal translocation. The two non-homologous chromosomes exchanged their end pieces. No gene is lost: all of A to H and M to Q are still present, just on different chromosomes.

  5. True or false: "A translocation always makes the cell lose genes."
    Answer

    False. In a reciprocal translocation the pieces are exchanged, so all genes stay present in their normal dose. The danger is the new position of the genes, not a missing gene.

  6. Which two structural changes leave the gene balance normal?
    Answer

    Inversion and reciprocal translocation. Deletion lowers the dose (genes missing); duplication raises it (genes doubled).

  7. If the gene balance is normal after an inversion, why can the phenotype still change?
    Answer

    Because a gene's expression depends on its location among neighbouring genes. After the inversion a gene has new neighbours and new nearby control DNA, so it may be read more, less, or wrongly. The syllabus says this can have devastating effects.

  8. What do you always get as a pair from an unequal crossover?
    • two deletions
    • a deletion and a duplication
    • an inversion and a translocation
    • two duplications
    Answer

    b. One chromatid gives away more than it receives (deletion); the other receives more than it gives (duplication). What one loses, the other gains.

  9. In which phase of the cell's life are deletions and duplications especially likely, and why?
    Answer

    In meiosis, because that is when homologs cross over and exchange pieces; an unequal exchange produces a deletion and a duplication.

  10. Why is a large deletion usually lethal in a male when it is on the X chromosome, even if it is "only" on one chromosome?
    Answer

    A male has only one X. There is no second X to supply good copies of the missing genes, so essential genes are completely absent, just as in an embryo that is homozygous for the deletion.

  11. Name the two causes of chromosome breakage given in the syllabus.
    Answer

    Errors in meiosis and damaging agents such as radiation.

  12. An exam option says Klinefelter syndrome (XXY) is caused by "translocation during meiosis". What is wrong with it?
    Answer

    A translocation moves a piece of a chromosome onto another chromosome. It cannot give a cell a whole extra X. An extra X comes from nondisjunction: the sex chromosomes were not shared out correctly in meiosis (page 1.1).

  13. Correct this statement: "In a duplication, the extra piece always comes from the same chromatid that broke."
    Answer

    The extra segment is reattached to a sister or non-sister chromatid: the receiving chromatid gets a piece that it already had, from one of its partners. That is how a region ends up present twice on one chromosome.
