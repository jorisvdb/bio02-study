2.7 Reading cancer genomes: hundreds of genes, three pathways

In one sentence: sequencing thousands of tumors showed that about 300 genes can drive cancer, but almost every cancer breaks the same three controls (cell-cycle entry via Rb, growth signals via Ras, stress response via p53), and one broken gene per control is enough.

Pages 2.5 and 2.6 found cancer genes one at a time. This page is about what we learned when it became possible to read a tumor's whole genome: which kinds of mutations oncogenes and tumor suppressors carry (you can tell them apart by the pattern), how many cancer-critical genes there are, and the simplifying discovery that all those genes fall into a handful of pathways. The exam objectives behind it: "explain the role of various proto-oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes" and "explain how mutations in a proto-oncogene and in a tumor suppressor gene have different effects at the protein level".

1. Reading the whole picture: how cancer genomes are scanned (2.2.5)

The genes of pages 2.5 and 2.6 were found piece by piece. Since the human genome was sequenced and sequencing became cheap (chapter 4), we can look at a cancer cell's genome in its entirety. Several levels can be scanned, from most to least expensive:

What is readWhat it shows
Whole genome sequence of the tumorevery mutation, anywhere (most costly, but no longer prohibitively so)
The exome: only the roughly 21 000 protein-coding genescheaper; finds mutations that change the amino acid sequence or prevent the protein from being made
Copy numberwhich regions are deleted or duplicated, and which chromosomes are lost or gained (aneuploidy)
Epigenetic changesmethylation patterns and other marks that silence or activate genes without changing the sequence
RNA sequencinglevels of gene expression: how much mRNA of each gene, and of regulatory non-coding RNAs
Mass spectrometry of the tumor's proteinshow much of each known cancer-critical protein is present (chapter 4)

In every case the cancer cells are compared with normal controls, ideally noncancerous cells from the same tissue of the same person. A big consortium, The Cancer Genome Atlas (coordinated by the US National Institutes of Health), applied these methods to more than 10 000 tumors of 33 cancer types. Free international databases such as COSMIC (the Catalog of Somatic Mutations in Cancer) keep compiling new data. These projects showed what types of alterations a cancer genome holds, which genes and pathways are altered across and within cancer types, how heterogeneous the cells in one tumor are, and how the pattern evolves during progression and treatment.

Samenvatting

Een tumorgenoom kan op meerdere niveaus gelezen worden: het hele genoom, alleen het exoom (de ~21 000 eiwitcoderende genen, goedkoper), kopieaantal (deleties, duplicaties, aneuploïdie), epigenetische merktekens, RNA (expressie) en eiwitten (massaspectrometrie), telkens vergeleken met normale cellen van dezelfde persoon. The Cancer Genome Atlas: >10 000 tumoren, 33 kankertypes; COSMIC: catalogus van somatische mutaties.

2. You can recognise an oncogene by its mutation pattern (Figure 2-24)

When you line up all the mutations found in one gene across many patients, oncogenes and tumor suppressors look different:

  • Oncogene: the same nucleotide change turns up again and again, at one or a few spots. Only a few possible changes can make a protein hyperactive, so selection keeps picking those. The syllabus example is PIK3CA, the gene for a subunit of PI 3-kinase (an enzyme in the growth-signal chain of section 4). Its mutations are missense mutations clustered in the kinase domain.
  • Tumor suppressor: truncating mutations that create stop codons, spread all over the gene. Inactivation is easy: a missense, nonsense or frameshift mutation almost anywhere will do. The example is Rb.
At the protein level

Only a few of all possible mutations in a coding sequence can activate a protein (you have to hit exactly the right spot in the right way). But inactivation can come from missense, nonsense or frameshift mutations almost anywhere. That is why oncogene mutations cluster and tumor-suppressor mutations scatter.

Samenvatting

Oncogen: steeds dezelfde missense-mutatie op enkele plekken (voorbeeld PIK3CA), want maar weinig veranderingen maken een eiwit hyperactief. Tumorsuppressor: truncerende mutaties (stopcodons) overal in het gen (voorbeeld Rb), want kapotmaken kan op veel manieren.

3. Hundreds of genes contribute to cancer (2.2.6)

Among the billions of somatic mutations now catalogued in cancer cells, how do we find the drivers? Most are passengers: they happened in the same unstable cell but are irrelevant. The main criterion is frequency: a driver mutation in a gene that matters is seen repeatedly, in many different patients with a particular cancer type; a passenger gives no advantage and is seen only rarely.

Compiling the data for all cancer types gives a catalogue of genes strongly suspected to be cancer-critical for at least one tumor type: about 300 genes, about 1 % of the genes in the human genome. They are amazingly diverse. Some encode the classical signaling and cell-cycle proteins you would expect, but others belong to surprising categories: metabolism, chromatin biology, RNA splicing, protein homeostasis (keeping proteins in balance) and cell differentiation. Changes in any of these processes can, in one tissue or another, help cells evolve cancerous properties. That sounds daunting, but the next section shows the complexity is smaller than it seems.

Samenvatting

Drivers herken je aan frequentie: steeds opnieuw in veel patiënten; passengers zijn zeldzaam. Zo'n 300 genen (~1 % van het genoom) zijn cancer-critical, van zeer uiteenlopende soorten: signalering en celcyclus, maar ook stofwisseling, chromatine, RNA-splicing, eiwithuishouding en differentiatie.

4. A handful of pathways are broken in almost every cancer (2.2.7)

Some genes, like Rb and Ras, are mutated in many cancers of many types. No surprise: they control fundamental processes of division and growth. But even these common culprits are mutated in well under half of individual cases. What is happening in the many cancers where Rb is intact or Ras is not mutated? The answer came from looking at pathways instead of single genes. Three names you need:

NameWhat it is
Rasa switch protein inside the cell that passes growth signals from receptors on the surface into the cell; a famous proto-oncogene
RTKreceptor tyrosine kinase: a receptor in the cell membrane that adds phosphate groups (to the amino acid tyrosine) when a growth factor binds it; the EGF receptor of page 2.5 is one
PI 3-kinasephosphoinositide 3-kinase: an enzyme further down the same signaling chain, which drives cell growth; PIK3CA codes for one of its subunits

The glioblastoma study

Glioblastoma is the most common human brain cancer (page 2.5). The genomes of tumor cells from 91 patients were analysed, and at least 79 genes were found mutated in more than one patient. Because the normal functions of most of these genes were known, they could be sorted into pathways. Three groups stood out, holding 21 of the recurrently mutated genes:

  1. the Rb pathway: Rb itself and the genes that directly regulate Rb; it governs the start of the cell-division cycle;
  2. the RTK / Ras / PI 3-kinase pathway, named after three of its core parts; it carries signals for cell growth and division from outside the cell to the inside;
  3. the p53 pathway: the response to stress and DNA damage (page 2.8).

Of the 91 glioblastomas, 74 % had identifiable mutations in all three pathways, and that percentage would be even higher if every upstream component were included. In other words: in almost every glioblastoma, the control of cell growth, the control of cell division and the control of responses to stress and DNA damage are all three disrupted.

One hit per pathway is enough

Strikingly, in a given tumor clone there is a strong tendency for no more than one gene per pathway to be mutated. If Rb itself is intact, some other part of the Rb pathway is hit, with the same effect. What matters for the tumor is that the control is disabled, not by which gene. Once a pathway is broken by one mutation, there is no further selection for a second mutation in the same pathway: it would give no extra advantage.

The same pattern appears in other cancers. In the major form of ovarian cancer: 67 % of patients had mutations in the Rb pathway, 45 % in the Ras / PI 3-kinase pathway (defined more narrowly than in the glioblastoma study) and more than 96 % in the p53 pathway. It seems these three fundamental controls are subverted in one way or another in virtually every type of cancer, although different tissues break them in different ways, through the different signaling pathways that relay signals to the core machinery.

Story from Alberts (background, not exam material)

The growth pathway explains an old observation. Tumor cells import glucose up to 100 times faster than their neighbours and burn it wastefully (the "Warburg effect", noticed by Otto Warburg a century ago). Alberts explains why: the PI 3-kinase chain, when switched on by mutation, drives the protein kinases Akt and mTOR, which push up glucose uptake and turn the sugar into building blocks for new proteins, DNA and membranes. The high glucose uptake is what makes tumors light up in whole-body PET scans. A frequent way to switch this chain on is losing PTEN, a phosphatase (an enzyme that removes phosphate groups) that normally counteracts PI 3-kinase.

Samenvatting

Zelfs Rb en Ras zijn in minder dan de helft van de gevallen gemuteerd; kijk daarom naar routes (pathways). Glioblastoom (91 patiënten, 79 genen): drie groepen springen eruit: de Rb-route (start van de celcyclus), de RTK/Ras/PI 3-kinase-route (groeisignalen van buiten naar binnen) en de p53-route (stress en DNA-schade); 74 % had alle drie. Eén gemuteerd gen per route volstaat: is de route kapot, dan is er geen selectie meer voor een tweede mutatie in dezelfde route. Eierstokkanker: 67 %, 45 %, >96 %. Vrijwel elke kanker breekt deze drie controles: celcyclus, celproliferatie, celöverleving.

Learning objectives: "the student explains the role of various proto-oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes in the development of cancer" and "can precisely explain how mutations in a proto-oncogene and in a tumor suppressor gene have different effects at the protein level".

7. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

What is the exome, and why sequence only that?
The roughly 21 000 protein-coding genes of the genome. Sequencing only the exome is much cheaper than the whole genome and still finds mutations that change the amino acid sequence or prevent synthesis of a protein.
Besides the DNA sequence, what else can be scanned in a tumor?
Copy number (deletions, duplications, chromosome gains or losses), epigenetic changes such as methylation, RNA levels (gene expression, including regulatory non-coding RNAs) and protein levels by mass spectrometry; always compared with normal cells, ideally from the same tissue and person.
What are The Cancer Genome Atlas and COSMIC?
The Cancer Genome Atlas is a consortium (US National Institutes of Health) that applied these methods to more than 10 000 tumors of 33 cancer types. COSMIC is the free Catalog of Somatic Mutations in Cancer.
How do you recognise an oncogene from its mutations across patients?
The same nucleotide change turns up repeatedly at one or a few spots, usually missense mutations (example: PIK3CA). Only a few changes can make a protein hyperactive.
How do you recognise a tumor suppressor gene from its mutations?
Truncating mutations (stop codons from nonsense or frameshift changes) scattered over the whole gene (example: Rb). Inactivating a protein is possible almost anywhere.
How do you tell a driver mutation from a passenger?
By frequency: a driver is seen repeatedly in many patients with that cancer type; a passenger, giving no advantage, is seen only rarely.
How many cancer-critical genes are there, and what kinds?
About 300, around 1 % of human genes. Besides classical signaling and cell-cycle genes they include genes for metabolism, chromatin biology, RNA splicing, protein homeostasis and cell differentiation.
Name the three pathways found in the glioblastoma study and what each controls.
The Rb pathway (start of the cell cycle), the RTK/Ras/PI 3-kinase pathway (transmits growth and division signals from outside the cell to the inside) and the p53 pathway (response to stress and DNA damage). 74 % of the 91 tumors had mutations in all three.
Why is there usually only one mutated gene per pathway in a tumor clone?
Because what matters is disabling the control, not which gene does it. Once one mutation has broken the pathway, a second mutation in it gives no extra advantage, so it is not selected for.
Give the ovarian-cancer numbers.
Rb pathway 67 %, Ras/PI 3-kinase pathway 45 % (narrowly defined), p53 pathway more than 96 %.
What are Ras, an RTK and PI 3-kinase?
Ras: a switch protein that passes growth signals from surface receptors into the cell. RTK: receptor tyrosine kinase, a membrane receptor that adds phosphate groups when a growth factor binds. PI 3-kinase: an enzyme further down that chain that drives growth; PIK3CA codes for a subunit of it.

8. To memorize

Learn these by heart
  • Scans: whole genome, exome (~21 000 genes), copy number, methylation, RNA, proteins; always versus a normal control.
  • The Cancer Genome Atlas: >10 000 tumors, 33 types. COSMIC: catalog of somatic mutations.
  • Oncogene pattern: same missense mutation at a few spots (PIK3CA). Tumor suppressor pattern: truncating mutations anywhere (Rb).
  • Reason: activating is hard (few spots), inactivating is easy (anywhere).
  • Driver = frequent across patients; passenger = rare. About 300 cancer-critical genes = 1 % of the genome.
  • Three pathways: Rb = cell-cycle entry, RTK/Ras/PI 3-kinase = growth signals in, p53 = stress and DNA damage.
  • Glioblastoma: 91 tumors, 79 genes, 74 % all three pathways.
  • Ovarian cancer: Rb 67 %, Ras/PI 3-K 45 %, p53 > 96 %.
  • One mutated gene per pathway; no further selection once the pathway is broken.
  • Short names: cell cycle, cell proliferation, cell survival: disturbed in virtually every cancer.

9. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. Sequencing only the exome of a tumor means reading:
    • the whole genome
    • only the protein-coding genes
    • only the RNA
    • only the methylated regions
    Answer

    b. The exome is the roughly 21 000 protein-coding genes. It is cheaper than the whole genome and catches mutations that change or abolish a protein.

  2. Why are tumor cells always compared with normal cells, ideally from the same person?
    Answer

    To see which changes are somatic, specific to the tumor, rather than normal differences between people. Cells from the same tissue and person are the best control.

  3. In a gene, mutations from 200 patients all turn out to be the same amino-acid change at two spots. Oncogene or tumor suppressor?
    Answer

    Oncogene. Only a few specific changes can make a protein hyperactive, so selection keeps picking the same missense mutations (like PIK3CA).

  4. In another gene the mutations are stop codons spread over the whole length. Oncogene or tumor suppressor?
    Answer

    Tumor suppressor. Truncating mutations anywhere destroy the protein, which is what a loss-of-function gene needs (like Rb).

  5. What is a truncating mutation? Name two kinds.
    Answer

    A mutation that cuts the protein short: a nonsense mutation (a codon becomes a stop) or a frameshift (insertion or deletion that shifts the reading frame, soon hitting a stop).

  6. How do researchers decide that a mutation is a driver and not a passenger?
    Answer

    By frequency: drivers in a gene that matters are found repeatedly in many patients with that cancer type; passengers give no advantage and are rare.

  7. About how many cancer-critical genes are there, and which fraction of the genome is that?
    • about 30, 0.1 %
    • about 300, 1 %
    • about 3000, 10 %
    • all 21 000
    Answer

    b. Around 300 genes, about 1 % of human genes, of surprisingly diverse function (signaling, cell cycle, metabolism, chromatin, splicing, protein homeostasis, differentiation).

  8. Name the three pathways and what each controls.
    Answer

    Rb pathway: start of the cell-division cycle. RTK/Ras/PI 3-kinase pathway: growth and division signals from outside to inside. p53 pathway: response to stress and DNA damage.

  9. In the glioblastoma study, what fraction of tumors had mutations in all three pathways?
    Answer

    74 % of 91 tumors (79 genes were mutated in more than one patient; 21 of those fell into the three pathways). Counting unknown upstream components, the fraction would be even higher.

  10. A tumor has no mutation in Rb itself. Does that mean its cell-cycle control is intact?
    Answer

    Usually not: in such tumors there is generally a mutation in another component of the Rb pathway, with the same biological effect. What matters is the disabled control, not the gene.

  11. Why does a tumor clone rarely carry two mutated genes in the same pathway?
    Answer

    Once one mutation has disabled the pathway, a second one gives no extra advantage, so there is no selection for it.

  12. Which pathway is mutated in more than 96 % of the major form of ovarian cancer?
    • Rb pathway
    • Ras / PI 3-kinase pathway
    • p53 pathway
    • none; ovarian cancer is different
    Answer

    c. p53 pathway > 96 %, Rb pathway 67 %, Ras/PI 3-kinase 45 %. Most cases break all three controls.

  13. What is Ras, in one sentence?
    Answer

    A switch protein inside the cell that passes growth signals from receptors on the surface (such as RTKs) into the cell; mutated in many cancers, a proto-oncogene.
