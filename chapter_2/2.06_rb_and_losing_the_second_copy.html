2.6 The first tumor suppressor: Rb, retinoblastoma and the six ways to lose the second copy

In one sentence: the Rb gene was discovered through a rare childhood eye cancer, it is a brake on the cell cycle, and a cell only becomes cancerous when both copies are gone, the second one usually through a sloppy, large-scale accident rather than a precise mutation.

Page 2.5 said that a tumor suppressor acts recessively: both copies must be lost. This page tells the story of how that was first understood (the retinoblastoma story, syllabus 2.2.3) and then lists the ways a cell can lose its remaining good copy (2.2.4, Figure 2-23). The learning objectives ask you to "describe the main characteristics of the Rb and TP53 gene, its significance in cancer development and its biological mechanism of action"; this page is the Rb half, page 2.8 the p53 half.

1. Why a lost gene needs another search strategy (2.2.3)

An oncogene can be found by its effect when it is added: put tumor DNA into normal cells and look for cells that start behaving like cancer cells. That trick does not work for a tumor suppressor, because you cannot add something that is simply not there. Finding a gene that has been inactivated needs a different approach: look for what is missing in the cancer cell. The key insight came from a rare special case, as often in biology, and it turned out to reveal a gene of general importance.

Samenvatting

Een oncogen vind je door het toe te voegen aan normale cellen en te kijken of ze zich als kankercellen gaan gedragen. Een tumorsuppressor is weg, dus je moet zoeken naar wat ontbreekt. De sleutel kwam van een zeldzaam speciaal geval.

2. Retinoblastoma: the special case

Retinoblastoma is a rare cancer of the retina (netvlies, the light-sensing layer at the back of the eye) that occurs in childhood, in about 1 child in 20 000. The tumors develop from neural precursor cells (cells that are still going to become nerve cells) in the immature retina. What makes it special: the retinal cells become cancerous with an unusually small number of mutations. There are two forms:

FormTumorsWhat the cells carry
Hereditarymultiple tumors, arising independently, usually in both eyesevery somatic cell of the child already has one deleted or inactivated copy of Rb, inherited from a parent. A tumor starts wherever a retinal cell also loses its remaining good copy by a somatic event. That is one accident, so it happens in several cells.
Nonhereditaryone tumor in one eyethe noncancerous cells have two good copies. The tumor cells have lost both copies through two independent somatic events in the same cell lineage. Two rare accidents in one line of cells: very rare, so one tumor.

How the gene was found: a few patients had a visibly abnormal karyotype with a deletion of a specific band on chromosome 13; inherited, that deletion predisposes to the disease (predispose = make more likely). The same region was also deleted in tumor cells of some patients with the nonhereditary form. So a critical gene must sit there, and the cancer must be caused by losing it. Mapping the deletion identified the Rb gene (named after retinoblastoma).

The logic to remember

Hereditary: the first hit is already in every cell, so only one somatic hit is needed → several tumors, both eyes, early. Nonhereditary: two somatic hits must happen in the same cell lineage → very rare, one tumor, one eye. This is the two-hit idea behind every tumor suppressor (compare Knudson's multiple hits, page 2.2).

Samenvatting

Retinoblastoom: zeldzame kanker van het netvlies bij kinderen (1 op 20 000), met opvallend weinig mutaties nodig. Erfelijke vorm: één slechte Rb-kopie in álle cellen (geërfd), een tumor ontstaat waar een netvliescel ook de goede kopie verliest → meerdere tumoren, beide ogen. Niet-erfelijke vorm: twee onafhankelijke somatische gebeurtenissen in één cellijn → zeldzaam, één tumor, één oog. Het gen werd gevonden via een deletie op chromosoom 13.

3. What Rb does, and where else it is lost

Rb is not only an eye-cancer gene. It is also missing in several common sporadic cancers: carcinomas of the lung, breast and bladder. Those cancers need a more complex series of genetic changes than retinoblastoma and appear much later in life, but in all of them loss of Rb function is frequently a major step toward malignancy.

The Rb gene encodes the Rb protein, a universal regulator of the cell cycle in almost all cells of the body. It is one of the main brakes on progress through the cell-division cycle: it holds the cell in G1 until the proper signals arrive, and when it is lost the cell can enter the cycle when it should not. (That is why page 2.7 calls the "Rb pathway" the control of cell-cycle entry.)

Exam link

Example exam question 7 asks which gene is called the "guardian angel of the genome". One option says "the RB1 gene, because it regulates the cell cycle by preventing cells from entering S phase inappropriately". That description of RB1 (another name for Rb) is correct biology, but it is the wrong answer to this question: Rb guards the cell-cycle gate, not the genome. The guardian of the genome is p53, which repairs DNA, halts the cycle and induces apoptosis when the DNA is damaged (page 2.8).

Samenvatting

Rb ontbreekt ook in veel voorkomende kankers (long, borst, blaas). Het Rb-eiwit is een universele rem op de celcyclus: het houdt de cel in G1 tot de juiste signalen er zijn. Examenvraag 7: Rb bewaakt de celcyclus-poort, maar de "bewaker van het genoom" is p53.

4. Six ways to lose the remaining good copy (2.2.4, Figure 2-23)

Begin state: a cell with one bad copy of a tumor suppressor (say Rb) and one good copy. It behaves as a normal, healthy cell. Goal of the question: how can it lose the good copy too? The syllabus makes a useful distinction:

  • The first copy is typically lost by a precise, small event: a small chromosomal deletion, or a point mutation from a random replication error.
  • The second copy is more often lost by a less specific, large-scale mechanism, the kind that happens in cells that have already become genetically unstable (page 2.3).

Figure 2-23 lists six ways. Two change the DNA letters locally, three move or lose whole chromosome regions, one is epigenetic:

WayWhat happens in plain wordsKind
1 · Deletionthe piece of chromosome holding the good copy is cut out and lostDNA sequence, small
2 · Point mutationone letter changes in the good copy and breaks the proteinDNA sequence, small
3 · Chromosome lossthe whole chromosome carrying the good copy is lost through a segregation error in mitosis (aneuploidy, as in chapter 1); the cell keeps only the chromosome with the bad copylarge-scale
4 · Mitotic recombinationduring mitosis the two homologous chromosomes exchange pieces, as in crossing-over; a daughter cell can receive the bad copy twice and the good copy not at alllarge-scale
5 · Gene conversionduring that recombination the good copy is "overwritten" by the sequence of the bad copy, so both copies now read the mutant version (conversion = being changed into)large-scale
6 · Epigenetic silencingthe C's of CG pairs in the promoter of the good copy become methylated; the gene is switched off permanently and the methylation is copied to all daughter cells. The DNA letters are intact, but the gene is silentepigenetic
Story from Alberts (background, not exam material)

Viruses can take the Rb brake out without any mutation. Human papillomaviruses (HPV), the cause of cervical cancer, carry two genes, E6 and E7, whose proteins bind and inactivate the host's Rb and p53 proteins. Both brakes are off, so the infected basal cells keep dividing. The HPV vaccine prevents the infection and therefore most cervical cancers.

Samenvatting

De eerste kopie gaat meestal verloren door een klein, precies ongeluk (kleine deletie, puntmutatie); de tweede door een groot, onspecifiek mechanisme in een al onstabiele cel. Zes manieren: (1) deletie, (2) puntmutatie, (3) verlies van het hele chromosoom door een segregatiefout, (4) mitotische recombinatie (stukken gewisseld: slechte kopie twee keer), (5) genconversie (goede kopie overschreven door de slechte), (6) epigenetische uitschakeling: methylering van de C's in CG-paren van de promoter, erfelijk voor dochtercellen, letters intact maar gen stil.

7. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

Why can a tumor suppressor gene not be found with the same method as an oncogene?
An oncogene is found by adding tumor DNA to normal cells and watching them transform. A tumor suppressor is inactivated, absent; you cannot add something that is not there. You have to look for what is missing in the cancer cell.
What is retinoblastoma?
A rare cancer of the retina in children (about 1 in 20 000), arising from neural precursor cells, caused by an unusually small number of mutations. One form is hereditary, the other is not.
How do the hereditary and nonhereditary forms differ in what you see?
Hereditary: several tumors arising independently, usually in both eyes. Nonhereditary: a single tumor in one eye.
Explain that difference with the two copies of Rb.
In the hereditary form every cell already lacks one working copy, so a tumor starts wherever one retinal cell loses its only good copy: one accident, which happens in several cells. In the nonhereditary form a cell lineage must lose both copies by two independent somatic events: so rare that only one tumor forms.
How was the Rb gene located?
Some patients had a visible deletion of a band on chromosome 13, which predisposes to the disease when inherited; the same region was deleted in tumor cells of nonhereditary patients. Mapping the deletion identified the gene.
What does the Rb protein do?
It is a universal regulator of the cell cycle in almost all cells, one of the main brakes on progress through the division cycle (entry into the cycle from G1). Losing it lets cells enter the cycle inappropriately.
Is Rb only important in the eye?
No. The gene is also missing in common sporadic cancers such as carcinomas of lung, breast and bladder, where its loss is frequently a major step toward malignancy.
How is the first copy of a tumor suppressor usually lost, and how the second?
The first by a precise small event (small deletion or point mutation from a replication error). The second more often by a less specific, large-scale mechanism typical of genetically unstable cells: losing the whole chromosome, mitotic recombination, gene conversion.
Name the six ways of inactivating the remaining good copy.
Deletion, point mutation, loss of the whole chromosome by mis-segregation, mitotic recombination, gene conversion during recombination, and epigenetic silencing by promoter methylation.
What is epigenetic silencing of a tumor suppressor?
The C nucleotides in CG sequences of its promoter become methylated in a heritable way. The DNA letters stay intact, but the gene is irreversibly switched off in that cell and all its progeny.
What is the difference between mitotic recombination and gene conversion here?
Mitotic recombination exchanges pieces between the two homologous chromosomes, so a daughter cell can end up with the mutant copy on both. Gene conversion is the overwriting of the good copy by the sequence of the bad copy during that recombination.

9. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. A child has retinoblastoma in both eyes, with several separate tumors. Which form is this most likely, and why?
    Answer

    Hereditary. Every cell already carries one bad Rb copy, so only one more accident is needed per tumor; that happens in several retinal cells, in both eyes.

  2. Why is the nonhereditary form so rare?
    Answer

    Because two independent somatic events must inactivate both Rb copies in the same cell lineage. Two rare accidents in one line of cells: it happens in about 1 in 30 000 people, giving one tumor in one eye.

  3. Which chromosome carries Rb, and how did that help to find the gene?
    • chromosome 9
    • chromosome 13
    • chromosome 21
    • the X chromosome
    Answer

    b. Some patients had a visible deletion of a band on chromosome 13, also found in tumor cells of nonhereditary patients. Mapping that deletion located the gene.

  4. True or false: "A cell with one inactivated copy of Rb already proliferates too much."
    Answer

    False. With one good copy the cell makes working Rb protein and behaves as a normal, healthy cell. It is predisposed, but the brake only fails when the second copy is lost too (recessive).

  5. What does the Rb protein do in a normal cell?
    Answer

    It is a universal regulator of the cell cycle, one of the main brakes on progress through the cycle: it holds the cell at the entry (G1) until proper signals arrive.

  6. Name three common cancers in which Rb is also lost.
    Answer

    Carcinomas of the lung, breast and bladder. They arise through a longer series of changes and later in life, but Rb loss is frequently a major step.

  7. Why does finding a tumor suppressor need a different strategy from finding an oncogene?
    Answer

    An oncogene shows its effect when added to normal cells. A tumor suppressor is absent in the cancer cell, and you cannot add what is not there; you must look for what is missing.

  8. Which of these loses the second copy without changing any DNA letter?
    • point mutation
    • gene conversion
    • promoter methylation
    • deletion
    Answer

    c. Methylation of the C's in CG pairs of the promoter is epigenetic: the sequence is intact but the gene is silenced, and the silencing is inherited by daughter cells.

  9. How can a segregation error in mitosis remove the good copy?
    Answer

    The whole chromosome carrying the good copy is not passed to the daughter cell (nondisjunction, as in chapter 1). That cell keeps only the homolog with the bad copy: no working Rb.

  10. What is mitotic recombination, and why is it dangerous here?
    Answer

    An exchange of pieces between the two homologous chromosomes during mitosis. A daughter cell can then receive the region with the bad copy from both homologs, and the good copy from neither.

  11. What does "gene conversion" mean?
    Answer

    During recombination the good copy is overwritten by the sequence of the bad copy, so both copies now read the mutant version.

  12. Why is the second copy usually lost by a sloppy, large event rather than by a precise mutation?
    Answer

    Because by then the cell is often already genetically unstable, so large accidents (losing a chromosome, mis-recombination) are common; they hit the whole region at once and are far more likely than a second point mutation at the same gene.

  13. Exam question 7 offers "RB1, because it prevents cells from entering S phase inappropriately" as the guardian of the genome. What is right and what is wrong about that option?
    Answer

    The description of RB1 is right: it is the cell-cycle brake. But it is the wrong answer: the "guardian of the genome" is p53, which reacts to DNA damage by repair, cell-cycle arrest or apoptosis (page 2.8).
