2.1 What cancer is: cells that break the social rules

In one sentence: cancer is a group of cells that keeps dividing when it should not and that invades the territory of other cells; a growth that stays in place is benign, a growth that invades is malignant and can spread (metastasis).

Chapter 2 is the longest chapter of the course, and its learning objectives are precise: explain benign, malignant, metastasis, proto-oncogene, oncogene, tumor suppressor gene, the multiple hit theory of Knudson, the hallmarks of cancer and intratumor heterogeneity; explain the roles of the genes Rb and TP53; and pick, from a list of mutations, the ones that make an oncogene. This first page gives the vocabulary that every later page uses. Three of the fifteen questions of the example exam are about exactly this page: the matching question with "malignant, sarcoma, primary tumor, tumor progression, benign, senescence", the question on angiogenesis, and the question "which alteration could make a cell proliferate more than it should".

1. The body is a society of cells (syllabus 2.1)

The syllabus starts with a picture: an animal's body works like a society or an ecosystem (a community of living things that depend on each other). Its members are cells. They divide, and they organise themselves into tissues. But this society is odd: the rule is self-sacrifice, not "survival of the fittest". Every somatic cell lives only to support the germ cells, because only sperm and eggs go on to the next generation. That is not a loss for the somatic cell: it has the same genome as the germ cells, so by helping them it still passes copies of its own genes on.

Free-living cells such as bacteria or yeast compete with each other. The cells of a multicellular body must collaborate. To coordinate, they send, receive and interpret an elaborate set of extracellular signals (signals from outside the cell) that act as social controls: they tell a cell whether to rest, grow, divide, differentiate (specialise) or die. A normal cell behaves "socially": it does what the body needs.

The balance of two kinds of signals (Figure 2-1)

  • Growth factors bind to receptors on the cell surface and promote growth, division and survival. They switch on signalling pathways (chains of proteins inside the cell that pass the message on) that push the cell through the cell cycle. Needed for tissue growth and repair.
  • Growth-inhibiting factors do the opposite: they stop the cell cycle, so that cells do not divide uncontrollably. They keep the tissue in homeostasis (homeostase: a stable state, where cell birth and cell death are in balance) and keep its architecture healthy.

Figure 2-2 of the syllabus is the cell-cycle control points from BIO01 chapter 7: the G1, G2 and M checkpoints. Cancer is, in the end, a failure of these controls.

2. One mutant cell can become a clone that takes over

Begin state: a human body of more than 1013 cells (read: ten to the power thirteen, 10 000 000 000 000). Every day, billions of these cells experience a mutation. Most mutations do no harm. The dangerous case is a mutation that gives one cell a selective advantage: it grows and divides a little more vigorously, or survives a little more readily, than its neighbours. That cell becomes the founder of a growing mutant clone (a clone = all the descendants of one cell, genetically identical to it, kloon).

Over time the clone goes through repeated rounds of mutation, competition and natural selection, exactly the process that drives the evolution of species, but now among the somatic cells of one body and within one human life span. The syllabus calls this cancer as a microevolutionary process. Cancer is a disease in which one mutant clone prospers at the expense of its neighbours; in the end, as the clone evolves and spreads, it can destroy the whole cellular society.

Keep this picture

Cancer = Darwinian evolution inside the body. The three ingredients are the same as in nature: variation (mutations), heredity (a mutation is copied into all descendants by mitosis) and selection (the cell that divides faster or dies less takes over). Page 2.2 follows this process step by step.

Herhaalde rondes van mutatie, concurrentie en natuurlijke selectie maken het erger: kanker is micro-evolutie binnen één lichaam.

3. The two defining properties of a cancer cell (2.1.1)

Cancer cells are defined by two heritable properties (heritable = passed on to their daughter cells):

  1. They reproduce in defiance of the normal restraints on cell growth and division.
  2. They invade and colonize territories normally reserved for other cells.

The combination is what makes cancer dangerous. The words that follow all hang on these two properties:

WordMeaning
tumor, neoplasma mass of cells made by an abnormal cell that went through repeated, inappropriate rounds of growth and division; neoplasm literally means "new growth" (gezwel)
benignthe tumor cells have not become invasive: the mass stays in place. Removing or destroying it locally usually cures it completely (goedaardig)
malignantthe cells have acquired the ability to invade the surrounding tissue. Only a malignant tumor is a true cancer (kwaadaardig)
invasivenessbreaking loose and growing into neighbouring tissue; the essential characteristic of cancer cells
metastasis (plural metastases)a secondary tumor at another site of the body, founded by cancer cells that entered blood or lymphatic vessels (uitzaaiing). Metastases usually kill the patient, by making a vital organ fail

The more widely a cancer has spread, the harder it is to eradicate. A benign tumor can be cut out; metastases in many organs cannot.

4. Naming cancers by the tissue they come from

Cancers are traditionally classified by the tissue and cell type they arise from. First some tissue words:

  • Epithelium (epitheel): the sheets of cells that cover the body surface and line all its tubes and cavities: skin, gut lining, airways, glands. Epithelial cells sit on a basal lamina.
  • Connective tissue (bindweefsel): the supporting tissue between organs: fibres, bone, cartilage, fat, and the cells that make them.
  • Hemopoietic cells (bloedvormende cellen): the white blood cells and the precursor cells in the bone marrow that make them.
NameArises fromHow common
carcinomaepithelial cellsby far the most common: about 85 % of cancers
myeloma, leukemia, lymphomawhite blood cells and their precursors (hemopoietic cells)the next most common group
sarcomaconnective tissue or muscle cellsmuch less common

Why are carcinomas so common? Two reasons from the syllabus: most of the normal cell turnover (cells dying and being replaced) in an adult happens in epithelia, and the cell types that go through the most division cycles have the greatest chance of accumulating the several mutations needed for cancer. In addition, epithelia are the tissues most exposed to physical and chemical damage (sunlight on the skin, smoke in the airways, food in the gut).

Benign names and malignant names go in pairs

An adenoma is a benign epithelial tumor with a glandular organisation (gland = klier, a structure that makes and releases a substance; "adeno" means gland). Its malignant counterpart is an adenocarcinoma. Figure 2-4 above shows both.

Cancers keep traits of the cell they came from

Two skin examples. A basal-cell carcinoma comes from a keratinocyte stem cell (a keratinocyte is the ordinary skin cell that makes the protein keratin; a stem cell is a cell that keeps dividing to renew a tissue). These cancer cells generally keep making their cytokeratin filaments (the protein fibres of a skin cell). A melanoma comes from a pigment cell of the skin and often keeps making pigment granules. And the two are very different diseases: basal-cell carcinomas are only locally invasive, rarely metastasize and are readily cured by surgery or local irradiation; melanomas can become much more malignant, often form metastases and are then frequently fatal.

7. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

Why does the syllabus call the body a society of cells?
Because the cells must collaborate rather than compete: somatic cells sacrifice themselves for the germ cells, and extracellular signals act as social controls that tell each cell when to rest, grow, divide, differentiate or die.
What are the two kinds of signals in Figure 2-1 and what is their balance for?
Growth factors (promote growth, division and survival) and growth-inhibiting factors (stop the cell cycle). Their balance keeps tissue homeostasis and prevents tumor formation.
What is the difference between quiescence, dormancy and senescence?
Quiescence: reversible rest (G0), the cell re-enters the cycle on a signal. Dormancy: a prolonged reversible low-activity state in bad conditions. Senescence: an irreversible stop of division, triggered by stress or damage; the cell stays alive. Senescence protects against tumors.
What are the two defining properties of cancer cells?
They reproduce in defiance of the normal restraints on growth and division, and they invade and colonize territory reserved for other cells.
When is a tumor benign, and when is it a true cancer?
Benign as long as its cells are not invasive; it stays in place and local removal usually cures it. It is a true cancer (malignant) when the cells can invade the surrounding tissue.
What is a metastasis and why does it matter?
A secondary tumor at another site, founded by cancer cells that broke loose and travelled through blood or lymphatic vessels. Metastases usually kill the patient by making a vital organ fail, and a widely spread cancer is very hard to eradicate.
How does a single mutant cell become a cancer?
A mutation gives it a selective advantage; it founds a growing clone; repeated rounds of mutation, competition and natural selection among somatic cells make the clone more and more abnormal: microevolution inside the body.
Name the main cancer classes by tissue of origin.
Carcinoma from epithelial cells (about 85 %); myeloma, leukemia and lymphoma from white blood cells and their precursors; sarcoma from connective tissue or muscle; cancers of the nervous system (rare).
Why are carcinomas so much more common than sarcomas?
Most cell turnover in adults happens in epithelia, so these cells go through the most division cycles and accumulate the most mutations, and epithelia are the most exposed to physical and chemical damage.
What is the difference between an adenoma and an adenocarcinoma?
Both are glandular epithelial tumors. The adenoma is benign and stays inside the basal lamina; the adenocarcinoma is malignant, breaks through the basal lamina and destroys the tissue.
Compare basal-cell carcinoma and melanoma.
Basal-cell carcinoma comes from a keratinocyte stem cell, keeps making cytokeratin, is only locally invasive and is cured by surgery or local irradiation. Melanoma comes from a pigment cell, often keeps making pigment, often metastasizes and is then frequently fatal.
Which of these makes a cell proliferate too much: a lost apoptosis protein, a broken checkpoint protein, too much growth-factor receptor, an activated oncogene?
All four. Less death, no brake, more "divide" signal and an overactive growth gene each tip the balance toward more cells.

8. To memorize

Learn these by heart
  • Body = society of cells; somatic cells serve the germ cells; social controls = extracellular signals.
  • Growth factors push, growth-inhibiting factors brake; balance = homeostasis.
  • Fates: proliferation, differentiation, maturation, quiescence (reversible, G0), dormancy (long, reversible), senescence (irreversible stop, alive), apoptosis (death).
  • 1013 cells, billions of mutations a day; a selective advantage → a mutant clone → microevolution.
  • Cancer cell: divides despite restraints + invades other territory.
  • Benign = not invasive, stays in place, local removal cures. Malignant = invasive = true cancer.
  • Metastasis = secondary tumor elsewhere via blood or lymph; metastases usually kill.
  • Basal lamina = the boundary; benign stays inside, malignant breaks through.
  • Carcinoma epithelium 85 %; sarcoma connective tissue/muscle; leukemia, lymphoma, myeloma white blood cells.
  • Adenoma (benign, gland) ↔ adenocarcinoma (malignant).
  • Basal-cell carcinoma: local, curable. Melanoma: metastasizes, often fatal.

9. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. A tumor is found in a gland and completely removed; it had not grown through the basal lamina. Benign or malignant, and what is its name?
    Answer

    Benign, an adenoma. Not invasive means not a true cancer; local removal usually cures it. If it had broken through, it would be an adenocarcinoma.

  2. Which property makes a tumor a true cancer?
    • it is large
    • it grows fast
    • its cells invade the surrounding tissue
    • it is found in an epithelium
    Answer

    c. Malignant = invasive. Size, speed and tissue type do not decide it; a large, fast benign tumor is still benign.

  3. Define senescence in one sentence, and say how it differs from quiescence.
    Answer

    Senescence is an irreversible growth arrest, often triggered by stress or damage, that stops a cell from ever dividing again while it stays alive. Quiescence is reversible rest (G0).

  4. What is a metastasis?
    Answer

    A secondary tumor at a different site, founded by cancer cells that broke loose, entered blood or lymphatic vessels and settled elsewhere. These usually cause death by making a vital organ fail.

  5. A cancer arises from muscle cells. What is it called?
    • carcinoma
    • sarcoma
    • lymphoma
    • adenoma
    Answer

    b. Sarcomas arise from connective tissue or muscle. Carcinoma = epithelium, lymphoma = white blood cells, adenoma is benign.

  6. About what fraction of human cancers are carcinomas, and why?
    Answer

    About 85 %. Epithelia have the most cell turnover (most divisions, so most chance to accumulate mutations) and the most exposure to physical and chemical damage.

  7. True or false: "A somatic cell that sacrifices itself for the germ cells loses its genes."
    Answer

    False. Somatic cells have the same genome as the germ cells, so by supporting the germ cells they still pass copies of their own genes on.

  8. What do growth-inhibiting factors do, and what happens when they are ignored?
    Answer

    They stop the cell cycle so that cells do not divide uncontrollably, keeping the tissue in homeostasis. A cell that ignores them keeps dividing: one of the ways a tumor starts.

  9. Explain "cancer is a microevolutionary process".
    Answer

    Among the body's cells there is variation (mutations), heredity (mitosis copies the mutation into all descendants) and selection (a cell with a selective advantage out-divides its neighbours). Repeated rounds of mutation, competition and selection turn a mutant clone into a cancer, within one human life span.

  10. Which of these two skin cancers often metastasizes: basal-cell carcinoma or melanoma?
    Answer

    Melanoma (from pigment cells). Basal-cell carcinoma (from keratinocyte stem cells) is only locally invasive and is cured by surgery or local irradiation.

  11. What is the basal lamina and why does it matter in Figure 2-4?
    Answer

    A thin protein sheet under an epithelium that marks the boundary of the tissue. A benign tumor stays inside it; a malignant tumor breaks through it, which is the first step of invasion.

  12. A protein that normally triggers apoptosis is mutated and no longer works. Could this contribute to cancer?
    Answer

    Yes. Fewer cells die, so the number of cells rises even if division is normal. The exam lists this together with a broken checkpoint brake, an overexpressed growth-factor receptor and an activated oncogene: all give excess proliferation.

  13. Give the pair of words for a glandular tumor: benign and malignant.
    Answer

    Adenoma (benign) and adenocarcinoma (malignant). "Adeno" = gland, "carcinoma" = cancer of epithelium.
