2.4 The tumor microenvironment and metastasis

In one sentence: a tumor is not only cancer cells but also the normal cells it recruits and manipulates around it, and the deadliest thing cancer cells learn is to leave this home, travel through the blood or lymph and found colonies in other organs, which very few of them manage.

This page closes the first half of the chapter (2.1.6 to 2.1.8). Two ideas: the microenvironment (the cells and matrix directly around the tumor) is an active partner of the cancer, and metastasis is a chain of difficult steps of which the last, colonization, is the hardest. The exam asks what angiogenesis does for a tumor; the word is explained here and on page 2.3.

1. The tumor microenvironment: the stroma is not a bystander (2.1.6)

The cancer cells in a tumor carry the dangerous mutations. But the other cells of the tumor, especially those of the supporting connective tissue, the stroma, are far from passive. A tumor develops through two-way communication between cancer cells and stroma, just as the normal development of an epithelial organ depends on communication between epithelial cells and mesenchymal cells (the connective-tissue cells of an embryo).

What the stroma is made of (Figure 2-16): the framework of the tumor, like normal connective tissue, with

  • fibroblasts: the connective-tissue cells that make the fibres of the matrix (in a tumor often called cancer-associated fibroblasts);
  • inflammatory white blood cells: immune cells that have moved in (inflammation = ontsteking, the body's reaction to damage or infection: swelling, redness, immune cells arriving);
  • endothelial cells that form blood and lymphatic vessels (lymphatic vessels carry lymph, a clear fluid that drains from the tissues, lymfe), with their attending pericytes (vascular smooth muscle cells: contractile cells wrapped around small vessels);
  • the extracellular matrix between all of them.

Only the cancer cells are thought to carry the mutations; the stromal cells are genetically normal, but their behaviour is changed.

How tumor and stroma shape each other

As a carcinoma progresses, the cancer cells change the stroma by secreting signal proteins that alter the behaviour of the stromal cells, and proteolytic enzymes (proteases: enzymes that cut proteins) that remodel the extracellular matrix. The stromal cells answer by secreting signal proteins that stimulate cancer-cell growth and division, and proteases that remodel the matrix further. So tumor and stroma evolve together, "like weeds and the ecosystem they invade", and the tumor can become dependent on its particular stromal cells.

The immune system: destroyer and helper

The immune cells in the stroma could destroy the tumor if they recognised it as abnormal tissue, but they can also promote it by giving signals that stimulate cancer-cell proliferation. The tumor manipulates the immune system in at least two ways:

  1. It invokes an inflammatory reaction, like the one that follows damage to normal tissue, and so acquires the stroma it needs. Tumors have been likened to unhealed wounds: they trigger some of the same responses, including an increase in the permeability of nearby blood vessels (permeable = letting things through), which lets signalling molecules flow in and out, and the deposition of extracellular matrix. Tumors also stimulate angiogenesis, the formation of new blood vessels, which keeps the tumor alive as it grows larger and its interior becomes hypoxic (hypoxia = too little oxygen).
  2. It builds an immunosuppressive microenvironment: it blocks the activation of the white blood cells that could destroy it. Treatments that override this suppression have recently become powerful tools in cancer therapy.
Exam link

Example exam question 4: angiogenesis supplies blood to the tumor cells. The reason is here: a tumor's interior becomes hypoxic as it grows, and new vessels bring oxygen and nutrients.

Story from Alberts (background, not exam material)

The immunosuppressive trick has a name: cancer cells put proteins such as PDL1 on their surface that bind an inhibitory receptor (PD1) on the T cells that could kill them. Antibodies that block PD1 or PDL1, "immune checkpoint inhibitors", release the brake; a large fraction of patients with metastatic melanoma go into remission for years, and tumors with many mutations (more foreign-looking proteins) respond best.

2. Metastasis: surviving in a foreign land (2.1.7)

To kill us, cancer cells generally need to spread and multiply at new sites: metastasis. It is the most deadly and the least understood aspect of cancer, responsible for 90 % of cancer deaths. Once spread through the body, a cancer is almost impossible to eradicate by surgery or local irradiation. Metastasis is a multistep process:

  1. Invade local tissue and vessels.
  2. Move through the circulation (the blood or the lymph).
  3. Leave the vessels.
  4. Establish colonies at distant sites.

Each step is complex and poorly understood at the molecular level, but the last step, colonization, is rate limiting (the slowest, hardest step that sets the pace of the whole process).

Step 1: invasion needs loss of adhesion

To become malignant a cell must break free of the constraints that keep normal cells in place. Malignant tumors therefore show a disorganised pattern of growth with ragged borders and extensions into the surrounding tissue (page 2.2, Figure 2-7). The molecular changes are not well understood, but invasiveness almost certainly needs a disruption of the adhesive mechanisms (adhesion = sticking; the proteins that tether a cell to its neighbours and to the extracellular matrix).

Step 2: entering the circulation, blood or lymph

The invasive cells must penetrate the wall of a blood or lymphatic vessel. Lymphatic vessels are larger and have flimsier walls than blood vessels, so cancer cells can enter them in small clumps; such clumps often get trapped in lymph nodes (small organs along the lymph vessels that filter the lymph, lymfeklieren), giving lymph-node metastases. Cells that enter blood vessels do so singly or, more rarely, in small clusters. With modern cell-sorting techniques these circulating tumor cells (CTCs) can be detected in a blood sample, although they are a minute fraction of all blood cells. In a mouse model, clusters of CTCs gave rise to metastases at a much higher rate than single CTCs, presumably because cells sticking to each other override death signals and suppress apoptosis.

Steps 3 and 4: the experiment with labelled cells

Which later step is hardest? Label cancer cells with a fluorescent dye or with GFP (green fluorescent protein, a protein from a jellyfish that glows green; BIO01), inject them into the bloodstream of a mouse and follow them. Observed: many cells survive in the circulation, lodge in small vessels and exit into the tissue, whether or not they come from a tumor that metastasizes. Then: some die at once in the foreign tissue; others survive but fail to proliferate; others divide a few times and stop, forming micrometastases of ten to several thousand cells; very few establish full-blown metastases. The syllabus numbers: fewer than 1 in 1000 cells put into the blood is alive after 24 hours, and less than 0.1 % of those survivors colonize a new tissue; overall fewer than one in thousands, perhaps one in millions. The syllabus compares them with the Vikings who landed in Greenland: most fail to survive in the alien environment, or found a small colony that dies out.

Dormant micrometastases

Many cancers are found before they have founded metastatic colonies and can be cured by destroying the primary tumor. But sometimes an undetected distant micrometastasis stays dormant (sleeping) for many years and then erupts into growth to form a large secondary tumor long after the primary tumor was removed.

3. Summary of the first half of the chapter (2.1.8)

The syllabus summary, in simple words. Cancer cells, by definition, grow and proliferate in defiance of normal controls (neoplastic) and gain the ability to invade surrounding tissue and colonize distant organs (malignant). Because they make secondary tumors, metastases, they are hard to eradicate by surgery or local irradiation. Cancers start from a single cell with one initial mutation, but its progeny must change many more times. Tumor progression takes years and is a Darwinian process (named after Charles Darwin, who described natural selection): mutation and epigenetic change, natural selection, and occasional bursts of genomic chaos that create heterogeneity.

As they evolve, cancer cells collect special properties: they grow and divide despite the signals that normally control proliferation; they lose the controls that permanently stop division or induce apoptosis after stress or DNA damage; they lose the mechanisms that keep cells in their proper place. All of this helps them survive and grow at home and then metastasize. The evolution of a tumor also depends on the stromal cells it attracts and manipulates. Because so many changes are needed, most cancer cells are genetically and/or epigenetically unstable; the instability is selected for because it accelerates the further changes.

Ze verliezen de remmen (stop, apoptose, plaats) en manipuleren het stroma. Instabiliteit wordt geselecteerd omdat ze de verdere veranderingen versnelt.

6. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

Which cells make up the tumor stroma, and which of them carry mutations?
Fibroblasts, inflammatory white blood cells, and the endothelial cells of blood and lymphatic vessels with their pericytes, in extracellular matrix. Only the cancer cells carry the mutations; the stromal cells are genetically normal but behave differently.
How do cancer cells change the stroma, and how does the stroma answer?
Cancer cells secrete signal proteins that change stromal-cell behaviour and proteases that remodel the matrix; stromal cells secrete signal proteins that stimulate cancer-cell growth and division and more proteases. They evolve together, and the tumor can become dependent on its stroma.
Why are tumors compared to unhealed wounds?
They invoke the same inflammatory reaction as damaged tissue: leakier blood vessels, deposition of extracellular matrix, and new blood vessels. This gives the tumor the stroma it needs.
What is angiogenesis and why does a growing tumor stimulate it?
The formation of new blood vessels. As the tumor grows larger its interior becomes hypoxic; new vessels supply oxygen and nutrients.
What is the second way the tumor manipulates the immune system?
It creates an immunosuppressive microenvironment by blocking the activation of the white blood cells that could destroy it. Therapies that override this suppression are powerful.
What fraction of cancer deaths is due to metastasis, and why?
About 90 %. A cancer that has spread through the body is almost impossible to eradicate by surgery or local irradiation.
List the steps of metastasis. Which is rate limiting?
Invade local tissue and vessels; move through the circulation; leave the vessels; establish colonies at distant sites. Colonization, the last step, is rate limiting.
Why do lymph-node metastases arise so often?
Lymphatic vessels are larger and have flimsier walls than blood vessels, so cancer cells enter them in small clumps, and the clumps get trapped in the lymph nodes.
What are circulating tumor cells, and what was found about clusters?
Cancer cells travelling in the blood, detectable by cell sorting although they are a minute fraction of blood cells. In a mouse model, clusters of CTCs metastasized far more often than single cells, probably because cell-cell adhesion suppresses apoptosis.
Describe the GFP experiment and its outcome.
Cancer cells labelled with GFP or a dye are injected into a mouse's bloodstream and followed. Many survive, lodge in small vessels and exit; then most die or fail to proliferate; some form micrometastases of ten to thousands of cells; very few form full metastases: fewer than 1 in 1000 survive 24 h, and less than 0.1 % of those colonize.
What is a dormant micrometastasis?
A tiny, undetected colony at a distant site that stays inactive for many years and then suddenly grows into a large secondary tumor, long after the primary tumor was removed.
Why does the summary call tumor progression a Darwinian process?
Because it works by mutation and epigenetic change (variation), inheritance by the clone, and natural selection, with occasional bursts of genomic chaos adding heterogeneity.

7. To memorize

Learn these by heart
  • Stroma = fibroblasts + inflammatory white blood cells + endothelial cells (blood and lymph vessels) + pericytes + matrix; only cancer cells are mutated.
  • Two-way talk: cancer cells → signal proteins + proteases; stroma → growth signals + proteases; "weeds and ecosystem"; tumor becomes dependent.
  • Immune trick 1: tumor = unhealed wound: inflammation, leaky vessels, matrix, angiogenesis against hypoxia.
  • Immune trick 2: immunosuppressive microenvironment; overriding it = powerful therapy.
  • Angiogenesis supplies blood (oxygen, nutrients) to the tumor.
  • Metastasis = 90 % of cancer deaths; steps: invade → enter vessel → travel → exit → colonize (rate limiting).
  • Invasion needs loss of adhesion.
  • Lymph vessels: flimsy walls, clumps, lymph-node metastases; blood: single cells = CTCs; CTC clusters do better.
  • GFP mouse: < 1 in 1000 alive after 24 h; < 0.1 % of those colonize; micrometastases of 10 to thousands of cells.
  • A dormant micrometastasis can wake up years after the primary tumor is gone.
  • Summary: neoplastic + malignant, single cell, Darwinian evolution, instability selected for.

8. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. Which cells in a tumor carry the cancer mutations?
    • the fibroblasts
    • the immune cells
    • only the cancer cells
    • all cells of the tumor
    Answer

    c. The stromal cells are genetically normal; the cancer cells change their behaviour with signals.

  2. What is a pericyte?
    Answer

    A vascular smooth muscle cell wrapped around a small blood vessel; part of the stroma together with the endothelial cells that form the vessel.

  3. Name two kinds of molecules cancer cells secrete to change their stroma.
    Answer

    Signal proteins (change the behaviour of stromal cells) and proteases (proteolytic enzymes that remodel the extracellular matrix).

  4. True or false: "A tumor is like a wound that never heals."
    Answer

    True. It invokes the inflammatory reaction of damaged tissue: leakier vessels, matrix deposition, new blood vessels; and that gives it the stroma it needs.

  5. Why does the interior of a large tumor need angiogenesis?
    Answer

    Because it becomes hypoxic (too little oxygen) as the tumor grows; new blood vessels bring oxygen and nutrients. That is what "angiogenesis supplies blood to the tumor" means.

  6. How does a tumor stop the immune cells in its stroma from killing it?
    Answer

    It builds an immunosuppressive microenvironment: it blocks the activation of the white blood cells that could destroy it.

  7. Put the steps of metastasis in order: leave the vessel, invade locally, colonize, travel through the circulation, enter a vessel.
    Answer

    Invade locally → enter a vessel → travel through the circulation → leave the vessel → colonize. The last step is rate limiting.

  8. Which step of metastasis is the hardest for a cancer cell?
    • entering the bloodstream
    • surviving in the circulation
    • leaving the vessel
    • colonizing the distant organ
    Answer

    d. Many cells manage the earlier steps; very few survive and proliferate in the foreign tissue. Colonization is rate limiting.

  9. Why do cancer cells enter lymphatic vessels in clumps but blood vessels singly?
    Answer

    Lymphatic vessels are larger with flimsier walls, so clumps can pass; those clumps get trapped in lymph nodes. Blood vessel walls only let single cells (rarely small clusters) through.

  10. What is a CTC and which CTCs form metastases most easily?
    Answer

    A circulating tumor cell in the blood. Clusters of CTCs metastasize much more often than single cells (mouse model), presumably because adhesion between the cells suppresses apoptosis.

  11. Give the two survival numbers from the syllabus.
    Answer

    Fewer than 1 in 1000 cancer cells put into the bloodstream is alive after 24 hours; less than 0.1 % of those survivors colonize a new tissue. Overall one in thousands to one in millions.

  12. What is a micrometastasis?
    Answer

    A small colony of ten to several thousand cells that stopped growing or dies out. One can stay dormant for years and later erupt into a large secondary tumor.

  13. Why was GFP used in the mouse experiment?
    Answer

    To label the injected cancer cells so they could be followed in the living mouse and their fate at each step of metastasis could be seen.

  14. Which molecular change is "almost certainly" needed for invasion?
    Answer

    Disruption of the adhesive mechanisms that keep a cell tethered to its neighbours and to the extracellular matrix.
