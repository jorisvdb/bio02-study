2.3 Genetic instability and the hallmarks of cancer

In one sentence: cancer cells mutate much faster than normal cells, sometimes in sudden catastrophes that shatter a whole chromosome, and the skills they collect this way can be summed up in a short list of "hallmarks" that almost every cancer shares.

Page 2.2 described tumor progression as slow, step-by-step evolution. This page adds two things: first, the steps are not always small, because cancer cells are genetically unstable; second, whatever route a cancer takes, it ends up with the same handful of abilities, the hallmarks of cancer (a hallmark = a distinguishing mark, kenmerk). The hallmarks are a named learning objective, and the example exam asks what angiogenesis does for a tumor.

1. Cancer can evolve abruptly: the Big Bang theory (2.1.4)

If cancer cells evolved only by the gradual accumulation of single mutations, the time from a premalignant lesion (a lesion = a damaged or abnormal patch of tissue, letsel; premalignant = not yet malignant) to metastatic cancer should be predictable for each cancer type. It is not. As in the evolution of species, cancer evolution can go through long periods with no visible change, punctuated (interrupted) by the sudden appearance of new phenotypes. The "Big Bang" theory of cancer evolution says that, besides gradual mutation and selection, periodic, cataclysmic genome disruption (a catastrophe that rearranges a large part of the genome at once) can produce rapid steps toward malignancy.

Genetic instability: two flavours

Unlike normal dividing cells, most human cancer cells accumulate genetic changes at an abnormally high rate: they are genetically unstable. This instability is itself a selective advantage: it speeds up tumor progression by supplying the many extra mutations a cancer needs. Its extent and its molecular cause differ from cancer to cancer:

FlavourWhat is seenLikely cause
karyotype normalthe karyotype (the set of chromosomes of a cell as seen at mitosis, sorted by size; karyotype) looks normal or nearly so, but there are many point mutations in individual genesfailure of the repair mechanisms that normally correct errors in DNA replication or maintenance (BIO01 ch 4)
karyotype disorderedmany chromosome breaks and rearrangements: deletions, duplications and amplifications (extra copies) of parts of the genome; extra copies of chromosomes; chromosomes made of pieces of different chromosomes (Figure 2-11)catastrophic events, likely defects in chromosome duplication or segregation during mitosis

2. Lagging chromosomes, micronuclei and chromothripsis (Figures 2-12 and 2-13)

Begin state: a cell in mitosis. Each copied chromosome consists of two sister chromatids, and correct segregation (verdeling) needs each sister to be attached to the opposite spindle pole (BIO01 ch 7: the spindle fibres grab the kinetochores). A common error in cancer cells is a failure to attach all chromosomes correctly. Figure 2-12 shows one mistake: one sister chromatid is attached to both poles, so in anaphase it is pulled in two directions, lags behind in the middle of the cell and becomes a lagging chromosome. Two outcomes:

  1. (A) Aneuploidy. The lagging chromosome ends up in the same daughter cell as its sister: one daughter has n + 1, the other n − 1 chromosomes (chapter 1).
  2. (B) A micronucleus. The lagging chromosome stays separated from the rest and, in the next interphase, gets its own small nuclear envelope: a micronucleus (a tiny extra nucleus with one chromosome in it). DNA replication in a micronucleus is often incomplete and DNA damage accumulates, but this does not delay the cell cycle. In the next mitosis, the isolated chromosome is prone to fragmentation while it condenses: chromothripsis (from Greek thripsis = shattering into pieces).

(C) Micronuclei can persist for several cell generations, going through rounds of fragmentation and reassembly, or be taken back into a daughter nucleus.

From an evolutionary point of view none of this is surprising: anything that raises the chance of random, heritable, not-too-harmful changes in gene function will speed up the evolution of a clone toward malignancy, so genome instability is selected for during tumor progression. It also feeds the heterogeneity of page 2.2: chromosome mis-segregation, aneuploidy and, more rarely, chromothripsis "shuffle the genetic cards", letting cancer cells try out many phenotypes and letting different clones coexist.

3. The hallmarks of cancer (2.1.5)

To become a cancer, a cell must collect a range of abnormal properties, "a collection of subversive new skills". Different cancers use different combinations, but all share some common hallmarks. The syllabus lists six key attributes of cancer cells:

  1. Altered homeostasis: cells grow and divide faster than they die.
  2. Bypass of the normal limits to cell proliferation.
  3. Evasion of cell-death signals (apoptosis).
  4. Altered cellular metabolism (metabolism = all the chemical reactions that make energy and building blocks; stofwisseling).
  5. Manipulation of the tissue environment to support survival and to evade a harmful immune response.
  6. Escape from the home tissue and proliferation in foreign sites: metastasis.

Hanahan and Weinberg 2000: six hallmarks (Figure 2-14)

Douglas Hanahan and Robert Weinberg, two cancer researchers, published "The Hallmarks of Cancer" in 2000 with six acquired capabilities:

HallmarkMeaning
self-sufficiency in growth signalsthe cancer cell makes its own "divide" signals or makes neighbours produce them; it no longer waits for the body
insensitivity to antigrowth signalsit ignores the "stop" signals that normally inhibit growth and division
evasion of apoptosisit survives in conditions that would normally trigger programmed death
limitless replicative potentialnormal cells can divide only a limited number of times; cancer cells maintain their telomeres (the protective chromosome ends that shorten at every division, BIO01 ch 4) or use another trick, and become an essentially immortal cell lineage
tissue invasion and metastasisinvading surrounding tissue and migrating to distant sites to form secondary tumors
sustained angiogenesisangiogenesis = the formation of new blood vessels (angio = vessel, genesis = birth); the tumor induces it to secure oxygen and nutrients for its growth

2011 and 2022: the list grows (Figure 2-15)

In 2011 the same authors added two provisional "emerging hallmarks": reprogramming cellular metabolism (first called "cellular energetics") and avoiding immune destruction. By 2022 both were considered part of the core set: eight hallmark capabilities. The 2011 paper also named two enabling characteristics (properties that do not harm by themselves but make it possible to acquire the hallmarks): genome instability and mutation (this page) and tumor-promoting inflammation (page 2.4). The angiogenesis hallmark was also widened: a tumor may become vascularized (get blood vessels) either by switching on angiogenesis or by taking over normal vessels, mainly by invasion and metastasis. The 2022 review proposed four more: unlocking phenotypic plasticity (cells changing their type), nonmutational epigenetic reprogramming (changing gene use without mutations), polymorphic microbiomes (the varied community of microbes living in and on the body) and senescent cells (cells in senescence that influence the tumor).

Exam link

Example exam question 4: "What role does angiogenesis play in supporting cancer cells?" Answer: it supplies blood to the tumor cells: new blood vessels bring oxygen and nutrients. It does not make growth factors, does not stop apoptosis and does not speed up DNA replication.

Story from Alberts (background, not exam material)

Why "altered metabolism"? Tumor cells take up glucose up to 100 times faster than normal cells and burn it mostly by glycolysis even when oxygen is plentiful, a wasteful way to make energy but a good way to make building blocks for new cells. This is the Warburg effect, noticed by Otto Warburg a century ago, and it is why a whole-body PET scan with radioactive glucose lights up tumors and metastases.

6. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

What does the "Big Bang" theory of cancer evolution say?
That cancer does not only evolve gradually: besides mutation and selection, periodic catastrophic disruptions of the genome can cause sudden, rapid steps toward malignancy, so the course of the disease is not predictable.
What does "genetically unstable" mean, and why is it an advantage for a cancer cell?
The cell accumulates genetic changes at an abnormally high rate. It speeds up tumor progression by providing the many extra mutations a cancer needs, so instability is selected for.
A cancer has a normal karyotype but many point mutations. What is the likely cause?
A failure of the repair mechanisms that normally correct errors during DNA replication or maintenance.
A cancer has a severely disordered karyotype with breaks, deletions, duplications and composite chromosomes. What is the likely cause?
Catastrophic events from defects in chromosome duplication or segregation during mitosis, for example chromosomes not correctly attached to the spindle.
What is a lagging chromosome and what are its two possible fates?
A chromatid attached to both spindle poles, pulled both ways and left behind in anaphase. It may end up with its sister (aneuploidy: n + 1 and n − 1) or stay separate and form a micronucleus.
What is chromothripsis?
The shattering of a chromosome (typically one isolated in a micronucleus) into many fragments that are reassembled in random order, with inversions, duplications and lost pieces: massive rearrangement in one event.
Name the six hallmarks of cancer of Hanahan and Weinberg (2000).
Self-sufficiency in growth signals, insensitivity to antigrowth signals, evasion of apoptosis, limitless replicative potential, tissue invasion and metastasis, sustained angiogenesis.
What is angiogenesis and why does a tumor need it?
The formation of new blood vessels. A growing tumor needs oxygen and nutrients delivered by blood; angiogenesis supplies them.
How do cancer cells achieve limitless replicative potential?
Normal cells can divide only a limited number of times because their telomeres shorten; cancer cells maintain their telomeres or use alternative mechanisms, making the lineage essentially immortal.
Which two hallmarks were added in 2011 and became core in 2022, and which two enabling characteristics?
Reprogramming cellular metabolism and avoiding immune destruction. Enabling characteristics: genome instability and mutation, and tumor-promoting inflammation.
What did the 2022 review add?
Unlocking phenotypic plasticity, nonmutational epigenetic reprogramming, polymorphic microbiomes and senescent cells.

7. To memorize

Learn these by heart
  • Big Bang theory: long quiet periods + sudden genome catastrophes (punctuated evolution).
  • Cancer cells are genetically unstable; instability is a selective advantage.
  • Two flavours: normal karyotype + point mutations (repair failure) or disordered karyotype (segregation defects).
  • Lagging chromosome (attached to both poles) → aneuploidy or micronucleus.
  • Micronucleus: incomplete replication, damage, no cycle delay → next mitosis chromothripsis = shattered, randomly reassembled, pieces lost.
  • Instability "shuffles the cards" → heterogeneity.
  • Hallmarks 2000: own growth signals · ignore antigrowth signals · evade apoptosis · limitless replication (telomeres) · invasion and metastasis · angiogenesis.
  • Angiogenesis = new blood vessels = oxygen and nutrients for the tumor (exam Q4).
  • 2011 emerging (core in 2022): reprogrammed metabolism, avoiding immune destruction → 8 hallmarks.
  • Enabling: genome instability and mutation; tumor-promoting inflammation.
  • 2022: phenotypic plasticity, nonmutational epigenetic reprogramming, polymorphic microbiomes, senescent cells.

8. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. What role does angiogenesis play in supporting cancer cells?
    • stimulates the production of growth factors
    • supplies blood to the tumor cells
    • inhibits programmed cell death
    • increases the rate of DNA replication
    Answer

    b. Angiogenesis = formation of new blood vessels; they bring oxygen and nutrients. (This is example exam question 4.)

  2. True or false: "Genetic instability is a harmful side effect that slows tumor growth."
    Answer

    False. It speeds up progression by supplying mutations, so it is selected for: a selective advantage for the clone.

  3. A chromatid is attached to both spindle poles. What is it called in anaphase, and what can happen to it?
    Answer

    A lagging chromosome. It may land in the same daughter as its sister (aneuploidy) or stay isolated and form a micronucleus, where it is prone to chromothripsis.

  4. Why does damage in a micronucleus accumulate?
    Answer

    DNA replication there is often incomplete and damage builds up, but this does not delay the cell cycle, so the cell divides anyway and the isolated chromosome shatters in the next mitosis.

  5. Which of these is chromothripsis?
    • a single point mutation
    • one chromosome shattered and reassembled in random order
    • a whole extra set of chromosomes
    • a reciprocal exchange between two chromosomes
    Answer

    b. Many fragments, random reassembly, with inversions, duplications and losses. (c is polyploidy, d is a translocation.)

  6. Which flavour of instability fits a tumor whose chromosomes look normal under the microscope?
    Answer

    A normal karyotype with many point mutations in individual genes, from a failure of DNA repair. The disorder is invisible at chromosome level.

  7. Name the hallmark: "the cancer cell keeps its telomeres and divides without limit".
    Answer

    Limitless replicative potential. Normal cells stop after a limited number of divisions because telomeres shorten; cancer cells become an immortal lineage.

  8. Name the hallmark: "the cell makes its own divide signals".
    Answer

    Self-sufficiency in growth signals. Its partner hallmark is insensitivity to antigrowth signals (ignoring "stop").

  9. Which two "emerging hallmarks" of 2011 are core hallmarks since 2022?
    Answer

    Reprogramming cellular metabolism (earlier "cellular energetics") and avoiding immune destruction. With the six of 2000 that makes eight.

  10. What is an "enabling characteristic", and name the two.
    Answer

    A property that helps cells acquire the hallmarks rather than being a hallmark itself: genome instability and mutation, and tumor-promoting inflammation.

  11. How was the angiogenesis hallmark widened in 2022?
    Answer

    A tumor can get its blood supply either by switching on angiogenesis or by co-opting (taking over) existing normal vessels, mainly through invasion and metastasis; the hallmark is now "induce or otherwise access vasculature".

  12. List the four proposals added in 2022.
    Answer

    Unlocking phenotypic plasticity, nonmutational epigenetic reprogramming, polymorphic microbiomes, senescent cells.

  13. Why does the syllabus say "none of this should be a surprise" about genome instability?
    Answer

    Because anything that raises the rate of random, heritable, not-too-harmful changes speeds up the clone's evolution toward malignancy, so natural selection inside the tumor favours it; it also feeds the heterogeneity of the tumor.
