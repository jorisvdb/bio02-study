2.2 From one cell to a tumor: clonal origin and tumor progression

In one sentence: almost every cancer starts from one single cell, and it takes many years and several extra mutations, each one selected because it helps the cell outgrow its neighbours, before that cell's descendants are a full-blown cancer.

This page gives two of the concepts in the learning objectives: the multiple hit theory of Knudson (several mutations are needed, one at a time) and intratumor heterogeneity (a tumor is not one kind of cell but a mix of competing subclones). It also explains two words of the exam's matching question: primary tumor and tumor progression. Keep page 2.1 at hand for benign, malignant and clone.

1. Most cancers come from a single abnormal cell (2.1.2)

Even after a cancer has spread, we can usually trace it back to one primary tumor: the original tumor, in the specific organ where the disease started (primair = eerste). The primary tumor is thought to arise by cell division from a single cell that experienced some heritable change. Later, more changes accumulated in some of its descendants, which let them outgrow, out-divide and often outlive their neighbours.

How big is a tumor when it is found? (Figure 2-5)

By the time a cancer is first detected, it has usually been developing for many years and already contains a billion (109) cancer cells or more. The syllabus figure plots the diameter of a typical breast tumor against the number of times the cell population has doubled, on a logarithmic scale (each step on the axis is 10 times more). The doubling time (the time the tumor needs to double its number of cells) of a typical breast tumor is about 100 days; aggressive tumors grow much faster.

The tumor is not only cancer cells: the stroma

A tumor usually also contains other cell types and extracellular matrix, together called the tumor stroma (stroma = the supporting framework of a tissue; extracellular matrix = the network of protein fibres and other molecules between cells, extracellulaire matrix). In a carcinoma the stroma contains fibroblasts (the cells of connective tissue that make matrix fibres), immune cells (white blood cells that defend the body) and vascular endothelial cells (the cells that form the inner wall of blood vessels; vascular = of the blood vessels). Page 2.4 returns to the stroma.

The proof that cancer is clonal: the Philadelphia chromosome

How do we know the cancer cells are all descendants of one cell? By looking at their chromosomes. In almost all patients with chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML; a cancer of white blood cells, chapter 1 page 1.3) the leukemic cells carry the Philadelphia chromosome: a shortened chromosome 22, made by a translocation (exchange of pieces) between the long arms of chromosomes 9 and 22 (Figure 2-6). When the DNA at the site of the translocation is cloned and sequenced:

  • the site of breakage and rejoining is identical in all the leukemic cells of one patient;
  • but it differs slightly between patients (by a few hundred or thousand base pairs).

That is exactly what you expect if, and only if, the cancer in each patient arose from a unique accident in a single cell: all its descendants inherited the same break point. Many other lines of evidence point the same way: most cancers originate from a single aberrant cell.

Naast kankercellen bevat de tumor stroma: fibroblasten, immuuncellen, endotheelcellen en matrix. 2. A long lag, and why: tumor progression (2.1.3)

For cancers with a known external cause, the disease shows up long after the exposure:

  • lung cancer rises steeply only after decades of heavy smoking;
  • leukemias after the atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki rose markedly only about 5 years after the explosions;
  • industrial workers exposed to chemical carcinogens (cancer-causing substances) develop their typical cancers 10, 20 or more years later.

During this long incubation period the prospective cancer cells go through a succession of changes. The same presumably holds for cancers without an obvious external cause. A cancer needs the gradual accumulation of mutations in a number of different genes in one cell. That explains tumor progression: an initial mild disorder of cell behaviour evolves gradually into a full-blown cancer.

Selection inside the tumor

At each stage some individual cell acquires one more mutation or epigenetic change (a heritable change in which genes are switched on, without a change in the DNA letters; BIO01 ch 6) that gives it a selective advantage over its neighbours. The environment inside a tumor is harsh: low oxygen, scarce nutrients, and the surrounding normal tissue acts as a barrier to growth. A cell that copes better with this thrives. And the more tumor cells there are, the higher the chance that one of them picks up a useful change: as the tumor grows, progression accelerates. The offspring of the best-adapted cells become the dominant clones.

What a pathologist sees: the cervix example (Figure 2-7)

A pathologist (a doctor who diagnoses disease from tissue samples) uses standard names for the stages seen in a slice of the uterine cervix (baarmoederhals), an epithelium made of several layers (stratified squamous epithelium: flat cells in layers):

StageWhat is seen
A normal epitheliumdividing cells are confined to the basal layer (the bottom layer on the basal lamina); the cells above are flattened and differentiated
B low-grade intraepithelial neoplasiadividing cells throughout the lower third of the epithelium; the surface cells still show differentiation. (Intraepithelial = inside the epithelium; neoplasia = abnormal new growth; low-grade = mild)
C high-grade intraepithelial neoplasiacells in all layers are proliferating and show defective differentiation
D invasive carcinomatrue malignancy: cells move through or destroy the basal lamina and invade the connective tissue beneath

Knudson's multiple hit theory (Figure 2-9) and clonal evolution (Figure 2-10)

Knudson's multiple hit hypothesis says that several genetic mutations ("hits") are necessary to turn a normal cell into a cancer cell; each hit contributes one step of the gradual transformation (the syllabus illustrates it with colon cancer: healthy colon cells → ... → malignant cells; page 2.8 names the genes in order). Why bigger is worse

Each clone's proliferation hastens the next step: a larger population of cells at risk means more chances of one more useful mutation. Tumor progression therefore involves a large element of chance and usually takes many years, which may be why most of us die of something else.

3. Subclones: a tumor becomes a mixed population

Just as in the evolution of plants and animals, a kind of speciation (the splitting of one lineage into several) happens inside a tumor: the original cancer cell lineage diversifies into many genetically different subclones (a subclone = the descendants of one cell within the clone, carrying an extra change). These subclones may:

  • coexist in the same tumor mass;
  • or migrate and colonize separate places that suit their individual quirks, where they settle, thrive and progress as independently evolving metastases.

As new mutations arise, one subclone may gain an advantage and predominate, only to be overtaken by others or outgrown by its own sub-subclones. This genetic diversity within one tumor is called intratumor heterogeneity (intra = within, heterogeneous = of mixed kinds). It is one of the chief reasons why cancers are hard to cure: a treatment that kills most subclones can leave a resistant one behind. It also makes early detection more important: a young tumor is less diverse.

Story from Alberts (background, not exam material)

Alberts adds that the incidence of most cancers rises steeply with age, which fits a process that needs several independent accidents in one cell line; if a single mutation were enough, the risk per year would be the same at every age. He also describes "cancer stem cells": in some tumors only a small fraction of cells can found a new tumor, while the rest divide a few times and die, which helps explain why a treatment that shrinks a tumor to almost nothing can still be followed by a relapse.

Samenvatting

In de tumor treedt een soort soortvorming op: de oorspronkelijke kloon splitst in genetisch verschillende subklonen, die naast elkaar leven of als aparte metastasen verder evolueren; de ene subkloon verdringt de andere. Deze intratumorale heterogeniteit maakt genezing moeilijk (een resistente subkloon overleeft de behandeling) en maakt vroege opsporing belangrijk.

6. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

What is a primary tumor?
The original tumor in the specific organ where the cancer started; metastases can be traced back to it. It arises by cell division from a single cell with a heritable change.
How many cells does a typical cancer contain when it is first detected, and how long has it been growing?
A billion (10⁹) cells or more, after many years of growth; a typical breast tumor doubles about every 100 days.
Why does the Philadelphia chromosome prove that CML is clonal?
The exact site of breakage and rejoining of the 9;22 translocation is identical in all leukemic cells of one patient but differs slightly from patient to patient. That only fits if each patient's cancer came from one unique accident in a single cell whose descendants all inherited the same break.
What is the tumor stroma?
The non-cancer part of a tumor: supporting connective tissue with fibroblasts, immune cells and vascular endothelial cells, plus extracellular matrix.
Why does lung cancer appear only decades after someone starts smoking?
Because a cancer needs the gradual accumulation of mutations in several different genes in one cell line; during this long incubation period the prospective cancer cells go through a succession of changes.
What is tumor progression?
The gradual evolution of an initial mild disorder of cell behaviour into a full-blown cancer, through successive mutations or epigenetic changes, each selected because it gives a cell an advantage over its neighbours.
What is Knudson's multiple hit theory?
Several independent mutations ("hits") are necessary to transform a normal cell into a cancer cell; each hit is one step in the gradual transformation.
Describe the environment inside a tumor and what it selects for.
Harsh: low oxygen, scarce nutrients, and surrounding normal tissue as a barrier to growth. It selects cells that thrive anyway; the best-adapted cells' offspring become the dominant clones.
Why does tumor progression accelerate as the tumor grows?
The larger the number of tumor cells, the higher the chance that at least one of them undergoes the next useful change.
What are the four stages seen in the cervix epithelium?
Normal epithelium (dividing cells only in the basal layer), low-grade intraepithelial neoplasia (dividing cells in the lower third), high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia (all layers proliferating, defective differentiation), invasive carcinoma (cells through the basal lamina).
What is intratumor heterogeneity and why does it matter?
The genetic diversity of subclones within one tumor, arising by a kind of speciation. It makes cancer cures difficult (some subclones resist a treatment) and makes early detection more important.

8. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. A tumor is just big enough to feel with the fingers. About how many cells does it contain?
    • 10⁶
    • 10⁸
    • 10⁹
    • 10¹²
    Answer

    c. About a billion cells, roughly 1 cm. 10⁸ is first visible on an x-ray; 10¹² is lethal.

  2. In two CML patients the Philadelphia break points differ by a few hundred base pairs, but within each patient every leukemic cell has the same break point. What does this tell you?
    Answer

    That each patient's leukemia came from one unique accident in a single cell: all the cancer cells are that cell's clonal descendants. Different patients had different accidents.

  3. True or false: "The leukemia rate in Hiroshima survivors rose immediately after the bomb."
    Answer

    False. It rose markedly only about 5 years later: the damaged cells first had to accumulate further changes (tumor progression).

  4. What is tumor progression?
    Answer

    The gradual evolution of a mild disorder of cell behaviour into a full-blown cancer, by successive mutations or epigenetic changes that each give one cell a selective advantage.

  5. Which statement matches Knudson's multiple hit theory?
    • one mutation is enough to make a cancer cell
    • several mutations, accumulated one after the other, are needed
    • cancer is caused by viruses only
    • mutations occur only in germ cells
    Answer

    b. Each "hit" is one step of the transformation from healthy to malignant cells.

  6. Name three things that make the inside of a tumor a harsh environment.
    Answer

    Low oxygen, scarce nutrients, and the natural barriers to growth presented by the surrounding normal tissue. A cell that copes better with these is selected.

  7. Why does a bigger tumor progress faster?
    Answer

    More cells means more chances that one of them undergoes the next advantageous mutation. Each clone's growth hastens the next step.

  8. In the cervix, at which stage are dividing cells found only in the lower third of the epithelium?
    Answer

    Low-grade intraepithelial neoplasia. Normal: only the basal layer. High-grade: all layers. Invasive carcinoma: through the basal lamina.

  9. What is the first step of metastasis in Figure 2-10?
    Answer

    Invasion through the basal lamina by the cells of the most advanced subclone.

  10. Which cell types are found in the tumor stroma?
    Answer

    Fibroblasts (connective tissue), immune cells (white blood cells) and vascular endothelial cells (vessel walls), in extracellular matrix.

  11. Explain the word "subclone" and the word "intratumor heterogeneity".
    Answer

    A subclone is the set of descendants of one cell within the tumor that picked up an extra change. Intratumor heterogeneity is the resulting genetic diversity among the cells of one tumor.

  12. Why does heterogeneity make cancer harder to cure?
    Answer

    A treatment that kills most subclones may leave a subclone with a different set of changes alive; it then regrows. Early detection helps because a young tumor is less diverse.

  13. A doubling time of 100 days: roughly how long do 30 doublings take?
    Answer

    About 3000 days, roughly 8 years. That is the silent phase before a breast tumor becomes visible: "many years".
