2.5 Cancer-critical genes: oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes

In one sentence: the genes that matter in cancer come in two classes, those that drive a cell toward cancer when they become too active (proto-oncogenes, which then become oncogenes) and those that do so when they are lost (tumor suppressor genes), and an oncogene needs only one damaged copy while a tumor suppressor usually needs both copies gone.

The first half of this chapter (pages 2.1 to 2.4) described cancer from the outside: a clone of cells that evolves, grows, invades and spreads. From this page on we go inside the cell and ask which genes are changed. This is the heart of the chapter for the exam: the learning objectives ask you to "explain how mutations in a proto-oncogene and in a tumor suppressor gene have different effects at the protein level" and to "select from a list of mutations those that result in an oncogene". Two questions of the example exam (5 and 6) and the extra question in syllabus section 2.4 test exactly this page.

1. Finding the genes that matter (syllabus 2.2 intro)

Cancer depends on heritable changes in somatic cells: changes a cell passes on to its daughters. To understand cancer at the molecular level we have to find which mutations and epigenetic changes are involved. Finding the cells is easy: natural selection favours them and they announce themselves as a tumor. Finding the genes is hard, for two reasons:

  • A typical cancer depends on a whole set of changes, and that set is never exactly the same in two patients (although tumor types share patterns).
  • Because cancer cells are genetically unstable (page 2.3), each cell also carries a large number of mutations that are accidental by-products. These are called passengers; the mutations that actually cause the disease are called drivers. Telling them apart is difficult.

Despite this, over the past 40 years many genes that are repeatedly altered in human cancers have been identified. The syllabus calls them cancer-critical genes: all genes whose alteration can contribute to the start or the evolution of cancer. There are about 300 of them (page 2.7).

2. Two classes: too much or too little (2.2.1)

Cancer-critical genes are grouped by the direction of the damage: does the cancer risk come from too much activity of the gene's product, or from too little?

ClassNormal geneDangerous changeResult
Proto-oncogenea normal gene that helps cells grow and divide ("proto" = before, the gene before it goes wrong)gain-of-function: the product becomes overactive or is overproducedthe mutant form is called an oncogene (onkos = tumor): it drives the cell toward cancer
Tumor suppressor genea normal gene that holds cell division back or protects the genome ("suppress" = hold down, onderdrukken)loss-of-function: the product is missing or brokena brake is gone; this contributes to cancer

Both kinds of mutation can push a cell toward cancer directly (the cell divides when it should not) or indirectly: by causing genetic or epigenetic instability, so that other changes that do stimulate growth arrive faster. The genes whose loss causes instability form a subclass of tumor suppressors called genome maintenance genes (genes that keep the genome in good repair, for example DNA repair genes from BIO01 chapter 4).

Flip sides of the same coin

From the cancer cell's point of view, an oncogene and a lost tumor suppressor can have the same effect. Example: "too much signal to divide" can come from an overactive growth-signal gene (oncogene) or from losing the gene that normally switches that signal off (tumor suppressor). Gas pedal pressed down, or brake cable cut: the car goes too fast either way (syllabus Figure 2-19).

Dominant versus recessive (Figure 2-18)

Here is the difference that the exam asks for, at the level of the protein:

  • An oncogene acts dominantly: mutation of a single copy of the proto-oncogene is enough. One overactive protein molecule type is already pressing the gas; the normal copy next to it cannot undo that.
  • A tumor suppressor acts recessively: usually (not always) both copies must be removed or inactivated before an effect is seen. As long as one good copy makes working brake protein, the brake still works. The second copy is often lost not by a new mutation but by losing the whole chromosome (page 2.6).
Exam link

Example exam question 6 is a matching question. Four definitions: "a mutant gene whose overactive form causes cancer" = oncogene; "a normal gene in which a gain-of-function mutation can drive a cell towards cancer" = proto-oncogene; "a tumor suppressor gene found mutated in about half of human cancers" = p53 (the gene for a protein that senses stress in the cell and then stops the cycle or triggers apoptosis; page 2.8); "a normal gene in which a loss-of-function mutation can contribute to cancer" = tumor suppressor gene. Learn the exact wording "gain-of-function" and "loss-of-function".

3. Three kinds of accident that make an oncogene (2.2.2, Figure 2-20)

Begin state: a normal proto-oncogene on a chromosome, making a normal amount of a normal protein. Goal of the question: which accidents turn it into an oncogene? There are two ways to end up "too strong": a protein that is hyperactive (each molecule works too hard), or a normal protein that is overproduced (too many molecules). Three kinds of accident give one or the other:

  1. A small change in the DNA sequence (a point mutation or a small deletion).
    • inside the protein-coding sequence → a hyperactive protein, made in normal amounts;
    • inside a regulatory region → a normal protein that is overproduced.
  2. Gene amplification: errors in DNA replication make extra copies of the gene, so the normal protein is greatly overproduced (amplify = make more of, versterken).
  3. A chromosomal rearrangement: the DNA breaks and is rejoined in a new way (deletions, inversions and translocations from chapter 1). Two possible outcomes:
    • the coding region is fused to another gene → a hyperactive fusion protein (a protein made of two pieces that do not belong together);
    • the gene lands next to the control regions of another gene that is actively transcribed → the normal protein is overproduced.

Example: the EGF receptor (Figure 2-21)

Some names first. EGF is epidermal growth factor: a small signal protein that tells cells to divide (epidermis = outer skin layer, where it was found; growth factor = BIO01 ch 7, a protein from other cells that says "divide"). The EGF receptor is a protein that sits in the cell membrane. Its extracellular domain (the part outside the cell) catches EGF; when that happens, the intracellular domain (the part inside) gets phosphate groups attached (phosphorylation, BIO01 ch 2: a phosphate group added as an on-switch) and sends the "divide" signal into the cell.

In the most common human brain tumor, glioblastoma, a deletion often removes part of the extracellular domain. The shortened receptor is active even without EGF: it is phosphorylated all the time and keeps sending "divide". The syllabus compares it to a faulty doorbell that rings even when nobody presses the button. This is accident type 1 (a deletion in the coding sequence → hyperactive protein).

Story from Alberts (background, not exam material)

The first oncogene was found in a chicken virus. Rous sarcoma virus causes connective-tissue tumors in chickens; its genome turned out to carry a passenger gene, v-Src, that is a mutated copy of a normal chicken gene, c-Src. The virus had picked the gene up from a host cell. Later, DNA from human tumor cells put into mouse cells revealed the human oncogene Ras (Ras is a switch protein that passes "grow and divide" signals from receptors into the cell), the same gene a rat tumor virus carries. That discovery, in the early 1980s, showed that cancers are caused by mutations in a limited number of genes. Another classic: in Burkitt's lymphoma a translocation puts the Myc gene (Myc is a protein in the nucleus that pushes the cell to grow and divide) next to the strong control regions of antibody genes in B cells, so Myc is massively overproduced (accident type 3).

4. Worked example: which mutations give an oncogene? (syllabus 2.4)

Exam link

Example exam question 5: "Which alteration could cause a cell to proliferate more than it should?" Options: altering a protein that promotes apoptosis (brake on survival lost), altering a protein that stops the cycle when something is defective (checkpoint brake lost), overexpressing a growth-factor receptor (gas pressed), activating a proto-oncogene into an oncogene (gas pressed). All of them can: the correct choice is "all options describe mechanisms that could lead to excessive cell proliferation". The first two are tumor-suppressor-style losses, the last two oncogene-style gains, and both sides of the coin give the same result.

6. What the quiz and exam expect from this section

Learning objectives: "the student can precisely explain how mutations in a proto-oncogene and in a tumor suppressor gene have different effects at the protein level" and "selects from a list of mutations those that result in an oncogene".

  1. Define proto-oncogene, oncogene and tumor suppressor gene with the words gain-of-function and loss-of-function (exam Q6).
  2. Explain dominant (one hit) versus recessive (two hits) at the protein level: one overactive protein presses the gas; one good copy of a brake still brakes.
  3. Say that both an oncogene and a lost tumor suppressor can give the same result, e.g. excess proliferation (exam Q5: all options).
  4. List the three kinds of accident that make an oncogene and say for each whether it gives a hyperactive protein or overproduction.
  5. Explain the EGF receptor example: deletion of the extracellular domain → active without EGF (faulty doorbell), glioblastoma.
  6. Decide for a list of mutations which can give an oncogene (silencer deletion, activating point mutation, translocation under a constitutive promoter) and which cannot (whole-gene deletion, enhancer deletion, splice-site loss, premature stop).
  7. Know what drivers, passengers, cancer-critical genes and genome maintenance genes are.

7. Questions and answers

Cover the answers and try each question first.

What is the difference between a driver and a passenger mutation?
A driver causes or advances the cancer and is found repeatedly in many patients. A passenger is an accidental mutation that happened in the same unstable cell but contributes nothing.
What is a proto-oncogene, and what is an oncogene?
A proto-oncogene is a normal gene that promotes cell growth or division. When a gain-of-function mutation makes its product overactive or overproduced, the mutant gene is called an oncogene.
What is a tumor suppressor gene?
A normal gene whose product holds division back or protects the genome; a loss-of-function mutation (losing it) contributes to cancer.
Why does an oncogene act dominantly?
Because the overactive protein made from the one mutated copy already drives the cell; the normal protein from the other copy cannot cancel that. One hit is enough.
Why does a tumor suppressor usually act recessively?
Because one good copy still makes working brake protein. Only when both copies are removed or inactivated is the brake gone. (Not always: for some tumor suppressors one damaged copy already has an effect.)
What are genome maintenance genes?
A subclass of tumor suppressor genes whose loss causes genetic or epigenetic instability, so that other mutations accumulate faster. They act indirectly.
In what sense are oncogenes and tumor suppressors "flip sides of the same coin"?
The same effect, e.g. too much signal to divide, can come from an overactive gas gene (oncogene) or from losing a brake gene (tumor suppressor).
Name the three types of accident that convert a proto-oncogene into an oncogene.
(1) A point mutation or small deletion: in the coding sequence → hyperactive protein; in a regulatory region → overproduction. (2) Gene amplification → extra copies → overproduction. (3) A chromosomal rearrangement → a hyperactive fusion protein, or a normal protein overproduced under the control region of another gene.
What goes wrong with the EGF receptor in glioblastoma?
A deletion removes part of its extracellular domain. The truncated receptor is phosphorylated and signals "divide" even when no EGF is bound: a faulty doorbell that rings without being pressed.
Does a deletion of the whole coding region of a proto-oncogene make an oncogene?
No. No protein is made, which is loss-of-function. An oncogene needs more activity or more product, not less.
Why does a translocation that places a gene under a constitutive promoter make an oncogene?
A constitutive promoter is always on, so the normal protein is made all the time at a high level: overproduction, accident type 3.
Why does deleting a silencer in the 3′ UTR count, but deleting an enhancer does not?
A silencer reduces expression; removing it gives more product (gain). An enhancer increases expression; removing it gives less product (loss).

8. To memorize

Learn these by heart
  • Driver = causes the cancer, seen repeatedly; passenger = accidental by-product. About 300 cancer-critical genes.
  • Proto-oncogene + gain-of-function → oncogene (overactive or overproduced).
  • Tumor suppressor gene + loss-of-function → brake gone. Subclass: genome maintenance genes (instability).
  • Oncogene = dominant, 1 hit. Tumor suppressor = recessive, 2 hits (usually).
  • Both sides of the coin can give the same effect (gas pressed = brake cut).
  • Three accidents: point mutation/small deletion, gene amplification, chromosome rearrangement.
  • Only two outcomes at the protein level: hyperactive protein or normal protein overproduced.
  • Rearrangement gives a fusion protein or puts the gene under a strong control region.
  • EGF receptor without its extracellular domain = active without EGF = faulty doorbell; glioblastoma.
  • Oncogene test: more activity or more product = yes; broken, truncated, missing = no.
  • Exam Q5: all listed alterations (lost apoptosis, lost checkpoint, overexpressed receptor, activated oncogene) can cause excess proliferation.

9. Mini-quiz

Answer each one in your head or on paper, then click to check. Written by Claude, not from the course quiz.

  1. A normal gene in which a gain-of-function mutation can drive a cell toward cancer is called a:
    • oncogene
    • proto-oncogene
    • tumor suppressor gene
    • passenger
    Answer

    b. The normal gene is the proto-oncogene; its mutant, overactive form is the oncogene. A tumor suppressor is dangerous when lost, not when overactive.

  2. How many copies of a proto-oncogene must mutate before the cell is affected? And of a tumor suppressor gene?
    Answer

    One copy of a proto-oncogene (dominant: the overactive protein wins). Usually both copies of a tumor suppressor (recessive: one good copy still makes working brake protein).

  3. True or false: "A mutation in a tumor suppressor gene makes its protein hyperactive."
    Answer

    False. Tumor suppressor mutations are loss-of-function: the protein is missing or broken. Hyperactive proteins are the oncogene pattern.

  4. Explain "flip sides of the same coin" with a car.
    Answer

    An oncogene is a gas pedal stuck down; a lost tumor suppressor is a cut brake cable. In both cases the car (the cell) goes too fast: the same effect from opposite kinds of damage.

  5. A point mutation in the regulatory region of a proto-oncogene results in:
    • a hyperactive protein in normal amounts
    • a normal protein in larger amounts
    • a fusion protein
    • no protein
    Answer

    b. The regulatory region decides how often the gene is read, not what the protein looks like. A change in the coding sequence would give a (hyperactive) protein with a different shape.

  6. What is gene amplification, and what does it do to the protein?
    Answer

    Errors in DNA replication make extra copies of the gene in one cell. Each copy is read, so the normal protein is greatly overproduced.

  7. Name the two possible outcomes of a chromosomal rearrangement that creates an oncogene.
    Answer

    (1) The coding region is fused to another gene → a hyperactive fusion protein. (2) The gene is placed next to the strong control regions of an actively transcribed gene → the normal protein is overproduced.

  8. Why is the truncated EGF receptor compared with a faulty doorbell?
    Answer

    Because it rings (sends the "divide" signal into the cell) even when nobody presses the button (no EGF bound). The deletion of its extracellular domain makes it active on its own. Common in glioblastoma.

  9. Which of these can result in an oncogene? (several)
    • deletion of the entire coding region
    • deletion of a silencer in the 3′ UTR
    • a premature stop codon
    • a translocation that puts the coding region under a constitutive promoter
    Answer

    b and d. Removing a silencer gives more product; a constitutive promoter makes the protein all the time. (a) gives no protein and (c) a truncated protein: both are loss-of-function. (Claude's reasoning, see section 4.)

  10. A point mutation changes one amino acid of the Ras protein so that it can no longer switch itself off. Oncogene or tumor suppressor pattern?
    Answer

    Oncogene: a hyperactive protein from a small change in the coding sequence (accident type 1). It acts dominantly: one mutated copy is enough.

  11. Why is deleting an enhancer of a proto-oncogene not a way to make an oncogene?
    Answer

    An enhancer boosts transcription; without it the gene is read less, so there is less product. An oncogene needs more product or more activity.

  12. What are genome maintenance genes, and are they oncogenes or tumor suppressors?
    Answer

    Genes that keep the genome in good repair (DNA repair, chromosome segregation). Their loss causes instability, so they are a subclass of tumor suppressor genes; they act indirectly, by speeding up other mutations.

  13. Exam-style: which single alteration could make a cell proliferate more than it should? (a) loss of a pro-apoptosis protein, (b) loss of a checkpoint protein, (c) overexpression of a growth-factor receptor, (d) activation of a proto-oncogene.
    Answer

    All four. (a) and (b) remove brakes (tumor-suppressor-style losses); (c) and (d) press the gas (oncogene-style gains). The exam's correct option is "all options describe mechanisms that could lead to excessive proliferation".
